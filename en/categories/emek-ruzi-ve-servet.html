An Organization Without an Action Plan

Image
Text

An organization without an action plan is like a ship that knows its destination but has no chart, no chosen route, and no assigned duties for its crew. It may reach port through luck, a skillful captain, or favorable weather. But lasting success needs more than fortunate circumstances: it requires careful planning and consistent action.

Over time, an organization without a plan also begins to lose its members. People are sustained not only by ideas, but by the hope of seeing those ideas put into practice. When hope fades, people leave; when people leave, the organization gradually weakens and eventually ceases to function.

Moral: A goal provides direction; a plan defines the path toward it. An organization may advance by chance without a plan, but lasting success requires deliberate and consistent action.

At the Fish Shop

Audio
Image
Text

After work, a man was walking toward the bus stop when he passed a fish shop. Remembering how much his wife and daughter loved fish, he went inside to surprise them with dinner.

He asked the seller for one kilogram, then changed the order to two. At that moment, an elderly woman in a worn coat approached timidly:

“Son, would you buy some fish for me too?”

Without hesitation, the man told the seller:

“Make it four kilograms—two for me and two for her.”

He then asked whether she had bread. When she said no, he hurried to the shop across the street, bought four loaves, and gave her a share.

“Is there anything else you need?”

The woman took an empty medicine bottle from her pocket. They were eye drops she had last been able to buy two months earlier; without them, her sight was worsening.

The man went to the pharmacy. The fish seller, who had watched everything, offered the woman a chair. Returning with the medicine, the man was heartened by the seller’s compassion as well.

He gave the woman the medicine and then all the money left in his pocket. Blessing him, she made her way home.

As the man headed toward the bus stop, he remembered that he had not paid for the fish. His pockets were empty. The seller simply smiled:

“Go on, brother. The fish is my gift.”

With no bus fare, the man walked home. He called his daughter, told her he had bought fish, and promised to arrive soon. As he walked through the rain, he thanked Allah for the chance to help; the tears on his cheeks mingled with the rainwater.

Moral: Do not wait for great means before doing good; kindness depends not on wealth, but on a generous heart. A person rich in spirit finds a way to help even while possessing little.

Baklava

Audio
Image
Text

When I was a child, our family had very little money. One day, my father brought home some baklava, and we enjoyed it with tea that evening.

The next day was a holiday. My mother packed one piece of baklava, a few olives, and some bread for me to take to school.

My classmates had many different foods. A boy I disliked saw my modest meal and laughed:

“Did you bring that baklava for the mice?”

When the whole class laughed, I ran home in tears and shouted at my mother:

“Why did you give me only one piece? Everyone else had so much food—you humiliated me!”

My mother answered sadly:

“My child, that was my own piece.”

Her words broke my heart. She had saved her only piece of baklava for me instead of eating it herself. I was too ashamed even to hug her. Ever since, I have asked for forgiveness whenever I remember how I behaved. I have never been able to eat baklava again.

Moral: Mocking another person’s poverty is among the gravest wrongs. Never forget that a mother may surrender her own share—even her final bite—for her child.

The Former Minister’s Cup

Audio
Image
Text

A former minister was invited to speak at a seminar. He stepped up to the lectern holding a plastic cup, but seemed distracted. Raising the cup, he took a deep breath and addressed the audience.

“Last year I spoke from this same lectern,” he said. “The difference is that I was a minister then. I flew business class. A limousine and escort cars met me at the airport and took me to a luxury hotel suite. The next morning, the same limousine brought me here. There was a special welcome, an expensive porcelain cup of coffee, and a reserved seat. Yesterday, I paid for my own flight.

“No one met me at the airport. I took a taxi to the hotel and found my own room. This morning, they checked my name on a list and let me in. I found a seat myself. When I asked for coffee, someone pointed to a machine in the hallway. That’s where I filled this plastic cup.”

The audience laughed. “Last year’s porcelain cup wasn’t for me,” he continued. “It was for my position.” The room broke into applause. When it quieted, he added:

“That taught me something. The praise, privileges, and special treatment that come with office belong to the position, not to the person holding it. Once you leave, the porcelain cup becomes a plastic one. That is what you are entitled to, like everyone else.”

Moral: The respect shown to you is often for your position, not for yourself. Therefore, do not brag about your position — your true value will be seen when you leave.

“Picture a gardener tending a garden. Can you see it?”

“Yes,” they replied.

The teacher says in a serious voice:

“Remember this: it would be wrong to say the garden grew entirely because of the gardener. But it would be just as wrong to say it grew entirely by itself.”

The Little Shop and the Supermarket

Audio
Image
Text

An elderly woman ran a small grocery store in our neighborhood. When a supermarket opened opposite, both shops began advertising prices outside. The supermarket put up a sign: “Butter: 70 dollars.” The woman’s sign read “Butter: 60 dollars.” The next day, the supermarket lowered its price to 50. She promptly advertised 40.

One of the residents of the neighborhood, concerned about this situation, turned to the woman:

“Auntie, you shouldn’t compete with them. They can afford these price cuts. You’ll go out of business.”

The woman replied without losing her composure:

“I don’t sell butter,” she replied calmly. “I just want people to be able to buy it cheaply.”

Moral: A person who lives by the question “Whom did I help?” rather than “What did I gain?” is different. The one who gives without expecting anything in return is actually the one who gains the most.

Henry Ford's Choice

Audio
Image
Text

It is said that Henry Ford once invited two young friends to dinner. Both hoped to work for his company, and both were talented graduates of the same university.

After the meal, Ford offered one a job and wished the other well. The disappointed young man gathered his courage and asked:

“Mr. Ford, we haven’t discussed cars or engineering all evening. We only talked about everyday things. Why did you offer my friend a job and turn me down?”

Ford replied:

“For two reasons. First, I watched you both during dinner. Your friend tasted his food before adding salt. You added salt before tasting it. I value people who examine something before deciding to change it.

“Second, your friend treated the waiters courteously. You ignored them and were polite only to me. Everyone deserves respect, whatever their position.”

Moral: Small actions reveal character, both in how we make decisions and in how we treat people with less power than ourselves.

Fresh Bread

Audio
Image
Text

The smell of hot, fresh bread spread throughout the street.

The old man approached the baker. He needed to buy hot bread for his grandson. The child had been asking for fresh bread for days.

People were standing in line to buy bread. He took his place in line.

There weren't many people, and his turn came quickly.

He took the change from his pocket. If he had had enough, he would have bought two loaves. But when he held out the coins, the baker waved them away and handed him a bag.

“Take these, uncle, and enjoy them.”

There were three loaves of bread in the package.

“But I only asked for one loaf.”

“Take all three. You can save some for later.”

The grandfather slowly approached the baker and whispered in his ear:

“I only have enough money for one,” he whispered.

The baker smiled:

“Please take them, uncle. It’s Friday, and every Friday we give away three fresh loaves. Enjoy them.”

“May Allah be pleased with you, my son.”

“Amen.”

He had just left the bakery when someone called out to the grandfather:

“Uncle, wait!”

The grandfather turned and looked:

“Yes, my son?”

“This is my mother’s zakat. We would be grateful if you accepted it.”

The young man put an envelope in the old man's pocket.

“May Allah accept her charity and bless her.”

As he passed the market, a woman called to him:

“Uncle, please wait a moment.”

The old man stopped and looked in the direction from which the voice came. A young woman handed him two full bags:

“We’re sharing food this Ramadan, uncle. Please accept these bags. We’d be so glad if you took them.”

“May Allah accept your kindness, my daughter.”

“Amen.”

The old man reached home with difficulty, exhausted.

“Welcome home, Father.”

“Where’s your mother?”

“In the kitchen. She’s making kaygana, the egg dish Orkhan loves.”

“Take these bags to her, please. My back is aching.”

“Grandpa, did you get fresh bread?” his grandson asked.

“Yes, plenty.”

“Hooray for Grandpa!”

“But Dad, this morning you didn’t even have money for tea.”

“Allah provided, my daughter.”

His wife entered the room:

“Where did all this come from?”

“It’s a gift for us,” he said.

The man told what had happened one by one.

When the call to prayer sounded, they broke their fast with kaygana and fresh bread. The bags also held butter and cheese, which they enjoyed with tea.

“Thank Allah for this meal. May He accept their kindness. The halva is delicious too.”

After eating, his grandson stood up.

“I’m going to my room to pray again.”

“Isn’t it early? You can pray before bed,” his grandfather said.

“I prayed at this time yesterday for warm bread and cheese, and look what came! Now I’ll ask Allah to send money for your teeth. It hurts you to chew.”

His mother smiled:

“I wish your father were here to see this.”

“He visits me in my dreams every night. He told me to pray.”

The old man suddenly remembered his jacket. He went to the hanger, put his hand in his pocket and took out the envelope. There was a large amount of money in the envelope.

His eyes filled with tears.

“What is it? Why are you crying?”

“The Lord has sent everything our grandson wished for. Look—there’s enough here for both of us to have our teeth fixed before the holiday. Praise be to Allah!”

Moral: A child’s sincere prayer and a few generous hearts can transform a family’s day. Where people are willing to share, no one need go hungry.

The Credit Ledger

Audio
Image
Text

A plainly dressed stranger entered a neighborhood shop. After looking around, he asked the shopkeeper:

“Brother, would you let me have some bread, tea, or eggs on credit?”

“There’s a man who has been taking bread on credit for three months,” the shopkeeper replied. “To be honest, I doubt he can pay. But he’s caring for three orphans. I can’t let those children go hungry. Allah is great; He will provide for us too. Take the bread you need. You don’t have to pay.” A smile spread across the stranger’s face. “I’ve found what I was looking for,” he thought.

“How much do people owe you altogether?” he asked, pointing to the credit ledger.

Surprised and a little irritated, the shopkeeper asked:

“Why do you want to know?”

The stranger answered calmly:

“Please don’t be upset, brother. I haven’t come to trouble you or waste your time. I want to buy that ledger. Would you add up all the debts and tell me the total?”

The shopkeeper looked from the stranger to the well-filled ledger. Seeing that the man was serious, he added up the debts and named the sum. The stranger took a bundle of banknotes from his jacket pocket and laid the money on the counter.

“May Allah be pleased with you, brother. You trust your neighbors and, for His sake, refuse to let orphans go hungry. You are a good man. I’ve paid everyone’s debts. Let this be my Ramadan gift to the neighborhood. Give me the ledger now. I’ll take it away and bury it, trusting that this record will speak for me on the Day of Judgment.” He took the book and left.

The shopkeeper counted the money and put it in the till, still stunned by what had happened. He looked out of the little window, but the stranger was gone. It was nearly time for iftar, the meal that breaks the daily fast, and everyone was hurrying home. Just then, a seven-year-old girl appeared in the doorway. She was the daughter of Suraiya, whose husband had died of tuberculosis.

“Uncle Ahmed,” she said shyly, “Mama asks for a loaf of bread and three eggs for iftar. We’ll pay you next month, when my uncle comes.” Her words brought the shopkeeper out of his daze. Out of habit, he reached beneath the counter for the ledger. Then he paused and smiled.

“Come, my child. Take the bread, the eggs, and some butter too. Tell your mother that uncle has already been here.”

Moral: Kindness given quietly has a special worth. Relieving someone’s debt and keeping an orphan from hunger reveal a truly generous heart.

Mercedes

Audio
Image
Text

Mercedes was not its founder’s surname. The car took its name from the daughter of a man who loved automobiles.

Her father believed her name would bring him good fortune.

And so it happened...

The car he named Mercedes went on to win race after race.

Thanks to these successes, countless Mercedes cars have been produced since 1902.

A father’s affection helped turn his daughter’s name into one recognized around the world.

Mrs. Mercedes, who had two unsuccessful marriages, lived in Vienna. In 1929, at the age of thirty-nine, she died of cancer and was buried in Vienna.

Moral: A name given with love, a work done with love can change the world. A father's love immortalized a name.

Rent for the Water

Image
Text

A man bought a well from a villager. But when he tried to draw water, the seller stopped him. “I sold you the well, not the water.” Unable to settle the dispute, they went before a judge.

“If the water is yours,” the judge told the seller, “you cannot store it in another man’s well. Take it away or pay him rent.”

The Scarf Seller

Audio
Image
Text

The city was bitterly cold that night. I picked my way over the icy pavement toward the bus, relieved to reach it without falling. A boy approached, offering scarves for sale. All I wanted was to get inside and warm up.

“Would you buy one, sister?”

“No, thank you.”

“Please take one. You’ll need it.”

“I haven’t any money,” I said, hoping he would leave me alone.

“Then take it as a gift.”

“No, I already have a scarf.” I hurried onto the bus, thinking only of myself.

Before sitting down, I put my hand into my coat pocket to take out my phone. My hand touched a package. I took it out and looked. I was very surprised to see a package with a scarf inside. So the child had slipped the package into my pocket without my noticing.

I hurried back outside. The boy was still offering scarves to passengers. He was twelve or thirteen, in a thin coat with no gloves. His nose was red, but his dark eyes shone, and he greeted me with an innocent smile. I touched his shoulder and held up the package. “What’s this?”

“A gift, sister. You might need it.”

“But how will you earn anything? You’re out here working in this cold.”

“It’s all right,” he said warmly.

I held out the money.

“No, please. It’s my gift.”

If he had asked where the money had come from when I claimed to have none, I would have wanted to disappear with shame. But he asked nothing. He seemed to trust everyone as naturally as he deserved to be trusted himself.

“Please take it,” I insisted. He accepted, then began searching his pocket for change.

“Keep the change.”

“It’s too much!” In his hurry to count the coins, he dropped some.

“Then let it be my gift to you,” I said, smiling.

His smile could have melted the ice. “Thank you, sister. May Allah be pleased with you.”

“And thank you,” I thought as I returned to my seat. “You have so little, yet such a generous heart.” Outside, the cold still gripped the city, where children like him worked to help their families and somehow kept their trust in people.

The bus pulled away, leaving the boy behind in the dark. I thought of everyone out in that cold with nowhere to go. My throat tightened, and I struggled to hold back tears.

Our bus continued on its way, winding through the snow-covered mountains.

Moral: Sometimes the most helpless person shows the greatest generosity. The pure heart of a child turns material poverty into wealth.

The Weight of Butter

Text

A baker regularly bought a certain amount of butter from a peasant. One day he noticed that the weight of the butter he received was decreasing day by day. He accused the peasant of cheating him and complained to the judge.

“I have no scales,” the farmer explained. “I hold the butter in one hand and the baker’s one-kilogram loaf in the other. I give him the same weight of butter as he gives me in bread.”

Moral: As you treat others, you often receive the same measure in return. The bread you give comes back to you as butter.

We Are Rich

Audio
Image
Text

Near the end of the fourth century, Emperor Nintoku of Japan climbed a high tower one day and surveyed his country. Seeing not even a wisp of smoke rising, he realized his people were in poverty and that no one could cook at home.

He suspended taxes for three years and ordered that people work for their own households. He even sent the palace workers home. By the end of that time, the people were prospering.

Three years later Nintoku climbed the tower again, saw smoke rising on every side, and was sure that hearths were burning across the land. Joyfully he said to the lady beside him:

“Now we are rich!”

But the empress pointed to the palace, neglected for three years — roof leaking, flowers withered — and asked:

“You call this wealth?”

Nintoku’s answer has been remembered by the Japanese for centuries:

“When our people are poor, we are poor. When they prosper, so do we.”

Moral: True wealth is not in the treasury, but in the people’s well-being. A leader’s greatness lies not in the post, but in the meaning given to that post.

Why Am I Poor?

Audio
Image
Text

Once a poor man met Buddha and asked him:

“Buddha, why am I poor?”

“Because you share none of your wealth.”

“But I have nothing to give.”

“You have five kinds of wealth that you could share.”

“What are they?”

The Buddha explained:

“You can offer a smile.

“You can look at someone with compassion.

“You can speak a kind word.

“You can wish someone happiness.

“And you can lend a helping hand.”

Moral: There is always something to give: a smile, a kind word, a compassionate look, a helping hand. A person is as rich as they share.
