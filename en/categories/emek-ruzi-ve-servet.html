An Organization Without an Action Plan

Audio
Image
Text

An organization without an action plan resembles a ship whose destination is known but whose nautical chart, route, and crew responsibilities have not been defined. Such a ship may occasionally reach port through good fortune, a capable captain, or favorable weather. That outcome, however, would be the product of circumstance rather than systematic management. Lasting success is achieved not by chance, but through thoughtful planning and disciplined execution.

Over time, an organization without a plan also begins to lose its members. People are sustained not only by ideas, but by the hope of seeing those ideas put into practice. When hope fades, people leave; when people leave, the organization gradually weakens and eventually ceases to function.

Moral: A goal provides direction; a plan defines the path toward it. An organization may advance by chance without a plan, but lasting success requires deliberate and consistent action.

At the Fish Shop

Audio
Image
Text

After work, a man was walking toward the bus stop when he passed a fish shop. Remembering how much his wife and daughter loved fish, he went inside to surprise them with dinner.

He asked the seller for one kilogram, then changed the order to two. At that moment, an elderly woman in a worn coat approached timidly:

“Son, would you buy some fish for me too?”

Without hesitation, the man told the seller:

“Make it four kilograms—two for me and two for her.”

He then asked whether she had bread. When she said no, he hurried to the shop across the street, bought four loaves, and gave her a share.

“Is there anything else you need?”

The woman took an empty medicine bottle from her pocket. They were eye drops she had last been able to buy two months earlier; without them, her sight was worsening.

The man went to the pharmacy. The fish seller, who had watched everything, offered the woman a chair. Returning with the medicine, the man was heartened by the seller’s compassion as well.

He gave the woman the medicine and then all the money left in his pocket. Blessing him, she made her way home.

As the man headed toward the bus stop, he remembered that he had not paid for the fish. His pockets were empty. The seller simply smiled:

“Go, brother. It is freely given.”

With no bus fare, the man walked home. He called his daughter, told her he had bought fish, and promised to arrive soon. As he walked through the rain, he thanked God for the chance to help; the tears on his cheeks mingled with the rainwater.

Moral: Do not wait for great means before doing good; kindness depends not on wealth, but on a generous heart. A person rich in spirit finds a way to help even while possessing little.

Baklava

Audio
Image
Text

When I was a child, our family had very little money. One day, my father brought home some baklava, and we enjoyed it with tea that evening.

The next day was a holiday. My mother packed one piece of baklava, a few olives, and some bread for me to take to school.

My classmates had many different foods. A boy I disliked saw my modest meal and laughed:

“Did you bring that baklava for the mice?”

When the whole class laughed, I ran home in tears and shouted at my mother:

“Why did you give me only one piece? Everyone else had so much food—you humiliated me!”

My mother answered sadly:

“My child, that was the only piece that belonged to me.”

Her words shattered me. She had saved her sole piece of baklava for me instead of eating it herself. I was too ashamed even to embrace her. Ever since, I have asked forgiveness whenever I remember my behavior; baklava became the one sweet I could never eat again.

Moral: Mocking another person’s poverty is among the gravest wrongs. Never forget that a mother may surrender her own share—even her final bite—for her child.

Everyone Has Work to Do

Audio
Image
Text

I am very lucky to have wise teachers. One of them was my literature teacher, the esteemed Baladadash. He would say that if someone asked you for something or assigned you a task, even if for a trivial reason, you should never leave it unanswered.

Everyone has work to do. A person bothers you not because he is idle, but because he needs you. Never ignore an appeal to you; do not keep that person waiting by staying silent. If you will not be able to help, say so openly. Leaving people without an answer destroys their trust in you.

Moral: It is a very painful feeling to live knowing you are regarded as unreliable and untrustworthy.

Former Minister at Seminar

Audio
Image
Text

A former minister was invited to a seminar. When it was his turn, he stood up on the podium with a plastic cup in his hand and began to speak. But it was clear that his mind was elsewhere. Suddenly, he raised the plastic cup, looked at it, and took a deep breath. He turned to the hall and said:

— Do you know what I think? Last year, I spoke on this very podium. The only difference was that I was a minister then. When I came here, I had a business class ticket, a limousine and escort cars were waiting for me at the airport. They took me to a hotel. I rested in a luxury suite, and the next day I came here in the same limousine. A special welcome was arranged. While I was waiting for my speech, they offered me coffee in an expensive porcelain cup. Then they seated me in a specially designated place. But today I am not speaking to you as a minister. I flew here yesterday with a ticket I bought with my own money.

No one met me at the airport. I took a taxi to the hotel, and no one accompanied me to my room. When I came here this morning, they checked my ID against the list and let me in. Then I sat down in the first place I found in the hall. I wanted coffee, so they showed me the coffee machine in the hallway. So I went out and filled this plastic cup that you see here.

The seminar participants had already started laughing. The former minister continued: “I think that last year’s coffee in an expensive cup was not for me, but for my position.” The hall erupted in applause. As soon as the applause died down, he said:

— I want to share with you this important lesson that I learned: all praise, all services rendered are only for your rank, role, or position. None of them belong to you. Instead of the expensive cup that you used while you were in office, when you leave office, you will be given a plastic cup — that is, what you deserve.

Moral: The respect shown to you is often for your position, not for yourself. Therefore, do not brag about your position — your true value will be seen when you leave.

Grocery store and supermarket

Audio
Image
Text

An elderly woman had a grocery store in our neighborhood. One day, a supermarket opened opposite her store. Both began to write and hang the prices of groceries on the sign in front of their shops. When the supermarket posted “Butter — 70 dollars,” the woman posted “We have butter — 60 dollars.” The next day, the supermarket posted “Butter — 50 dollars”; the aunt immediately updated her sign: “We have butter — 40 dollars.”

One of the residents of the neighborhood, concerned about this situation, turned to the woman:

— Aunt, it’s better if you don’t compete with this supermarket. They have wide means, and if the price drops they won’t suffer, but you will go bankrupt.

The woman replied without losing her composure:

— I don’t sell butter at all… Let people at least buy it cheaply.

Moral: A person who lives by the question “Whom did I help?” rather than “What did I gain?” is different. The one who gives without expecting anything in return is actually the one who gains the most.

Henry Ford's Choice

Audio
Image
Text

It is said that one day Henry Ford, the founder of the Ford Motor Company, invited two young friends who wanted to work for his company to dinner. Both young men graduated from the same university and were very talented and educated specialists.

The dinner was over, the party ended. As they left the restaurant, Ford told one of the young men that he had hired him, and wished the other good luck and said that he could not give him a job. The disappointed young man gathered all his courage and asked Ford:

— Mr. Ford, we have not talked about cars or engineering skills all evening, we have only touched on general topics. But you offered my friend a job, and deprived me of this opportunity. Why did you make such a decision?

Ford replied:

— Look, young man, there are two reasons for this. First: I have observed the behavior of both of you throughout the meal. I noticed that your friend added salt after tasting the food, while you added salt first and only tasted it afterwards. I value those people who decide to make changes only after experimenting and measuring.

— The second reason is this: your friend was very polite to the waiters, and you ignored the waiters and only showed politeness to me. And people should be treated the same, regardless of their position.

Moral: A person's character is shown in his small actions: in his impulsive decisions and in his attitude towards the weak.

Hot bread

Audio
Image
Text

The smell of hot, fresh bread spread throughout the street.

The old man approached the baker. He needed to buy hot bread for his grandson. The child had been asking for fresh bread for days.

People were standing in line to buy bread. He took his place in line.

There weren't many people, and his turn came quickly.

He took out some change from his pocket. If he had a lot of money, he would have bought two loaves of bread. He handed the money to the baker. But the baker didn't take money from the grandfather and gave him a package.

— Take it, uncle, and enjoy it.

There were three loaves of bread in the package.

— Son, I was going to buy one loaf.

— Take all three, uncle, and eat them later.

The grandfather slowly approached the baker and whispered in his ear:

— Son, I only have money to buy one loaf of bread.

The baker smiled:

— Uncle, this is our share. Today is Friday; we get three hot loaves of bread every Friday. Enjoy it.

— May God be pleased with you, my son.

— Amen!

He had just left the bakery when someone called out to the grandfather:

— Uncle, oh uncle!

The grandfather turned and looked:

— Here, my son.

— Uncle, this is my mother's zakat; I would be very happy if you accept it.

The young man put an envelope in the old man's pocket.

— May God accept it, my son. May God be pleased with your mother.

As they passed by the market, a woman called out to him:

— Uncle... Wait a minute, please wait.

The old man stopped and looked in the direction from which the voice came. A young woman handed him two full bags:

— Uncle, this Ramadan we are giving food to everyone who passes by, please accept it. What can I do, don't let us down, take these bags.

— May God accept it, my daughter.

— Amen, uncle!

The old man reached home with difficulty, exhausted.

— Welcome, father.

— Where is your mother, my daughter?

— In the kitchen. She said I will cook eggs, Orkhan loves kaygana (a local egg scramble) very much.

— Take these and give them to your mother, my back is broken.

— Grandpa, did you buy hot bread? — the grandson said.

— Yes, I bought a lot.

— Long live grandpa!

— Dad, you didn’t have money in the morning, not even to drink tea.

— God gave it to you, my daughter.

His wife entered the room:

— How much did you buy, man?

— These are your share, wife.

The man told what had happened one by one.

The adhan was called. They opened the iftar with kaygana (a local egg scramble). The hot bread was perfect. There was butter and cheese in the bag. It went very well with the tea.

— Yes, it was very good, thank God. May God accept it. The halva was also very tasty.

When his grandson got up from the table and said:

— I'm going to go to my room and pray a little more, — the grandfather was surprised:

— Do it before you go to bed, my son, it's still early.

— I did it at this time yesterday. I asked God for warm cheese and bread. Look, today it came. Now I'll go and pray that He will send you money to fix your teeth. Your teeth are rotten, and you chew hard with these teeth.

His mother smiled:

— I wish your late father could see these days.

— My father comes to me in my dreams every night anyway and sees these things. In fact, he told me to pray.

The old man suddenly remembered his jacket. He went to the hanger, put his hand in his pocket and took out the envelope. There was a large amount of money in the envelope.

His eyes filled with tears.

— What happened, man, why are your eyes wet?

— My Lord has sent everything that our grandson has in his heart, wife. Just look at this money — we can both get our teeth fixed before the holiday. Thank God!

Moral: A sincere child's prayer, the kindness of a few kind-hearted people can solve a family's problems. In a society that is able to give, no one goes hungry.

Loan book

Audio
Image
Text

A plain-clothed stranger enters a neighborhood shop. After looking at the seller and the counter for a while, he says:

— Brother, if I ask you for a loan of bread, tea, or eggs, will you give it to me?

— There is a man who has been carrying bread for free for three months. To be honest, I don't believe he will pay this fee. But what can I do, he takes care of three orphans, let alone leave their children hungry. God is great, he will provide for us too. I don't want the fee for the bread I gave you. Take it and go, you will be happy. — A smile spreads across the stranger's face and he says in his heart: "I think I found what I was looking for."

— How much debt do you have in that loan book?

The seller asks, somewhat surprised and somewhat annoyed:

— What are you doing?

The man who came continued the conversation calmly, somewhat reassuringly:

— Look, brother, I beg you, don't get angry. I'm not such a vain person that I'd come here and spoil your nerves, take up your time. I'll buy that notebook of yours. Please, count all the debts in it right now and tell me.

The seller looked at the man in front of him, and then at the overflowing notebook. Seeing that the man in front of him was stubborn, he didn't resist; he added up all the debts recorded in the notebook and told the man the amount received. The man who came took a wad of money from his jacket's breast pocket, put that amount in front of the seller and said:

— Brother, may God be pleased with you. If you trust this community so much, if you don't leave an orphan hungry out of consideration for God, then you are a good person. I have paid everyone's debts. Let this be my share of Ramadan to this community. Now give me that notebook. I will take him and bury him, and I believe that list will be my help on the Day of Judgment. — The man took the notebook and left the store.

When the shopkeeper counted the money in front of him and put it in the cash register, he still couldn't fully understand what was happening. It was as if he had been hit by frost. He looked out the small window of the shop. There was no stranger in sight. Everyone was hurrying home because it was almost time for iftar. At that moment, the seven-year-old daughter of Suraiya, whose husband had died of tuberculosis, appeared at the door and said shyly:

— Uncle Ahmed, my mother says to give me one loaf of bread and three eggs for iftar. We will give you the money when my uncle comes to us next month. — The shopkeeper seemed to wake up from a dream at the girl's words. As usual, she reached under the counter to take out the notebook. She paused for a moment, smiled, and said:

— Come, my daughter, take the eggs, the bread, and the butter. Tell your mother that that uncle came and went...

Moral: A kindness done silently is the highest kindness. Forgiving a person's debt and not leaving an orphan hungry is the true richness of the heart.

Mercedes

Audio
Image
Text

Mercedes was not the surname of its founder or the name of a large automobile company. It was the name of the daughter of a father who was passionate about cars.

The father believed that the name of his daughter would bring him success.

And so it happened...

The car he named "Mercedes" won consecutive victories in the races it participated in.

Thanks to these successes, countless Mercedes cars have been produced since 1902.

The father's love for his daughter turned the Mercedes name into the brand of one of the largest automobile manufacturers in the world.

Mrs. Mercedes, who had two unsuccessful marriages, lived in Vienna. In 1929, at the age of thirty-nine, she died of cancer and was buried in Vienna.

Moral: A name given with love, a work done with love can change the world. A father's love immortalized a name.

Scarf seller

Audio
Image
Text

The freezing cold of the night had enveloped the city. The ground was icy and slippery. My breath felt as if it would freeze. I was trying to walk very carefully so as not to fall. I approached the bus door, said “thank goodness,” and managed to get there safely. At that moment, a child approached me with the scarves he was trying to sell. I had no strength to pay him any attention; my only concern was to get on the bus as soon as possible.

The child: — Will you buy one, sister? — he said, holding out a scarf. To get rid of him, I said: — No, I don’t want one. — Take it, sister, take it, you’ll need it, — he said. To escape the situation, I said: — I don’t have any money. — Never mind, sister, you take it — I don’t want money; let it be from me, — he said. — That won’t do; I don’t want it, I already have a scarf, — I said, and threw myself onto the bus. At that moment I was thinking only of myself; I did not care about the child at all. I hurried toward my seat.

Before sitting down, I put my hand into my coat pocket to take out my phone. My hand touched a package. I took it out and looked. I was very surprised to see a package with a scarf inside. So the child had slipped the package into my pocket without my noticing.

I immediately threw myself out of the bus door onto the street. The child selling scarves was still in the same place, trying to sell the scarf in his hand to people boarding the bus. I approached this child who paid no mind to the cold — his nose bright red, his pitch-black eyes shining like stars in the night, his innocent smile showing his white teeth, about twelve or thirteen years old, wearing a thin coat, without gloves, trying to earn money in the night cold with bare hands. I put my hand on his shoulder and showed him the scarf: — What is this?

The child smiled and said: — It’s nothing, sister; you’ll need it — let it be from me.

— If you do that, how will you earn money? Aren’t you sorry for yourself in this cold? — I said.

— It’s all right, sister, — he said with loving eyes.

I held out the money.

— No, sister, I don’t want it; let it be from me, — he said again.

Yet if he had looked at me and said, “Sister, you said you had no money — where did you get this?”, I would have sunk into the ground from shame at that moment. But he was a straightforward, innocent, pure child, with an untainted heart, who considered everyone as pure as himself.

— Take this, — I said, looking firmly into his face. Seeing my determination, he took the money and with his other hand began to take change from his pocket to give me back.

— No, I don’t want it; keep the change, — I said.

— Sister, this is too much; that won’t do, — he said, and hurriedly tried to separate the money; some of it fell to the ground.

— It’s all right; may you be well — keep it from me, — I said with a smile.

Embarrassed, with that smile that melts the ice, the child said: — Thank you, sister; may God be pleased with you.

“Thank you too! Thank you too, child of my poor country — big-hearted and content!” I said in my heart, and went and sat down. Outside, ice-cold air still covered this child’s small, beloved city — a city in which there are still children who remain pure in their innocence, whose trust in people has not been damaged, who turn winter into spring with their laughter, and who try to earn something to support their families.

We set off. In the darkness of the night, that city and that boy selling scarves were left behind. With a bitterness that had settled into my heart, I thought of the souls cast out of their homes in this cold. Various emotions I could not name rose inside me; something knotting in my throat took my breath away, and tears kept looking for a chance to fall from my eyes.

Our bus continued on its way, winding through the snow-covered mountains.

Moral: Sometimes the most helpless person shows the greatest generosity. The pure heart of a child turns material poverty into wealth.

We Are Rich

Audio
Image
Text

Near the end of the fourth century, Emperor Nintoku of Japan climbed a high tower one day and surveyed his country. Seeing not even a wisp of smoke rising, he realized his people were in poverty and that no one could cook at home.

He at once issued a decree ordering the people to work only for themselves for three years. He also sent the palace workers home. Working for themselves and paying no tax, the people came to abundance after three years.

Three years later Nintoku climbed the tower again, saw smoke rising on every side, and was sure that hearths were burning across the land. Joyfully he said to the lady beside him:

— Now we are rich!

But the empress pointed to the palace, neglected for three years — roof leaking, flowers withered — and asked:

— Do you call this wealth?

Nintoku’s answer has been remembered by the Japanese for centuries:

— Our people’s poverty is our poverty, and their wealth is our wealth.

Moral: True wealth is not in the treasury, but in the people’s well-being. A leader’s greatness lies not in the post, but in the meaning given to that post.

Why am I poor?

Audio
Image
Text

Once a poor man met Buddha and asked him:

— Buddha, why am I poor?

— Because you don’t give anything from your wealth to anyone.

— How can I do that? I have nothing to give to others!

— You have five riches that you could share with others, but you don’t.

— Please explain those five riches to me.

— Okay.

1. You could give others a smile with your face, but you don’t.

2. You could look at others with compassion and love with your eyes, but you don’t.

3. You could say good words to others with your tongue, but you don’t.

4. You could wish others happiness with your heart, but you don’t.

5. You could do good deeds for others with your hands, but you don’t.

Moral: There is always something to give: a smile, a kind word, a compassionate look, a helping hand. A person is as rich as they share.
