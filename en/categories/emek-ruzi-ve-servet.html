Without a plan

Audio
Image
Text

An organization without an action plan is like a ship with a known destination but no chart, no chosen route, and no assigned duties for its crew. It may reach port thanks to luck, a skillful captain, or favorable weather. But that would not be the result of systematic management. Lasting success needs more than fortunate circumstances: it requires careful planning and consistent action.

Over time, an organization without a plan also begins to lose its members. People are sustained not only by ideas, but by the hope of seeing those ideas put into practice. When hope fades, people leave; when people leave, the organization gradually weakens and eventually ceases to function.

Moral: A goal provides direction; a plan defines the path toward it. An organization may advance by chance without a plan, but lasting success requires deliberate and consistent action.

Bakhtiyar Sirajov

At the Fish Shop

Audio
Image
Text

On his way to the bus stop after work, a man paused outside a fish shop. Remembering how much his wife and daughter enjoyed fish, he went inside.

“A kilo, please… Actually, make it two.”

Just then, an elderly woman in a worn coat approached him hesitantly.

“Could you buy me a little fish too, please?”

Without hesitation, he turned to the fishmonger.

“Make that four kilos. Two for my family and two for her.”

Then he asked the woman:

“Do you have any bread at home?”

She shook her head. He went to the shop opposite, bought four loaves, and gave her two.

“Is there anything else you need?”

She took an empty bottle of eye drops from her pocket.

“I last bought these two months ago. I can’t afford another bottle. My sight hasn’t been as good lately…”

He took the bottle and went to the pharmacy. When he returned, the woman was sitting on a chair. The fishmonger had brought it out so she would not have to stand while she waited. The man smiled at the gesture.

He handed her the medicine, then put the money he had left into her palm.

She thanked him again and again, blessing him as she set off home.

The man picked up his bag of fish and headed towards the bus stop. Then he stopped short: he had forgotten to pay. He returned to the shop, embarrassed.

“I’m sorry—I forgot to settle up. I’ve given away all the money I had with me. I’ll bring it later.”

The fishmonger smiled.

“No need, my friend. Let me have a share in the kindness. The fish is on me.”

The man thanked him. With nothing left for the bus fare, he started walking home. He called his daughter.

“I’ve bought some fish. Tell Mom I’m on my way, but I’ll be a little late.”

Rain began to fall. As he walked, the day’s weariness seemed to lift. He felt grateful that he had been able to ease someone’s burden—and that another person had chosen to help too.

The tears on his cheeks mingled with the rain.

Moral: You do not have to be wealthy to show kindness. Whatever help you can offer may lighten someone’s burden and inspire another person to give. Sometimes compassion is what we pass from hand to hand.

Baklava

Audio
Image
Text

When I was a child, my family had very little money. One day, my father brought home some baklava. That evening, we gathered for tea. There was a small piece for each of us.

The next day, we were having a celebration at school. My mother packed a piece of bread, a few olives, and a slice of baklava in my bag.

My classmates had brought all sorts of food. One boy looked at my little portion and laughed.
“Did you bring that baklava for the mice?”

The whole class burst out laughing. I felt as though everyone was staring at my tiny slice. My face burned with shame. Unable to hold back my tears, I ran home.

As soon as I stepped through the door, I shouted at my mother:
“Why did you give me just one little piece? Everyone else had so much food! You embarrassed me!”

My mother looked at me and said softly:
“That was my piece, sweetheart. I saved it for you.”

I fell silent.

So she hadn’t eaten her share the night before. She had put it aside so I would have something sweet for the school celebration. And I had taken out on her the hurt that others had caused me.

I wanted to hug her and apologize. But I was too ashamed to lift my head or say a word.

Years have passed since that day. Whenever I remember it, I hear my mother’s gentle voice again, and silently ask her forgiveness.

I have never been able to eat baklava since.

Moral: Never mock someone for being poor. A small portion may hold a great deal of love. And do not take out the hurt others cause you on those who love you.

More than money

Audio
Image
Text

Cherish your parents while they are still here. Ask whether they have enough money, but do not let your care end with that question. Sometimes they may keep their needs to themselves because they do not want to burden you.

Help where you can, respect their wishes, and preserve their dignity. Remember that their needs are not only financial: hearing your voice, talking with you, and sharing a meal can mean a great deal to them too.

Moral: Show your parents love while you have the chance—through both practical help and the time you spend together. Sometimes the best thing to add to “Do you need anything?” is “I’ve come to spend some time with you.”

Everyone Has Work to Do

Audio
Image
Text

I have been fortunate to learn from wise teachers. One was my literature teacher, the esteemed Baladadash. He taught us: if someone asks you for something or assigns you a task and you cannot do it, never say, even if it is true, “I had so much work that I had no time and I could not get it done.” Those words make the other person think, “Don’t I have things of my own to do? Am I bothering you for nothing?”

Everyone has things to do. When someone asks for help, it is usually because they need you, not because they have nothing better to do. Do not ignore the request or leave them waiting in silence. If you cannot do it, say so at once and do not leave them in uncertainty, because they are counting on you and trust you. Coming back much later to say “I had no time” destroys that trust.

Moral: It is painful to live knowing that others regard you as unreliable and untrustworthy.

Bakhtiyar Sirajov

The plastic cup

Audio
Image
Text

A former minister invited to speak at a seminar stepped up to the lectern holding a plastic cup of coffee. After a few sentences, he paused, looked at the cup, and smiled.

“If you’ll allow me, I’d like to share something that’s been on my mind. I spoke here last year too. Back then, I was a minister.”

The room grew quiet.

“They booked me a business-class flight. A car was waiting at the airport. A comfortable hotel room had been arranged. The next morning, I was driven here, welcomed, and shown to my seat. Before my speech, someone brought me coffee in a beautiful porcelain cup.”

He raised the cup in his hand.

“This time, I bought my own ticket and took a taxi from the airport to the hotel. This morning, someone checked my name on the list and let me in. I found an empty seat. When I asked about coffee, they pointed me towards the machine in the corridor. So I went and filled this cup.”

A ripple of laughter passed through the audience.

“At first, it felt strange. Then I realized: last year, the porcelain cup wasn’t for me. It was for my position.”

He paused before continuing.

“I’m not telling you this because I feel slighted. I’ve still been invited to speak, and you’re here listening. I can simply see more clearly now the difference between the attention given to me and the privileges attached to my office.”

He set the cup on the lectern.

“When you hold a senior position, it’s easy to mistake the open doors, the praise, and the special treatment for proof of your own importance. Yet some of those things belong to the role. Tomorrow, someone else will occupy it, and the cup will be theirs. What stays with us is the kind of person we are and how we have treated others.”

Moral: Do not confuse the privileges of office with your worth as a person. A porcelain cup does not make you greater, nor a plastic one make you less. Lasting respect is earned through conduct, not titles.

The Garden and the Gardener

Audio
Image
Text

A teacher said to his students:

“Imagine a gardener tending a garden: loosening the soil, watering the trees, and pruning dead branches. The garden grows, blossoms, and bears fruit. Who makes it grow?”

“The gardener,” one student answered.

“The trees grow by themselves,” said another.

The teacher smiled.

“A gardener cannot grow in a tree’s place. But that does not make the gardener’s work unnecessary. It creates the conditions for growth. The tree has life within it; the gardener helps that life flourish.”

He paused, then added:

“People grow in much the same way. We should recognize both their own efforts and the support they receive along the way.”

Moral: Growth owes something to our own efforts and something to the care we receive. Value your hard work, and remember those who helped you flourish.

The butter price war

Audio
Image
Text

An elderly woman ran a small grocery shop in our neighborhood. One day, a large supermarket opened directly across the street.

Both shops displayed their prices on boards outside. As soon as the supermarket advertised “Butter — 70 dollars,” the woman put up her own sign:

“Butter here — 60 dollars.”

The next morning, the supermarket dropped its price to 50 dollars. The woman changed her sign:

“Butter — 40 dollars.”

Watching this price war unfold, a worried neighbor spoke to her.

“You shouldn’t try to compete with them. They have deep pockets and can afford to take a loss. You could lose everything.”

The woman smiled, perfectly untroubled.

“But I don’t even sell butter. I just want people to get it cheaper over there!”

Moral: Not every reward ends up in your pocket. Sometimes the satisfaction comes from helping others rather than gaining something for yourself.

Henry Ford’s Choice

Audio
Image
Text

A story told about Henry Ford describes how he once invited two young friends to dinner. Both hoped to work for his company. They had graduated from the same university and were equally talented and well qualified.

They talked about all sorts of things that evening. As they left the restaurant, Ford offered one of them a job. He wished the other well but said he would not be hiring him.

Disappointed, the young man summoned the courage to ask:

“Mr. Ford, we didn’t discuss cars or our engineering skills all evening. Why did you choose my friend?”

“There are two reasons,” Ford replied. “During dinner, I watched you both. Your friend tasted his food before adding salt. You added salt without tasting it. I want to work with people who understand what is in front of them before deciding to change it—people who observe rather than assume.”

He paused, then continued:

“The second reason is how you treated people. Your friend was courteous to the waiters too. You gave me your full attention but barely acknowledged them. To me, respect is not something reserved for people in important positions. We should value people for who they are, not for what they can do for us.”

Moral: Character reveals itself in small actions. Seek to understand before making a decision, and show respect regardless of someone’s position.

Busy but unproductive

Audio
Image
Text

A man bought ten eggs for ten coins and sold the lot for nine. His friends were puzzled.

“You lose money on every sale. What’s the point?”

“Never mind the loss!” he replied. “People see me buying and selling, so they think I’m a merchant. What matters is that everyone sees I’m busy!”

Moral: Looking busy is not the same as doing something useful. Judge work by what it achieves, not by the impression it creates.

Hot Bread

Audio
Image
Text

The smell of freshly baked bread filled the street.

An elderly man stopped outside the bakery. His grandson had been asking for warm bread for days. He checked the coins in his pocket and joined the queue. He had just enough for one loaf. If he could have afforded it, he would have bought two.

When his turn came, he held out his money. Instead of taking it, the baker handed him a bag.

“Here you are, sir. Enjoy.”

There were three loaves inside.

“But I only asked for one.”

“Take all three. You can share them at home.”

The old man leaned closer and lowered his voice.

“I only have enough money for one.”

The baker smiled.

“There’s nothing to pay. It’s Friday—we give away fresh bread every Friday. These three are for you.”

“May God bless you, my son.”

“Amen. Enjoy your bread!”

He had walked only a few steps when a young man called after him.

“Sir, just a moment!”

The old man turned.

“This is my mother’s zakat—her charitable offering. We’d be grateful if you would accept it.”

He slipped an envelope into the old man’s pocket.

“May God accept her gift and bless her,” the old man said.

As he passed the market, a woman stopped him. She was carrying two full bags of groceries.

“We’re giving out food for Ramadan. Please take these home with you.”

“May God bless your kindness, my daughter.”

By the time he reached home, he was breathless with the effort of carrying everything. His daughter opened the door and took the bags.

“Welcome home, Dad.”

“Where’s your mother?”

“In the kitchen, making eggs for iftar. You know how much Orkhan loves them.”

His grandson came running.

“Grandpa, did you get warm bread?”

“Yes, my boy. Three loaves!”

“Hooray for Grandpa!”

His daughter looked inside the bags in astonishment.

“Dad, you hardly had any money this morning. How did you buy all this?”

“God provided, my daughter.”

His wife came out of the kitchen, and he told them everything that had happened.

When the call to prayer sounded, the family gathered to break their fast. There were eggs and warm bread, and the bags held butter, cheese, halva, and other groceries. It had been a long time since their table had been so full or their hearts so much at ease.

After the meal, Orkhan stood up.

“I’m going to my room to pray.”

His grandfather smiled.

“You can pray before bed, my boy. It’s still early.”

“No, Grandpa. I prayed at this time yesterday. I asked God for warm bread and cheese, and today they came. Now I’m going to ask Him to send money to have your teeth fixed. It’s so hard for you to chew.”

A wistful smile crossed his mother’s face.

“If only your father were alive to see you now…”

“I see Dad in my dreams, Mom. In my dreams, he tells me to pray.”

Suddenly, the old man remembered the envelope. He went to his coat, hanging by the door, and took it out. Inside was far more money than he had expected.

His eyes filled with tears.

“What is it?” his wife asked anxiously.

He handed her the envelope.

“Look… There’s enough here for both of us to have our teeth fixed before Eid.”

Then he looked at his grandson and whispered:

“Thank You, God. So many kind hands brought joy into our home today.”

Moral: A child’s prayer and the kindness of strangers met at this family’s table. When we notice someone’s need and offer help, we may help make their hopes come true. The bread we share can bring both nourishment and joy to a home.

The Credit Ledger

Audio
Image
Text

One day during Ramadan, a stranger in plain clothes walked into a neighborhood shop and approached the shopkeeper.

“Brother, would you let me have bread, tea, and eggs on credit?”

“Of course,” the shopkeeper answered kindly. “There’s a woman who’s been taking bread on credit for three months. Honestly, I don’t know whether she’ll ever be able to repay me. But she’s raising three children without their father. We can’t let them go hungry. God will provide for us too. If you’re in need, take what you need. Don’t worry about paying for the bread. You’re welcome to it.”

The stranger smiled. “I think I’ve found the right place,” he thought.

“How much do the debts in your ledger come to?”

The shopkeeper looked surprised.

“Why do you ask?”

“I’d like to pay them all.”

The shopkeeper glanced from the stranger to the old ledger he had taken from beneath the counter.

“All of them?”

“Yes, brother. Please add them up.”

Seeing that the man was serious, the shopkeeper calculated the total. The stranger took money from his inside pocket, counted it, and laid it on the counter.

“May God bless you. You’ve stood by these people through difficult times. Let this be my Ramadan gift. Tell them their outstanding debts have been paid. They don’t need to know my name.”

Then he pointed to the ledger.

“Let me take that too. There’s no need for their names to become a subject of gossip or for these debts to trouble them again. It is enough that God knows our intentions.”

He took the ledger and quietly left.

Even after putting the money in the till, the shopkeeper could hardly believe what had happened. He looked through the little window. The stranger was gone. It was almost time to break the fast, and everyone was hurrying home.

Just then, Surayya’s seven-year-old daughter appeared in the doorway. Her father had died, leaving her mother to raise the children alone.

“Uncle Ahmed,” she said shyly, “Mama asks for a loaf of bread and three eggs for iftar. We’ll pay you next month, when my uncle comes.” Her words brought the shopkeeper out of his daze. Out of habit, he reached beneath the counter for the ledger. Then he paused and smiled.

“Come, my child. Take the bread, the eggs, and some butter too. Tell your mother that her brother came by.”

Moral: True generosity protects the dignity of the person receiving it. A burden quietly lifted from one person’s shoulders can inspire kindness in another.

Mercedes

Audio
Image
Text

“Mercedes” was neither a company founder’s surname nor the name of an existing car manufacturer. It was the name of a girl whose father was passionate about cars.

Austrian businessman Emil Jellinek first used his daughter Mercédès’s name as a pseudonym in motor racing. Later, a new car he commissioned from Daimler also came to bear her name.

Mercedes cars achieved remarkable success at Nice Race Week in 1901. The name was registered as a trademark in 1902. When Daimler and Benz merged in 1926, the name “Mercedes-Benz” was born. A girl’s name had become part of motoring history.

Yet while her name became famous, Mercédès Jellinek’s own life was far from easy. Her first marriage ended in divorce, and her second husband died just months after their wedding. She died of bone cancer in 1929, aged thirty-nine, and was buried in Vienna’s Central Cemetery.

Her life was brief, but her name lived on. Behind a name known around the world were a father and his beloved daughter.

Moral: Behind a world-famous name, there may be a deeply personal story of love. Remembering the people whose names became famous brands gives those names a human meaning.

Pay Rent for the Water

Audio
Image
Text

A man buys a well from a farmer. But when he tries to draw water, the seller stops him.

“I sold you the well, not the water in it!”

An argument follows. Unable to settle their dispute, they go before a judge.

After hearing both sides, the judge turns to the seller.

“If the water belongs to you, why are you keeping it in someone else’s well? Either take your water away or pay the owner rent!”

Moral: Someone who tries to cheat another out of what is rightfully theirs may be caught out by their own words. Fair-minded reasoning exposes the contradiction behind the trick.

The Scarf Seller

Audio
Image
Text

A bitter winter night had settled over the town. Ice covered the ground, and it was difficult even to keep my footing. It felt as though my breath might freeze. Taking careful steps, I finally reached the bus.

“Thank goodness I made it without falling,” I thought.

Just then, a boy carrying scarves approached me. I had no energy to listen to him. All I wanted was to get on the bus and warm up.

“Would you like one, miss?” he asked, holding out a scarf.

“No, thank you,” I said, hoping to end the conversation.

“Go on, miss. It might come in handy.”

“I haven’t got any money,” I said, hoping he would leave me alone.

“That’s all right. Take it. I don’t want any money. It’s a gift from me.”

“No, I couldn’t. I already have a scarf.”

With that, I hurried onto the bus. At that moment, I was thinking only of myself. I walked towards my seat without giving the boy another thought.

Before sitting down, I reached into my coat pocket for my phone. My hand touched a small packet. I took it out and stared: there was a scarf inside. The boy had slipped it into my pocket without my noticing.

I quickly got off the bus. He was still standing in the same spot, offering scarves to the passengers.

He could not have been more than twelve or thirteen. His coat was thin, his hands were bare, and his nose was red with cold. His dark eyes shone like stars in the night, and when he smiled, his teeth gleamed white.

I went over, rested a hand on his shoulder, and held up the scarf.

“Did you put this in my pocket?”

He smiled.

“Yes, miss. You might need it. It’s a present.”

“But how will you earn anything if you give your scarves away? Isn’t it hard standing out here in the cold?”

“I’ll be all right,” he said, looking at me kindly.

I held out some money.

“No, miss. I don’t want it. I gave you that scarf.”

Had he asked, “But didn’t you say you had no money? Where did this come from?” I would have wished the ground would swallow me. Yet he did not question me. He had believed what I said, thought I needed help, and shared what he had.

“Please take it,” I said, firmly this time.

Seeing that I meant it, he accepted the money. Then he reached into his pocket for change.

“Keep the change.”

“No, miss. This is too much. I can’t.”

He began hurriedly sorting through his coins. A few slipped from his fingers and fell to the ground.

“It’s all right. This is my gift to you,” I said with a smile.

A little shyly, he smiled back—a smile that seemed warm enough to melt the ice.

“Thank you, miss. God bless you.”

“Thank you, too, my young friend,” I thought. “You have so little, yet such a generous heart, and you ask for nothing in return.”

I got back on the bus and sat down. Outside, the cold was as fierce as ever. The boy was still standing there—a child who had not lost his trust in people, whose smile could turn winter into spring, and who was trying to earn something to help his family.

We pulled away. The town and the scarf seller disappeared into the darkness behind us. I thought of him, and of everyone who had nowhere to shelter on a night like this. A lump rose in my throat, and I struggled to hold back my tears.

The bus traveled on, following the winding road between mountains blanketed in snow.

Moral: Generosity is measured not by how much we own, but by our willingness to share. Sometimes the person we think needs our help is the one who reminds us what kindness means.

The Weight of the Butter

Audio
Image
Text

A baker regularly bought butter from a farmer. After a while, he noticed that the portions were getting lighter. He accused the farmer of cheating him and took his complaint to the judge.

The judge summoned the farmer and asked:

“How do you weigh the butter you sell?”

The farmer replied:

“I have no weights for my scales. Every day, I put the one-kilogram loaf I buy from this baker on one side and the butter I’m going to sell him on the other. I add butter until the scales balance.”

Moral: The measure you use for others may one day be used for you. The bread you give determines the butter you receive.

We Are Rich

Audio
Image
Text

According to legend, Emperor Nintoku of Japan once climbed a tall tower to look out over the surrounding land. When he saw no smoke rising from the houses, he understood that his people were living in poverty: they had nothing to cook.

He immediately decreed that, for three years, the people would pay no taxes and owe no labor to the imperial court. He even postponed repairs to the palace so that they could devote their efforts to supporting themselves.

Three years passed. The people’s circumstances improved, and their homes were well provided for again.

Nintoku climbed the tower once more. Seeing smoke rising from houses in every direction, he turned joyfully to his wife.

“Now we are rich!”

The empress pointed to their neglected palace, with its leaking roof.

“You call this wealth?”

Nintoku replied:

“If our people are poor, so are we. If our people are rich, so are we.”

Moral: True wealth is measured by the well-being of the people, not by the riches in the treasury. A leader’s greatness lies in what they do to improve people’s lives, not in the title they hold.

Giving without money

Audio
Image
Text

In a story told about the Buddha, a poor man approaches him and asks:

“Why am I poor?”

“Perhaps you do not recognize the riches you already possess or share them with others.”

“But I have nothing to give!”

“You have five treasures you can share.”

“What are they? Please explain.”

The Buddha replies:

“With your face, you can offer someone a smile.

“With your eyes, you can look at them with compassion and love.

“With your words, you can comfort and encourage them.

“In your heart, you can wish them happiness.

“With your hands, you can help them and do good.”

Moral: You do not have to be wealthy to be generous. A smile, a kind word, a compassionate glance and a helping hand are valuable gifts too. There is always some kindness you can share.
