Those Who Give Love Receive Love

Audio
Image
Text

An old story set in China tells of a young woman named Li-Li who married and moved into a home with her husband and his mother.

Before long, tensions arose between the two women. Their temperaments were very different. A remark that seemed harmless to one would hurt the other, and even small matters turned into arguments. The strain wore down both Li-Li and her husband, who felt caught between two people he loved.

One day, Li-Li visited an old friend of her father’s, a man who prepared herbal remedies. She poured out her troubles, then said bitterly:
“I can’t bear it anymore. I want to be rid of her for good.”

The old man listened and brought her a container of herbs. He told her it was a slow-acting poison. Then he added:
“But there is one condition: you must treat your mother-in-law kindly. Don’t interrupt her or argue with everything she says. Take care of her, so no one notices any hostility between you.”

Li-Li returned home and began adding the mixture to her mother-in-law’s food. She cooked her favorite meals, asked how she was feeling, and listened instead of starting arguments.

At first, she did this only to avoid suspicion. But gradually, she began to notice things about her mother-in-law that she had never seen before. Sometimes there was worry behind her sharp words, and concern behind her criticism.

Her mother-in-law was changing, too. She responded to Li-Li’s kindness with warmth and began treating her like her own daughter. Arguments gave way to quiet conversations, and resentment slowly softened into affection.

One day, Li-Li looked at her mother-in-law and realized she was afraid of losing her.

She hurried back to the old man.
“Please help me! Give me something to save her. I love her. I don’t want anything to happen to her!”

“Don’t be afraid, Li-Li,” he said gently. “The mixture I gave you wasn’t poison. You haven’t poisoned her.”

Li-Li stared at him in astonishment.

“What was poisoning your relationship was the resentment that had built up between you,” he continued. “By changing the way you treated her, you gave yourselves a chance to know and understand each other. At first, kindness was something you chose to practice. Then it became something you truly felt.”

Li-Li’s eyes filled with tears. She could hardly wait to go home and embrace her mother-in-law.

Moral: Resentment can keep us from seeing the good in another person. Listening, understanding, and showing care can open the way to change. Genuine closeness grows where both people treat each other with respect.

Before you marry

Audio
Image
Text

A young couple preparing to marry received this advice from their elders:

“Don’t compare your marriage with anyone else’s. Don’t say, ‘Look at her husband…’ or ‘Look at his wife…’ From the outside, you can never know what life is really like in someone else’s home. Build your own life together.

“Don’t expect perfection from each other. Everyone has flaws. Learn which differences you can accept with understanding and which problems you need to work on together. Don’t bring up past mistakes every time you argue.

“Don’t expect your partner to read your mind. Speak openly and calmly about your wishes, worries, and hurt feelings. Listen to understand, not just to reply.

“Sharing a life doesn’t mean losing your individuality. You will each have your own thoughts, interests, and need for time alone. Even hedgehogs must find a way to keep each other warm without hurting each other with their spines. Stay close, but give each other room to breathe.

“Neither of you should rule over the other or be expected to obey without question. Be companions who walk through life side by side. Make decisions together, share responsibilities, and respect each other’s right to an opinion.

“Don’t promise, ‘Everything will always be wonderful.’ Instead, say, ‘When things get difficult, you won’t have to face them alone.’

“Even when you’re upset with each other, remember to say goodnight. Not every disagreement can be resolved in one evening, but you can still show kindness while you work through it. Let a new day bring another chance to listen and make peace.”

Moral: A strong marriage is built by two people who know how to listen, understand, and speak with care—not by two perfect people. Sharing a home means supporting each other, not owning each other.

Keep your hearts close

Audio
Image
Text

Why do we raise our voices during an argument when the person beside us can already hear us?

One way to picture it is this: as hurt grows, so does the distance between our hearts. We raise our voices as though we could shout across that distance. Yet shouting often makes it harder, not easier, to hear one another.

Where there is love and trust, a quiet word can be enough. Sometimes a whisper, a glance, or a hand resting on another’s hand says more than a long speech. When we feel heard and understood, there is less need to raise our voices.

Closeness does not mean never disagreeing. It means treating each other with care even when we disagree.

So when anger rises, pause. Say what you need to say without belittling the other person. Explain your hurt without trying to hurt them in return. If you cannot speak calmly, take a break and return to the conversation later.

Let the argument end—not the bond between you.

Moral: Aim to understand each other, not to silence each other. Choose words that leave a way back to closeness once the anger has passed.

Early Marriage

Audio
Image
Text

I had just graduated from university and started teaching at a school. One day, I was in the teachers’ lounge marking exam papers. I was erasing and correcting some of the girls’ wrong answers so they could move up to the next class.

A man I did not recognize entered the room. After watching me for a moment, he asked:

“Why are you changing the girls’ answers?”

I put down my pen.

“If some of these girls fail, their fathers will take them out of school and marry them off at twelve or thirteen. If they pass, they may get a few more years of education. In that time, they could learn, gain confidence, and perhaps find a different path for their lives.”

The man was silent for a while. Then he said goodbye and left.

About half an hour later, the principal called me into his office.

“You spoke to someone in the teachers’ lounge. What did you tell him?”

I grew anxious.

“Did he complain about me?”

“No. He had come to inspect the school.”

My heart sank. The principal continued:

“He said there was a young teacher here who cared about the children’s futures. He asked me to pass on his thanks.”

I breathed a sigh of relief. Yet my unease remained. Changing a few answers would not be enough to protect those girls’ futures. Their right to stay in school should never depend on an exam mark—or a teacher secretly altering it.

Moral: A teacher’s care can change a child’s life. But no girl should have to rely on such a chance to protect her childhood and education. We all share a responsibility to oppose child marriage and support girls in learning and shaping their own futures.

Friendship and Love

Audio
Image
Text

Write your quarrels on leaves, so that autumn may carry them away and take the hurt with them.

Write your anger on clouds, so that it may fall with the rain and your heart may clear with the sky.

Write your hatred on snow, so that it may melt when the sun returns.

But write friendship and love in children’s hearts—through kind words, care, and the example you set. As the children grow, let that love grow with them and find its way into other hearts.

Moral: Nurture love rather than resentment. The kindness we plant in others can live on long after us.

I Kiss Your Eyes

Audio
Image
Text

Do you know why people say, “I kiss your eyes”?

Because the eyes bear so much of the pain of longing: they watch and wait, shed tears, and search for a loved one. Perhaps that is why they deserve the joy of reunion most of all.

Moral: Our eyes hold the longing we cannot put into words. To kiss a loved one’s eyes is to offer tenderness to the ache of missing someone.

I Love You

Audio
Image
Text

The story goes that a nightingale fell in love with a white rose. Every night, he perched on her branch and sang of his love. But the rose remained indifferent, offering no answer to his songs.

One day, the nightingale gathered his courage and opened his heart once more.

“I love you with all my heart.”

The rose answered sharply:

“Leave me alone. I don’t love you.”

However much he pleaded, her answer did not change.

Overwhelmed by the pain of rejection, the nightingale saw no way forward and took his own life. According to the legend, his blood turned the white rose’s petals red.

Only after losing him did the rose understand the depth of his love. But it was too late. The song she had heard every night had fallen silent forever.

And so, the tale says, the red rose came to mean “I love you.”

Moral: Unrequited love can hurt deeply, but it does not diminish a person’s worth. Love should never have to be proved through self-destruction. Being gentle with another person’s feelings matters, as does respecting their choices and valuing your own life.

Loving from Afar

Audio
Image
Text

Two friends meet and start talking.

“I think I’ve fallen for someone.”

“That’s wonderful! Does she know?”

“No.”

“Then why don’t you tell her?”

“I would, but I’m afraid of disturbing my peaceful life. I’d only be asking for trouble. We’d start going to the cinema and the theater, walking hand in hand. Then would come the arguments and the making up: ‘Why were you looking at her?’ ‘Why did you smile at that woman?’ If we’re going to break up in the end, why put ourselves through all that?”

He pauses, then adds:

“I’d rather just love her from afar.”

Moral: Sometimes we fear the end of a love that has not even begun. In trying to protect ourselves from a possible separation, we may also deny ourselves the chance of closeness and joy.

The Purple Jacket

Audio
Image
Text

A tale about the Yörük communities tells of a purple jacket placed among a bride’s belongings. This short traditional garment was kept at the very bottom of her dowry chest, with the hope that she would never need to wear it.

For the purple jacket was no ordinary piece of clothing. It was a call for help.

When a woman put it on and stood on a rooftop, in the village square, or somewhere everyone could see her, her message was:

“I am suffering in this marriage. I was married against my will, or my husband is mistreating me. I want to leave. Help me!”

In the tale, that call never went unanswered. The villagers would gather around the woman, defend her, and demand an explanation from her husband. The shame lay not in her asking for help, but in his having driven her to that point.

At the sight of the purple jacket, everyday life seemed to stop. People busy milking cows, spinning wool, or weaving rugs would set their work aside. Mothers and grandmothers would leave everything and hurry to her.

If a wedding was taking place, the music would fall silent and the celebrations would pause. One woman’s distress became the concern of the whole community.

A man who had abused his wife would be ashamed to appear in public. Even at the teahouse, no one would meet his gaze. He could not stand in the way of her wish to leave, and if he sought to marry again, few would trust him.

In this tale, the purple jacket is a promise that a woman will not be left alone: “We will hear you. We will stand beside you.”

Moral: There is no shame in asking for help when facing abuse. The shame lies in inflicting it or turning a blind eye. Solidarity begins with hearing a woman’s voice, protecting her safety, and respecting her decisions.

Text

Water a flower too much, and it rots. Too little, and it withers. Give it the water it needs, and it grows and blooms.

Moral: In love and in raising children, care should respond to the needs of the person receiving it. Neither smothering nor neglect helps them flourish; thoughtful care gives them room to grow.

Five more minutes

Audio
Image
Text

On a sunny day in the park, a woman pointed out a child on the swings to the man sitting beside her.

“The boy in the red shirt is my son.”

The man smiled.

“And the one in blue is my son, Jamil.”

A little later, he checked his watch and called out:

“Jamil, time to go home.”

“Five more minutes, Dad. Please!”

His father agreed. After a while, he called again. Jamil asked for another five minutes, and his father smiled and nodded.

“You’re a very patient father,” the woman said admiringly.

A sad smile crossed his face.

“It’s more than patience,” he said. “Last year, my older son, Kamal, was killed by a drunk driver on a road near here. I never spent enough time with him. I always thought we had plenty of time ahead of us. Now I’d give anything for just five more minutes with him. I’ve promised myself I won’t make the same mistake with Jamil.”

He fell silent for a moment and watched his son.

“He thinks he’s getting five more minutes to play. But those five minutes are a gift to me, too—a chance to watch him, hear him laugh, and be here with him.”

Moral: Make time for the people you love today. Five ordinary minutes together may be one of life’s most precious gifts.

An Eraser for Marriage

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a father asked his newly married son to bring him a sheet of white paper, a pencil and an eraser.

“What for, Dad?” his son asked.

“Bring them, and you’ll see.”

The son brought the paper and pencil but could not find an eraser.

“Go and buy one, then,” his father said.

Puzzled, the son did as he was asked and sat down beside him.

“Write something,” his father said.

“What should I write?”

“Whatever comes to mind.”

The son wrote a sentence.

“Now erase it.”

He rubbed it out.

“Write another sentence.”

“Dad, what are you trying to do?”

“Be patient. Write something else.”

The son wrote, and his father asked him to erase it again. After they had done this several times, his father asked:

“Is any of your writing still on the paper?”

“No, Dad. I’ve erased it all. But what does this mean?”

His father rested a hand on his shoulder.

“Son, you need an eraser like this in marriage too. You must learn to forgive your wife’s small mistakes instead of holding on to every hurt. She needs one too, so she can show the same understanding towards you. Otherwise, the pages of your married life will soon be filled with grievances.”

Then he added:

“If you want love and respect, you must both give them freely. When you make a mistake, remember to apologize, put it right and forgive each other. You must each have an eraser.”

Moral: Love and respect in marriage depend not only on forgiving mistakes, but also on recognizing and correcting your own. Mutual understanding keeps small hurts from growing into lasting resentment.

The Mother-of-Pearl Flower

Audio
Image
Text

A court was hearing the divorce case of a couple in their eighties. Everyone in the courtroom was saddened that two people who had shared fifty years of marriage now wanted to part.

The judge looked at the woman, whose eyes were sunken from crying, and gently asked:

“Tell me, why do you want a divorce?”

She took a deep breath.

“This man has worn me out. I’ve felt alone for fifty years…”

She fell silent. Only the click of a camera broke the stillness. The journalists following the case waited for her to continue.

“I have a mother-of-pearl flower that I cherish,” she said, wiping her eyes. “Fifty years ago, I grew it from a cutting taken from flowers he had brought me. We never had children. I cared for that plant as though it were my child.

“After a while, it began to wilt. My husband told me that if I got up at night to water it, it would recover. So I promised I would. For fifty years, I’ve got up every night to look after it. Not once did he say, ‘You sleep. I’ll take care of it tonight.’”

Her voice trembled.

“The other night, I was exhausted and didn’t wake up. He didn’t wake me either. In the morning, I found that the plant hadn’t been watered. It may seem a small thing to you. But to me, it felt as though nothing I had cherished in fifty years had ever mattered to him.”

The judge turned to her husband.

“Is there anything you would like to say?”

The old man rose, leaning on his walking stick.

“I worked as a gardener during my military service. That was when I met Fatima. I used to make bouquets for her from the loveliest flowers.”

He glanced at his wife, then continued:

“We were newly married when she began having neck pain. I took her to a doctor. As I remember it, he told her not to stay still for too long, but to get up and walk around occasionally. Fatima wouldn’t take care of herself, and I worried about her.

“Then the plant began to wilt. I told her it would recover if she got up at night to water it. I hoped that, if nothing else, the plant would persuade her to get up and move a little. She gave me her word.”

His voice faltered.

“Every night, I woke her and watched how tenderly she cared for it. She was protecting her plant, and I thought I was protecting her. Once she had gone back to bed, I would empty the excess water from the saucer beneath the pot so the roots wouldn’t rot.”

He paused.

“The other night, I didn’t wake up either. We’ve grown old… When I saw how hurt she was in the morning, I didn’t know what to say. I had kept silent for fifty years, and I stayed silent again.”

No one spoke. Even the journalists had tears in their eyes.

After so many years under the same roof, two people were hearing, for the first time, what lay behind each other’s silence.

Moral: Quiet care may come from love, yet remain unseen when it is never spoken of. Love means caring, but also listening, sharing our feelings, and trying to understand one another.

The Red Dress

Audio
Image
Text

The red dress hung in my mother’s wardrobe. Among the faded, worn clothes she had worn for years, it seemed to glow.

I had come to visit her. The moment I saw her, I knew she was nearing the end of her life.

Looking at the dress, I said:

“Mom, what a beautiful dress! I’ve never seen you wear it.”

“I never have, sweetheart,” she answered softly. “Come and sit beside me. There’s something I want to tell you before I go.”

I sat on the edge of her bed. She drew a deep breath.

“I don’t have much time left. I always thought I was setting you a good example. Now I realize that some of the lessons I taught you were wrong.”

“What do you mean, Mom?”

“Listen, darling. All my life, I believed a good woman should put herself last. Everyone else’s wishes had to come first. Mine were buried beneath theirs. I thought my turn would come one day. But that day never came. My life was spent making sacrifices for your father, your brothers and sisters, and you.”

“You did everything a mother could do for us.”

“I don’t regret loving you or caring for you. But I should have cared for myself too. I never learned to ask for anything for myself. Your father is sitting in the next room now, staring angrily at the walls. When the doctor told him I was dying, he came in and said, ‘You can’t die! Do you hear me? What am I supposed to do without you?’”

She paused to catch her breath, then continued:

“I worked for you seven days a week. I was always the first awake and the last to bed. I took the burnt toast and the smallest portions. Every penny I saved went on your clothes and books, sometimes even when you didn’t need them. I never bought myself something beautiful simply because I liked it.”

Her eyes turned towards the red dress.

“Last year, for once, I bought that dress for myself. I had a little money put aside to repair the washing machine. When I brought the dress home, your father said, ‘Where are you going to wear that? To the opera?’”

Her voice trembled.

“And I thought he was right. As though I needed a special reason to wear something beautiful. Apart from trying it on in the shop, I never wore it.”

She fell silent for a moment.

“I used to think that if I forgot myself in this world, I would find happiness in the next. I think differently now. I want to believe that God wants us to love the life He has given us and make room for our dreams while we are still alive. If a miracle happened and I could get out of this bed, I would live differently.”

Then she took my hand.

“Don’t forget yourself the way I did, darling. Promise me.”

I promised.

Those were her last words to me. A little later, she took her final breath.

Moral: Caring for the people you love does not mean giving up on yourself. Make room for their dreams, but keep your own alive too. Your life, your joy, and your needs matter as well.

True Love

Audio
Image
Text

Once upon a time, all the feelings lived on an island.

One day, word spread that the island was sinking. Everyone hurried to prepare their boats and leave.

Only Love lingered. As though trusting that a miracle would save the island, Love waited until the very last moment. But when water surrounded everything, there was no choice left but to ask for help.

Just then, Wealth passed by in a large boat.

“Wealth, will you take me with you?” Love called.

“I can’t. My boat is full of gold and silver. There’s no room for you.”

Love turned to Pride, who was sailing away in a beautiful sailboat.

“Pride, please help me!”

“I can’t take you aboard. You’re soaking wet. You’ll spoil my boat.”

Sorrow was passing nearby.

“Sorrow, may I come with you?”

“Oh, Love, I’m so sad… I need to be alone.”

Happiness was not far away either, but was so absorbed in joy that Love’s cries went unheard.

Suddenly, a voice called out:

“Come, Love. I’ll take you!”

Love was so relieved that asking the rescuer’s name never crossed their mind. Upon reaching the shore, the rescuer dropped Love off and continued on.

Only then did Love realize how much was owed to this stranger. Meeting a wise elder, Love told the story and asked:

“Who was it that saved me?”

“Time,” the elder replied.

“Time? Why was Time the one to help me?”

The elder smiled.

“Because only Time knows the true value of Love.”

Moral: The worth of true love reveals itself over time. Love is sustained not merely by beautiful words, but by years of care, faithfulness, and standing by each other through hardship.

What women endure

Audio
Image
Text

What women have endured in this world…

Even in fairy tales, they were cast as witches or cruel stepmothers.

What wasn’t said about them…

They were not allowed to be themselves. Yet they deserved to be seen as whole human beings, with dreams, thoughts and love of their own.

Woman…

“Long hair, little sense,” they said.

“Your place is in the kitchen. Stay out of matters like these,” they said.

Oh, woman, what you have endured in this world…

Too few recognized your worth.

Too few saw your love, your heart, your kindness.

When you stood up for your rights, you were difficult. When you stayed silent, you were naïve.

When you showed your love, some imagined that they owned you.

Those you lifted to the skies with your love sometimes forgot even to look down at you.

They did not ask, “How are you?”

They barely noticed whether you were there.

They forgot that you, too, had a soul.

They forgot you were human.

They hurt you, crushed you, treated you as though you did not exist…

Yet you wanted to make life more beautiful, to offer support, to change something for the better.

The heart that gave so much love was left longing to receive it.

They spoke to you of heaven and made your life a hell.

They looked into your eyes and denied the pain they saw there.

They did not hear the cry rising within you.

Most of all, you loved being a mother.

You loved looking into your child’s eyes with tenderness.

There were moments when the love in that embrace was the only love in the world you could truly trust.

Moral: Valuing women means more than praising their sacrifices. It means hearing their voices, recognizing their work and respecting their choices and dignity. Love and peace grow where everyone is valued equally.

What Is Loyalty?

Audio
Image
Text

It is remembering,

not turning away when times are hard,

not closing your door to a friend…

It is caring when a friend is crying,

feeling their pain and sharing their sorrow…

Loyalty is more than a word.

It means being there with a heart that understands.

A balm for the wounded,

water for the thirsty.

Loyalty is understanding—

not abandoning a rose because it has thorns.

Moral: Loyalty means standing by those you love through difficult times and not turning away because of their imperfections. Its worth is shown in the care you give, not merely in the promises you make.

Can a Broken Heart Love Again?

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a man asked a dervish:

“Can a broken heart love again?”

“Of course it can,” the dervish replied.

The man hesitated.

“But have you ever drunk water from a broken cup?”

The dervish smiled.

“Have you ever given up drinking water because a cup broke?”

Moral: A broken heart has not lost its capacity to love. Give yourself time to heal, but do not let one painful experience close your heart to love forever.

The Woman and the Mirror

Audio
Image
Text

One morning, a woman wakes up and looks in the mirror. She sees that she has only three hairs left on her head.

“Lovely! I’ll braid my hair today,” she thinks.

She does, and has a wonderful day.

The next morning, she looks in the mirror and sees only two hairs.

“Today I’ll part my hair down the middle.”

She does, and enjoys another lovely day.

On the third morning, she finds just one hair left.

“Never mind. A ponytail it is!”

She puts it in a ponytail and goes about her day in good spirits.

The following morning, she looks in the mirror and discovers that she has no hair at all.

“Wonderful!” she says cheerfully. “I don’t have to do my hair today!”

Moral: We cannot change every situation, but we can choose how we approach it. Sometimes a little humor and a fresh perspective can make a difficult day easier.

Yellow and Red Flowers

Audio
Image
Text

It is said that, in the past, flowers placed in a window sometimes carried a quiet message.

Yellow flowers meant, “Someone in this house is ill; please keep your voices down as you pass.” Red flowers meant, “A young woman lives here; please mind your language.”

Moral: A gentle gesture can sometimes say more than many words. Courtesy means not only expressing ourselves thoughtfully, but also noticing another person’s unspoken request.
