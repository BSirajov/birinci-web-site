A Meaningless Question

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a graduate of the University of California, Berkeley, visited a rabbi in New York and asked for help studying the Talmud.

“Do you know Aramaic? No? What about Hebrew? Did they at least read the Torah to you as a child?”

The young man answered no to every question.

“But I defended a dissertation on Socratic logic! I would like to study the Talmud to fill some gaps in my education.”

“The Talmud is one of the greatest works of human thought, but you are not yet ready to study it. Still, since you insist, I will give you a logic question. If you answer correctly, I will teach you. Two people climb down the same chimney. One emerges with a clean face and the other with a face covered in soot. Which one will go and wash?”

“You call that a logic question?” the Berkeley graduate asked smugly. “The person with the dirty face, of course!”

“Wrong. The person with the clean face will see the other’s sooty face, assume that his own face is dirty too, and go to wash. The person covered in soot will see a clean face in front of him and suppose that he has remained clean.”

“What subtle logic! Could you give me another chance?”

“All right. Two people climb down the same chimney. One has a dirty face and the other a clean one. Which will go and wash?”

“But we have just discussed that. The person with the clean face will go.”

“Wrong again. Both will wash. The person with the clean face will see his companion’s dirty face and think that he is dirty too. The other will notice that his companion looked at him and then went to wash, so he will realize that his own face must also be dirty.”

“I hadn’t thought of that. Could you give me one more question?”

“Very well. I will ask for the last time: two people climb down the same chimney. One has a clean face and the other a dirty one. Which will go and wash?”

“Now it is clear: both of them will go!”

“No. Neither will go. The person with the dirty face will see a clean face before him and assume that he is clean as well. The person with the clean face will notice that his companion is not going to wash and conclude that there is no need to do so.”

Growing desperate, the young man begged the rabbi for one more question. The rabbi began:

“Two people climb down the same chimney. One has a dirty face and the other a clean one…”

“Neither of them will wash!” the young man answered hastily.

“Wrong again,” the rabbi said calmly. “As a specialist in Socratic logic, think for yourself: how could two people climb down the same chimney together and only one get soot on his face? The question itself makes no sense. Spend your life answering meaningless questions, and neither your answers nor your life will gain meaning.”

Moral: An answer to a meaningless question cannot lead to truth. The right question is already half the right answer.

It means living with virtue, wisdom, a cultured manner, justice, honor, and dignity.

Education or Character?

Audio
Image
Text

A king asked his minister:

“Minister, which matters more: education or character?”

The minister answered without hesitation:

“Character, Your Majesty.”

The king had it proclaimed throughout the realm and ordered that the finest animal trainer be found and brought before him. When the man was brought before him, the king asked:

“How long would it take you to teach a cat to serve guests with a tray in its paw?”

“Six months, Your Majesty.”

Six months later, the man was summoned to the palace with the cat he had trained.

“Have you taught it?” the king asked.

“Yes, Your Majesty.”

The court gathered, and the cat began serving the guests. As it approached the minister, the king asked again:

“Minister, which matters more: education or character?”

Before answering, the minister took out the mouse he had kept ready in his pocket and released it. The cat immediately dropped the tray and chased it.

Then the minister turned to the king.

“Character, Your Majesty.”

Moral: Education does not change character; when the chance comes, true nature shows itself. Being knowledgeable is good, but character is more important.

Everything Is in Our Own Hands

Audio
Image
Text

(From Indian folklore)

A boy decided to confuse an old monk. He caught a bird, concealed it in his hands behind his back, and asked:

“Wise old man, is the bird I’m holding alive or dead?”

“If he says it’s alive,” the boy thought, “I’ll squeeze it to death. If he says it’s dead, I’ll open my hands and let it fly away. Either way, I’ll embarrass him.”

The monk thought for a moment, then answered:

“My son, the answer is in your hands.”

The boy stood still and lowered his head.

The monk continued:

“War and peace, punishment and forgiveness, good and evil—all lie in your hands. A person's path in life is determined not by his intellect, but by his intentions. Every person is capable of both good and evil. There is always a choice, and that choice is ours.”

Moral: Good and evil are a person’s own choice. Our fate is determined not by our intellect, but by our intentions.

How Can I Escape My Thoughts?

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a man came to visit one of the renowned scholars of his time and asked:

“How can I escape the painful thoughts that keep me prisoner?”

Instead of answering, the scholar stood, wrapped his arms around a nearby post, and began shouting with all his might:

“Separate me from this post — save my life from it!”

The man, looking at him in surprise, asked:

“Why are you doing that? I came to ask for help because I thought you were wise. But I see that you do not seem like a wise man. The post isn’t holding you—you’re holding it. Let go, and your life will be saved!”

The scholar let go of the post and said to the man:

“You’ve answered your own question. Your thoughts are not holding on to you; you are holding on to them. Let them go, so that your life may be freed as well.”

Moral: You yourself hold the thoughts that oppress you; it is in your hands to let them go. Change your perspective, and your burden will lighten.

Text

Reflections from someone who has weathered hard times:

I no longer try to make people love me. I know that anyone who dislikes me will always find an excuse.

I know my worth now. I do not have to prove myself to anyone.

I know I am happier with people around whom I can be myself.

Moral: True maturity is not about changing others, but about knowing who to let into your life and who to stay away from.

I Got Busy with Cleaning

Audio
Image
Text

Today I did some cleaning.

And throughout my whole body.

I cleaned all my muscles, my nerves, my veins, and even my blood.

I cleared out everything that had been gathering in the depths.

First, the disappointments. I let them go without even looking at what they were.

You should have seen

how much space they had taken up.

Into the space that opened, I carefully placed the feeling of forgiveness. In the place of every hurt, I planted a seed of forgiveness.

Then I swept out jealousy.

How jealous I had been.

Letting it go was easy, and I felt happy,

as though I had recovered something I had lost.

Thank Allah, I found no hatred or grudges in my heart.

I don’t know how I cleaned.

Next came my fears.

At first I was afraid even to take them out.

I had grown so used to living with them.

How had I become accustomed to so much pain and worry?

Each day, fresh anxieties had brought new fears.

Meanwhile, I had kept putting off happiness and hope.

If only I had cared for my happiness and my hopes in time—if I had increased my hope a little more each day, given without expecting a return, loved without expecting anything back, and, most importantly, known the worth of the love and the strength within me. Then there would have been no need for such a cleaning.

I had difficulty clearing out my fears.

They had linked together like a chain and were almost locked. Still, I let them go without reproaching myself for once keeping them inside me like a nightmare.

I knew that if I had gotten angry at my fears and shouted at them, they would have come back at me.

Today I did some cleaning.

I planted joy, trust, and love.

I planted giving without receiving, loving without being loved, and sharing.

I planted wildness, naturalness, and forgiveness inside me.

I planted passion in every cell of my body.

I planted enthusiasm, excitement, and quiet.

All beautiful thoughts come to me in silence.

And I planted acceptance:

not surrender, but acceptance of things as they are.

Moral: True cleanliness is in the heart: throwing away disappointment, fear, and resentment and planting love, forgiveness, and hope in their place. The one who cleans his inner world finds peace.

Words to Avoid

Audio
Image
Text

Avoid these phrases, especially in the family, and never use them against your spouse:

“Don’t talk nonsense!”

These words humiliate your spouse and tell them that you do not value their thoughts and feelings and that you do not understand them. Instead, try saying gently, “I see it differently” or “I don’t entirely agree.” In this way you both state your own view and preserve mutual respect.

“Don’t exaggerate!”

This can make your spouse feel that their view of events is wrong and that they are being criticized. Instead, ask, “Can you tell me more about what’s worrying you and how you feel?” That opens the way to understanding.

“Look at their husband (their wife)! That one does this and does that — and what about you?”

This sentence is very dangerous, because it brings a third person into the family. Comparing your spouse with someone else brings them insecurity. Your spouse will think your mind is on that person, and their sense of self will be wounded. They will feel unloved. Instead of such comparisons, explain your expectations clearly.

“You don’t understand!”

This is an insult: you are humiliating your spouse. If you think they will not understand you on this subject, or you do not want to share your thought, say, “I don’t want to talk about this; don’t be hurt; let’s talk about something else,” or “This probably doesn’t interest you.”

“Don’t go there, don’t talk to them, don’t do this, don’t wear that, don’t talk like that, bring this, take that!”

Your spouse is not a slave to your commands. Respectfully discuss what you do not want or dislike, and agree on it. Make requests rather than issuing orders, and remember to say “please.” That prevents needless quarrels.

Moral: Words can hurt the person we love the most. In the family, we should use the language of respect and requests, not orders and insults.

Moral: The most valuable adornment is honesty. A person rises not by outward appearance, but by true words and a clear conscience.

Text

On a cold winter day, a king changed his clothes and went out walking, taking his chief vizier with him. Beside a ravine they met an elderly man soaking hides in the water and beating them.

The king greeted the old man:

“Peace be upon you, venerable elder.”

“And upon you, ruler of the world,” the old man replied.

“What did you do during the six?” the king asked.

“Unless you add six to six, it does not come to thirty-two,” the tanner replied.

The king asked again:

“Did you never rise in the night?”

“We did, Your Majesty, but others benefited.”

The king laughed and asked his last question:

“If I send you a goose, will you pluck it?”

“Certainly—and without making it cry out.”

The king and vizier walked on. The king asked:

“Did you understand our conversation?”

“No, Your Majesty,” the chief vizier answered in distress.

The king grew angry.

“Find out what it meant by this evening, or you will lose your head.”

After taking the king to the palace, the frightened vizier hurried back in alarm to the ravine. The tanner was still at work.

“What were you and the king talking about?” he asked.

The old man looked him up and down.

“Forgive me, I will not say it for nothing. Give me a hundred gold pieces and I will tell you.”

The vizier handed over the coins.

“You greeted the king as ruler of the world. How did you know he was the king?”

“I’m a tanner. No one but a king could wear the fur he had on.”

The vizier scratched his head.

“Then what did ‘Unless you add six to six, it does not come to thirty-two’ mean?”

The tanner collected another hundred coins before answering.

“He asked whether I had worked during the six months of summer, since I was working in this winter cold. I told him I had to work all twelve months to feed the thirty-two teeth in my mouth.”

The vizier went on:

“And what did he mean about rising in the night?”

The tanner accepted another hundred coins.

“He was asking whether I had children. I told him I had children, but all of them were girls; they had married and were of benefit to others.”

The vizier lowered his head.

“And the goose he said he would send?”

The tanner laughed.

“That one you can work out for yourself.”

Moral: A wise word is revealed to those who understand it. Not everyone understands what they hear — some answers a person must find for themselves.

Text

A beekeeper filled containers with the honey he had collected and brought them to a grocer to sell. They struck a bargain on the price and began to weigh the honey.

Because the grocer had only a one-kilo weight, he broke off pieces of honeycomb by hand and weighed the honey kilo by kilo. But before placing each piece on the scale, he never failed to lick his honey-smeared fingers.

At last the beekeeper’s patience ran out:

Moral: Even a small share in a job usually brings some gain from it.
