A generous man was once asked:

“Do you expect the poor to be grateful to you for everything you share with them?”

He replied:

“I am like a ladle in a cook’s hand. Although the food is served with the ladle, it is the cook—not the ladle—who gives it.”

Moral: Those who do good should not consider themselves the true owners of what they give. They are only instruments; the true Giver is God.

A Meaningless Question

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a graduate of the University of California, Berkeley, visited a rabbi in New York and asked for help studying the Talmud.

“Do you know Aramaic? No? What about Hebrew? Were you at least taught the Torah as a child?”

The young man answered no to every question.

“But I defended a dissertation on Socratic logic! I would like to study the Talmud to fill some gaps in my education.”

“The Talmud is one of the greatest works of human thought, but you are not yet ready to study it. Still, since you insist, I will give you a logic question. If you answer correctly, I will teach you. Two people climb down the same chimney. One emerges with a clean face and the other with a face covered in soot. Which one will go and wash?”

“You call that a logic question?” the Berkeley graduate asked confidently. “The person with the dirty face, of course!”

“Wrong. The person with the clean face will see the other’s sooty face, assume that his own face is dirty too, and go to wash. The person covered in soot will see a clean face in front of him and suppose that he has remained clean.”

“A very clever answer! Could you give me another chance?”

“All right. Two people climb down the same chimney. One has a dirty face and the other a clean one. Which will go and wash?”

“But we have just discussed that. The person with the clean face will go.”

“Wrong again. Both will wash. The person with the clean face will see his companion’s dirty face and think that he is dirty too. The other will notice that his companion looked at him and then went to wash, so he will realize that his own face must also be dirty.”

“I truly had not considered that. May I have one more question?”

“Very well. I will ask for the last time: two people climb down the same chimney. One has a clean face and the other a dirty one. Which will go and wash?”

“Now it is clear: both of them will go!”

“No. Neither will go. The person with the dirty face will see a clean face before him and assume that he is clean as well. The person with the clean face will notice that his companion is not going to wash and conclude that there is no need to do so.”

Growing desperate, the young man begged the rabbi for one more question. The rabbi began:

“Two people climb down the same chimney. One has a dirty face and the other a clean one…”

“Neither of them will wash!” the young man answered hastily.

“Wrong again,” the rabbi said calmly. “As a specialist in Socratic logic, think for yourself: how could two people climb down the same chimney together and only one of them emerge with soot on his face? Can you not see that the question itself is meaningless? If you spend your life searching for answers to meaningless questions, neither your answers nor your life will acquire meaning.”

Moral: An answer to a meaningless question cannot lead to truth. A well-formed question is already half the answer.

Avoid These Questions

Audio
Image
Text

Do not ask an unemployed person, “Are you still without work?”, an employee, “How much do you earn?”, a childless person, “You still have no children?”, or a single person, “When are you getting married?”

Nor should we embarrass someone by asking, “You did not know that?” What seems like ordinary curiosity to us may touch another person’s deepest wound.

Moral: Some questions wound the heart. Every painful question left unasked may lift a burden from someone’s shoulders.

Audio
Image
Text

It means living with virtue, wisdom, civility, justice, honor, and dignity.

Being courteous, perceptive, respectful, and humble—showing regard for others without praising oneself.

Thinking before acting, maintaining balance, remaining consistent, and standing upright in adversity.

Keeping one’s word; refusing to worship money or position; neither stealing nor taking what belongs to another.

Remaining free of hypocrisy and hatred, refusing to turn people against one another, and knowing how to love.

Moral: True worth lies not in knowledge alone, but in humanity. A heart capable of respect also makes room for love and justice.

Education or character

Audio
Image
Text

The king asks his minister:

— Minister, is education important or character?

The minister answers without thinking:

— Character, my king.

The king sends a proclamation to the country, orders to find the best animal trainer and bring him to him. They find such a person and bring him. The king asks the man:

— How long can it take you to train a cat to serve the assembly with a bowl in its paw?

— Six months, my king.

Six months pass. The man is called to the palace with the cat he has trained. The king asks:

— Did you teach it?

— I did, my king.

The entire palace gathers. The cat begins to serve them with a bowl in its paw. When the cat approaches the minister, the king asks:

— Minister, is education important or character?

Before the minister can answer the king, he takes out a mouse he has been holding ready in his pocket and drops it on the ground. Seeing the mouse, the cat drops its paw and runs after it.

The vizier answers the king:

— Character, my king.

Moral: Education does not change character; when the opportunity arises, the true nature is revealed. Knowledge is good, but character is more important.

Everything is in our own hands

Audio
Image
Text

(From Indian folklore)

A young boy gets the idea of ​​“playing a game” with an old monk. He catches a bird. He takes the bird in his hand and hides it behind his back, and asks the monk:

— O wise old man, tell me, is the bird in my hand alive or dead?

But he thinks to himself: if the old man says “it is alive”, I will strangle the bird and kill it; if not, “it is dead”, I will open my hand and let the bird fly away, and thus scare it away.

The wise old man thinks for a while and says:

— My dear son, the answer to your question is in your own hands.

The boy stops and lowers his head.

The old man continues:

— War and peace, punishment and pardon, good and evil - all this is in your own hands. The path of a person’s life is determined not by his mind, but by his intentions. Remember that every person is capable of doing both good and evil. But there is always a choice, and this choice is in our own hands.

Moral: Both good and evil are human choices. Our destiny is determined not by our minds, but by our intentions.

How can I get rid of my thoughts?

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a man came to visit one of the scholars of his time and asked:

— How can I get rid of the painful thoughts that I am a prisoner of?

Instead of answering him, the scholar stood up, hugged a nearby pole and began to shout with all his might:

— Separate me from this pole, save my life from it!… —.

The man, looking at him in surprise, asked:

— Why are you doing this? I thought you were a wise man and came to ask for help. But I see that you do not look like a wise man. You yourself held the pole, it did not hold you. Let go of it, and your life will be saved!

The scholar let go of the pole and said to the man:

— You yourself answered your own question. It is not you who holds thoughts, but you! Let them go so that your life will be saved.

Moral: You yourself hold the thoughts that oppress you; it is in your hands to let them go. Change your perspective, and your burden will lighten.

Text

From someone who has fallen hard in life:

I no longer try to make anyone love me. I am aware that those who don't like me can always find an excuse.

I now know my worth and I am aware that I don't have to prove myself to anyone.

I am now aware that I am happier with the person I am "myself" with.

Moral: True maturity is not about changing others, but about knowing who to let into your life and who to stay away from.

Phrases to avoid

Audio
Image
Text

Avoid these phrases, especially in the family, and never use them against your spouse:

“Don’t talk nonsense!”

This phrase makes your spouse feel that their thoughts and feelings are worthless, misunderstood, and belittled. Instead, if you gently say, “I don’t think like you, I don’t completely agree with you on this issue,” you will show that you take their opinion into account and maintain respect between you.

“Don’t exaggerate!”

This phrase may sound like a sign that your spouse’s view of events is wrong; he or she will feel criticized. Instead, by asking, “Can you explain a little more clearly why you are worried and what you are feeling?” you will take a step towards eliminating the misunderstanding that has arisen.

“Look at his or her husband (wife)! He or she does this, and you do that?”

This phrase is very dangerous because when you say it, you are bringing third parties into your family relationship. Comparing your spouse to someone else will make them distrust you. They will think that you are thinking the same thing, and they will feel hurt and unloved. Instead of making comparisons and analogies, clearly state your expectations.

“You don’t understand!”

This is an insult—you are putting your spouse down. If you think that they won’t understand you on this topic or don’t want to share your opinion, it is more appropriate to say, “I don’t want to talk about this, don’t be offended, let’s talk about something else” or “This probably doesn’t interest you.”

“Don’t go there, don’t talk to her, don’t do this, don’t wear that, don’t talk like that, bring this, take that!”

Your spouse is not your subordinate. It is better to respectfully discuss with them what you don’t want or don’t like and to avoid conflict. Instead of using impassioned statements, use requests (please, please, etc.).

Moral: Words can hurt the person we love the most. In the family, we should use the language of respect and requests, not orders and insults.
