A generous man was asked:

“When you help people in need, do you expect their gratitude?”

He replied:

“I think of myself as a ladle in a cook’s hand. The food passes through it, but the ladle did not provide the meal. I simply share what God has given me. Rather than expect praise, I am grateful for the chance to help.”

Moral: Do not make kindness a debt others must repay. Share your blessings humbly and respect the dignity of those you help. The opportunity to give is itself a blessing.

Question the question

Audio
Image
Text

A story is told of a Berkeley graduate who visited a rabbi in New York and asked for help studying the Talmud.

The rabbi asked:

“Do you know Aramaic? What about Hebrew? Were you introduced to the Torah as a child?”

The young man answered no to every question, then added:

“But I’ve defended a dissertation on Socratic logic. I want to study the Talmud to fill some gaps in my education.”

“Studying the Talmud requires serious preparation,” the rabbi said. “But since you’re so keen, let me ask you a question first. Two men climb down a chimney. One has a clean face; the other’s face is covered in soot. Which one washes his face?”

The young man answered confidently:

“The one with the dirty face, of course!”

“Consider this instead: the clean-faced man sees the soot on his companion’s face, assumes his own face is dirty too, and goes to wash. The dirty-faced man sees a clean face before him and feels no need to wash.”

“Interesting! May I have another chance?”

The rabbi repeated the question:

“Two men climb down a chimney. One has a clean face, and the other has a dirty face. Which one washes?”

“This time I know: the one with the clean face!”

“But both might wash. The clean-faced man looks at his companion and goes to wash. The other notices that his companion went to wash after looking at him and realizes that his own face must be dirty.”

The young man paused.

“I hadn’t considered that. Ask me again.”

The rabbi repeated the question. This time, the young man did not hesitate:

“They both wash!”

“Perhaps neither does. The dirty-faced man sees his companion’s clean face and stays where he is. The clean-faced man notices that his companion isn’t washing and assumes that his own face must be clean as well.”

Thoroughly confused, the young man asked for one last chance. The rabbi began:

“Two men climb down a chimney. One has a clean face, and the other—”

“Neither washes!” the young man interrupted.

The rabbi smiled.

“Again, you rushed to answer. Why didn’t you ask how one man’s face stayed clean when they both came down the same chimney? How do we know what they saw or thought? Each time, I introduced a new assumption, and you accepted it as part of the question.”

He paused, then continued:

“Sometimes the problem is not that we cannot find an answer, but that we seek one without examining the question. First, we must distinguish what we know from what we merely assume.”

Moral: Not every question has a single, definite answer. Clear thinking begins with examining the question and separating facts from assumptions, rather than rushing to a conclusion.

Avoid These Questions

Audio
Image
Text

Pause before asking someone who is looking for work, “Still haven’t found a job?” Think twice before asking an employed person how much they earn, a couple without children whether they have children yet, or someone who is single when they are getting married.

And when someone doesn’t know something, don’t embarrass them by saying, “You didn’t even know that?”

Behind every answer may be a story the person is not ready to share: worry, loss, disappointment, or simply a personal choice they do not have to explain.

A question that satisfies our passing curiosity may reopen someone else’s old wound. Before you ask, consider whether you want to support the person or merely satisfy your curiosity.

Moral: Tact means respecting other people’s privacy. Sometimes care is shown by asking a question; sometimes it is shown by choosing not to.

Being Human

Audio
Image
Text

“Becoming a scholar is easy; becoming a decent person is hard,” the saying goes. But what does it mean to be a decent person?

It means living by your conscience: acting honestly and fairly, preserving your own dignity without diminishing anyone else’s.

It means being considerate, courteous, and humble. Respecting people for their humanity, rather than their wealth or position. Recognizing your own shortcomings, not just those of others.

It means thinking about the consequences of your actions, admitting your mistakes, and taking responsibility for what you do. Keeping your composure in an argument and not losing heart when difficulties arise.

It means keeping your word even when it is inconvenient. Refusing to measure a person’s worth by money or status, to covet what belongs to others, or to take what is rightfully theirs.

It means not lying, spreading slander, or betraying someone for personal gain. Standing up for those treated unjustly, even when staying silent would be easier.

It means not turning people against one another or taking pleasure in someone else’s suffering. Knowing how to love—and showing that love through care and action, not words alone.

Moral: Our knowledge shows what we have learned; our actions show who we have become. Our humanity is revealed in our everyday choices: to be honest, to respect others’ dignity, and to care when someone is hurting.

Audio

Text

Do not boast about your wealth to someone struggling financially, your health to someone who is ill, or your carefree life to someone weighed down by sorrow.

Do not flaunt your freedom before someone deprived of theirs, or your strength before someone feeling powerless.

Speak sensitively about your children with someone longing for a child, and about your parents with someone grieving theirs.

Share your joy, but remain mindful of another person’s pain. Sometimes listening and being present matter more than speaking.

Moral: Do not turn your blessings into boasts that deepen someone else’s hurt. Choose your words with understanding: seek to recognize their feelings rather than remind them of what they lack.

Think before speaking

Audio
Image
Text

Before you speak, ask yourself: will your words offer comfort, help with a difficulty, or bring clarity where it is needed?

Speaking kindly does not mean hiding the truth. Even a painful truth can be told without humiliating someone. If your words serve only to wound, silence is the better choice.

Moral: The value of words lies in the good they do, not merely in how pleasant they sound. Speak the truth with respect, and never speak simply to hurt.

Try again

Audio
Image
Text

Days come and go. Some end in joy, others in weariness or sadness. Sometimes our plans remain unfinished, and however hard we try, we do not get the result we hoped for.

At such moments, do not turn “I didn’t finish” into self-blame, or “I couldn’t do it” into a reason to give up. Something that did not work out today may still work out another day.

Rest a little and gather your strength. Tomorrow may bring another chance to try again, take a different approach, or take even a small step forward.

Moral: An unfinished task does not mean you are incapable. Give yourself time: moving forward sometimes means starting again, and sometimes it means resting before you continue.

Education or Character?

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a king asked his vizier:

“Which matters more in a person: education or character?”

“Character, Your Majesty,” the vizier replied.

The king disagreed. He ordered the finest animal trainer in the kingdom to be brought to the palace.

“How long would it take you to teach a cat to carry a tray and serve guests?” he asked.

“Six months, Your Majesty.”

Six months later, the trainer returned with the cat. The guests assembled, and the gathering began. Holding a tray, the cat calmly approached each guest. Everyone watched in amazement.

The king turned to his vizier with a smile.

“Now tell me: education or character?”

Before answering, the vizier released a little mouse he had brought with him. The moment the cat saw it, it dropped the tray and raced after it.

“Character, Your Majesty,” the vizier said quietly.

The king fell silent. The behavior that had impressed everyone had vanished in an instant.

Moral: Learned behavior is not the same as strength of character. The value of education should be evident not only in knowledge and skills, but also in honesty, responsibility, and the choices we make when tested.

In your hands

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a young man decided to outwit a wise monk. He caught a little bird, concealed it between his palms, and held his hands behind his back.

“Tell me, wise man,” he asked, “is the bird in my hands alive or dead?”

He thought to himself: “If he says it’s alive, I’ll squeeze it to death. If he says it’s dead, I’ll open my hands and let it fly away. Either way, I’ll prove him wrong.”

The monk looked at him and answered calmly:

“The answer, my son, is in your hands.”

The young man hesitated and lowered his head.

The monk continued:

“You want to prove me wrong, but this bird’s fate depends on your choice. You can hurt it, or you can set it free. Wisdom is shown not by trapping someone with a clever question, but by how you use the power you have.”

The young man brought his hands forward and opened them. The bird spread its wings and flew away.

This time, he did not try to prove himself right. He chose to do what was right.

Moral: Not everything in life is within our control, but we are responsible for our choices. Good intentions gain meaning through the actions that follow. When you have the power to hurt, choose to protect.

Letting go of painful thoughts

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a man came to a wise scholar and asked:

“Painful thoughts won’t leave me alone. I feel trapped by them. How can I free myself?”

Instead of answering, the scholar stood up, wrapped his arms around a nearby post, and shouted:

“Help! Someone free me from this post!”

The man stared in astonishment.

“I came to you for advice, and now look at you! The post isn’t holding you—you’re holding it. Open your arms, and you’ll be free!”

The scholar let go and said quietly:

“You’ve just found part of your answer. Sometimes we return to a painful thought again and again, holding it as tightly as I held that post. We don’t have to cling to every thought or follow wherever it leads.”

Moral: We cannot always choose which thoughts arise, but we can learn how to respond to them. Letting go does not mean forcing ourselves to forget. It means learning not to let pain govern our whole lives. Sometimes that takes time and support.

Now I Understand

Audio
Image
Text

In the words of someone who has weathered life’s ups and downs:

I no longer try to make everyone like me. I understand that, whatever I do, some people simply won’t.

I know my own worth now. I do not owe everyone proof of it.

I keep my distance from people who build themselves up by putting others down.

I can now tell the difference between words and intention: those who want to act look for a way; those who do not often find a “but.”

I do not share my secrets with people who gossip. If they betray someone else’s trust, I cannot assume they will respect mine.

I no longer exhaust myself trying to persuade someone who refuses to listen. Not every conversation needs to become an argument, and not every disagreement needs to become a battle.

I make no room in my life for people who habitually lie, make empty promises, and go back on their word.

I distance myself from those who mistake cruelty and harshness for strength. I value people who can recognize their mistakes, apologize, and change their behavior.

Now I understand that I am happier with people around whom I can be myself.

Moral: Maturity lies less in trying to change others than in recognizing your own worth and boundaries. Knowing whom to welcome into your life and when to step away is a way of respecting yourself and finding peace.

Inner housekeeping

Audio
Image
Text

Today I did some cleaning.
Not in my home, but within myself…

I looked into corners of my heart I hadn’t visited in a long time. One by one, I brought to light old hurts, fragments of broken hopes, and grievances I had never voiced.

I began with the disappointments. I didn’t try to relive each one or work out who had been right and who had been wrong. I simply realized that I no longer wanted to carry them.

How much room they had taken up inside me…
I thought I had forgotten them long ago. But forgetting, it turned out, was not the same as being free.

I made room for forgiveness—for others and for myself. Not because what had happened was acceptable, but because I didn’t want to carry the pain for the rest of my life.

Then I faced my envy. How much beauty in my own life had I overlooked while watching other people’s lives! There had been times when I had treated someone else’s success as my own failure.

I put gratitude in its place. I remembered what I had, the people beside me, and all I could still do. It felt as though I had rediscovered something long lost: the value of my own life.

I looked carefully into my heart. Thankfully, I found no deep-rooted hatred or malice. But some old wounds still ached. I didn’t blame myself for them. They needed time to heal.

Then came my fears.

At first, I was afraid even to approach them. I had grown so used to living with them that imagining their absence felt strange. Each was linked to the next: the fear of making mistakes, of losing what mattered, of not being liked…

Every worry had given rise to another fear. And I had let those fears persuade me to put off my dreams, my joy, my life.

“I’ll wait a little longer…”
“Once everything is sorted out…”
“I’ll start later…”

How many todays had I lost while waiting for that later?

I couldn’t make all my fears disappear in a single day. But I tried to listen to them and understand: which were protecting me, and which were standing in my way? I realized that fear didn’t always have to stop me. Sometimes it was possible to take a step while still being afraid.

I didn’t blame myself for the way I had lived before. Yesterday, I hadn’t understood what I understood today.

Today I did some cleaning within myself.

I made room for joy.
For hope, love, and faith in myself…

I made room for kindness, for giving and sharing without expecting anything in return. I promised myself that loving others would not mean forgetting myself.

I welcomed back a little playfulness, a little courage, and a little childlike openness. I remembered that I didn’t need a special reason to laugh.

I made room for silence, too. Sometimes the noise inside me has to settle before I can hear my own voice.

Finally, I began learning to accept myself, my past, and the things I could not change.

That didn’t mean surrendering to everything. It meant ending the struggle with what I couldn’t change, so I could give my energy to what I could.

Today I did some cleaning within myself.
There was still more to do.
But inside, I had room to breathe again.

Moral: Caring for your heart means understanding your feelings, rather than suppressing them, and gradually letting go of what weighs you down. Self-compassion makes room for love, hope, and peace.

Justice and compassion

Audio
Image
Text

According to a tale told about the Gallipoli campaign, morphine was so scarce that it was given only to wounded soldiers who still had a chance of survival.

One day, among the severely wounded brought to Dr. Tarık Nusret was his own son. Seeing that he could not save him, the doctor made no exception for his son and withheld the injection.

Moral: Refusing to let personal ties influence a decision can be a painful test. Yet justice and compassion must remain inseparable: even when recovery is no longer possible, a person’s suffering and dignity still matter.

“Yes.”

“What kind of lies?”

“A lie I tell only to myself. Every day, I say, ‘You’re stronger, more knowledgeable, and more capable than anyone else.’”

“Perhaps you really are?”

“No, I can’t claim that. There are people who are stronger, know more, or can do things better than I can.”

Moral: You do not need to think you are better than everyone else. Saying “I can learn; I can improve” can give you strength too. Self-belief becomes a foundation for growth when it is matched by effort.

Other People’s Thoughts

Image
Text

One day, a man lost his comma (,). His words ran together, and he forgot where to pause for breath.

Another day, he lost his period (.). His thoughts stretched on endlessly; he could no longer finish one before moving to the next.

Then he lost his exclamation mark (!). His joy, anger, and wonder fell silent.

One day, he lost his question mark (?). He stopped asking questions and accepted everything he heard without question.

Next, he lost his colon (:). He forgot how to explain his thoughts or give reasons for what he said.

By the end of his life, all he had left were quotation marks.

And inside them were nothing but other people’s thoughts.

Moral: When we stop questioning, thinking, and expressing our feelings, we begin to lose our own voice. Learning from others is valuable, but repeating their thoughts must not take the place of thinking for ourselves.

Words to Avoid

Audio
Image
Text

Disagreements are part of family life. But the words we choose can either help us understand one another or damage the closeness between us.

“Stop talking nonsense!”

This tells your partner, “Your opinion does not matter to me.” You can disagree without dismissing what they say:

“I see this differently. I’d like to understand your point of view too.”

“You’re overreacting!”

Something that seems minor to you may deeply trouble your partner. Rather than dismissing their feelings, ask:

“What worries you most about this? I want to listen.”

“Look at her husband—or his wife! Why can’t you be like that?”

Comparisons can leave someone feeling inadequate and unappreciated. Instead of holding up another person as an example, explain what you need:

“It matters to me that we share the housework. Can we find an arrangement that works for both of us?”

“You wouldn’t understand!”

This closes the door to conversation. If you cannot find the words or are not ready to talk, say so honestly:

“I’m finding it hard to explain how I feel. Please give me a little time, and we can talk later.”

“Don’t go there! Don’t speak to that person! Don’t wear that!”

Your partner’s decisions are not yours to control. Explain your concerns, listen to their perspective, and seek agreement on matters that affect you both. Adding “please” to an order does not automatically make it a respectful request; you must also recognize the other person’s right to choose.

“This worries me. Can we talk about it and try to understand each other?”

Moral: Respect matters even when we disagree. Explain your feelings, make your needs clear, and listen in return. Closeness grows when we hear each other, not when we silence each other.

The Flower of Honesty

Audio
Image
Text

In a distant kingdom, a young prince was preparing to take the throne. By royal custom, he had to marry before his coronation. He invited the young women of the kingdom to the palace so that he could choose his future wife.

The daughter of a woman who worked at the palace loved the prince. When she heard about the invitation, she told her mother:

“I want to go too.”

Her mother was worried.

“The daughters of wealthy, distinguished families will be there. I’m afraid you’ll be disappointed.”

“I know, Mother. But I’d like the chance to meet him, even just once.”

On the appointed day, the palace hall filled with young women in beautiful gowns. The prince gave each of them a seed.

“Plant this and care for it,” he said. “Return in six months with the flower you have grown. I will marry the woman who brings the most beautiful flower.”

The young women went home full of hope.

The servant’s daughter planted her seed in a pot. Every day, she checked the soil, watered it as needed, and eagerly waited for the first green shoot. But days and weeks passed, and nothing appeared.

She sought advice from gardeners and followed their suggestions. Still, the seed did not sprout.

When the six months were over, her mother asked:

“Will you really go with an empty pot?”

The young woman looked down at it.

“Yes. I’ve done everything I could. I can’t take a different flower and pretend I grew it from his seed.”

At the palace, flowers bloomed in every color. Her own pot held only soil. Embarrassed, she stood to one side.

The prince entered the hall and examined the pots one by one. When he reached her, he asked:

“Where is your flower?”

“I cared for the seed for six months, but it never sprouted,” she replied. “I have no flower to show you.”

The prince listened carefully. Then he turned to the gathering.

“I have made my choice.”

He pointed to the young woman holding the empty pot.

A murmur of astonishment filled the hall.

“But she has no flower!”

“None of the seeds I gave you could grow,” the prince explained. “This young woman had the courage to bring me the result as it was. She has brought me the most precious flower of all: honesty.”

Moral: Honesty sometimes means having the courage to stand before everyone with an empty pot. There is no shame in falling short of the result you hoped for. Pretending you achieved it means betraying your conscience.

Plucking the goose

Audio
Image
Text

One bitter winter day, a king changed out of his royal robes and went for a walk with his grand vizier. They came upon an old man working beside a stream, dipping hides into the icy water and beating them as he worked the leather.

“Peace be upon you, venerable elder,” the king said.

“And upon you, ruler of the world,” the old man replied.

“What did you do during the six?” the king asked.

“Unless you add six to six, there isn’t enough for thirty-two.”

“And did you never get up in the night?”

“I did, Your Majesty. But other households have reaped the benefit.”

The king smiled.

“If I send you a goose, can you pluck it?”

“Certainly, Your Majesty—and without a squawk!”

The king and his vizier walked on. Once they were out of earshot, the king asked:

“Did you understand our conversation?”

“No, Your Majesty,” the vizier admitted.

“Then work it out before nightfall,” the king said sternly, “or you will answer with your head!”

After escorting the king back to the palace, the frightened vizier hurried to the stream. The old man was still at work.

“What were you and the king talking about?” he demanded. “You must explain!”

The old man looked him up and down.

“Each answer will cost you a hundred gold coins.”

The vizier had little choice. He handed over the money.

“First, how did you know he was the king?”

“I work with hides. One look told me what his fur coat was worth. Only a king could afford such a thing.”

The vizier produced another hundred coins.

“What did six have to do with thirty-two?”

“He was asking what I had done during the six months of summer, if I still had to work in this bitter cold. I told him that unless I worked six months in summer and six in winter, I couldn’t keep these thirty-two teeth fed.”

The vizier paid a third time.

“And what did he mean about getting up in the night?”

“He was asking whether I had children. I told him I did, but they were all daughters. They have married and now have households of their own to look after.”

The vizier paused before asking his final question.

“And the goose he promised to send you—who was that?”

The old man glanced at the gold and smiled.

“That one you can work out for yourself!”

Moral: Hearing the words is not the same as understanding them. High office does not guarantee wisdom; sometimes an ordinary person, taught by life, sees what a royal adviser cannot.

Learning to say “yet”

Audio
Image
Text

Instead of saying, “I can’t do this,” say, “There are things I still need to learn. With more knowledge and practice, I can get better.”

Moral: The way you speak to yourself influences your next step. Adding “yet” to “I can’t do this” leaves room for learning. A mistake becomes an opportunity to grow when you learn from it and try again.

Text

A beekeeper brings several containers of honeycomb to a grocer to sell. They agree on a price and begin weighing it.

The grocer has only a one-kilogram weight, so he breaks the honeycomb into pieces by hand and weighs it a kilogram at a time. But before placing each new piece on the scales, he licks the honey from his fingers.

Once, twice, three times…

The beekeeper notices that some of his honey is reaching the grocer’s mouth before it reaches the scales. He objects, but the grocer carries on as though nothing has happened.

At last, the beekeeper loses patience.

Still licking his sticky fingers, the grocer gives him a sly smile.

Moral: It is natural to gain something from the work you do. But that is no excuse for helping yourself to someone else’s share.
