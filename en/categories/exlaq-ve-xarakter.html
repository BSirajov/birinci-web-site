A Meaningless Question

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a graduate of the University of California, Berkeley, visited a rabbi in New York and asked for help studying the Talmud.

“Do you know Aramaic? No? What about Hebrew? Were you at least taught the Torah as a child?”

The young man answered no to every question.

“But I defended a dissertation on Socratic logic! I would like to study the Talmud to fill some gaps in my education.”

“The Talmud is one of the greatest works of human thought, but you are not yet ready to study it. Still, since you insist, I will give you a logic question. If you answer correctly, I will teach you. Two people climb down the same chimney. One emerges with a clean face and the other with a face covered in soot. Which one will go and wash?”

“You call that a logic question?” the Berkeley graduate asked confidently. “The person with the dirty face, of course!”

“Wrong. The person with the clean face will see the other’s sooty face, assume that his own face is dirty too, and go to wash. The person covered in soot will see a clean face in front of him and suppose that he has remained clean.”

“A very clever answer! Could you give me another chance?”

“All right. Two people climb down the same chimney. One has a dirty face and the other a clean one. Which will go and wash?”

“But we have just discussed that. The person with the clean face will go.”

“Wrong again. Both will wash. The person with the clean face will see his companion’s dirty face and think that he is dirty too. The other will notice that his companion looked at him and then went to wash, so he will realize that his own face must also be dirty.”

“I hadn’t thought of that. May I try once more?”

“Very well. I will ask for the last time: two people climb down the same chimney. One has a clean face and the other a dirty one. Which will go and wash?”

“Now it is clear: both of them will go!”

“No. Neither will go. The person with the dirty face will see a clean face before him and assume that he is clean as well. The person with the clean face will notice that his companion is not going to wash and conclude that there is no need to do so.”

Growing desperate, the young man begged the rabbi for one more question. The rabbi began:

“Two people climb down the same chimney. One has a dirty face and the other a clean one…”

“Neither of them will wash!” the young man answered hastily.

“Wrong again,” the rabbi said calmly. “You studied Socratic logic. Ask yourself: how could two people climb down the same chimney together and only one get soot on his face? The question itself makes no sense. Spend your life answering meaningless questions, and neither the answers nor the search will give you meaning.”

Moral: An answer to a meaningless question cannot lead to truth. A well-formed question is already half the answer.

Training or Nature?

Audio
Image
Text

A king asked his minister:

“Which matters more: training or nature?”

The minister answered without hesitation:

“Nature, Your Majesty.”

The king ordered his subjects to find the finest animal trainer in the country. When the man was brought before him, the king asked:

“How long would it take you to teach a cat to serve guests with a bowl in its paw?”

“Six months, Your Majesty.”

Six months later, the trainer returned to the palace with the cat.

“Have you taught it?” the king asked.

“Yes, Your Majesty.”

The court gathered, and the cat began serving the guests. As it approached the minister, the king asked again:

“Which matters more: training or nature?”

Before answering, the minister took a mouse from his pocket and released it. The cat immediately dropped the bowl and chased it.

Then the minister turned to the king.

“Nature, Your Majesty.”

Moral: Training may shape behavior, but an opportunity can reveal the nature beneath it. Learning matters, but character matters too.

It Is in Our Hands

Audio
Image
Text

(From Indian folklore)

A boy decided to trick an old monk. He caught a bird, concealed it in his hands behind his back, and asked:

“Wise old man, is the bird I’m holding alive or dead?”

“If he says it’s alive,” the boy thought, “I’ll squeeze it to death. If he says it’s dead, I’ll open my hands and let it fly away. Either way, I’ll prove him wrong.”

The monk thought for a moment, then answered:

“My son, the answer is in your hands.”

The boy stood still and lowered his head.

The monk continued:

“War and peace, punishment and forgiveness, good and evil—all lie in our hands. Our intentions, not merely our cleverness, determine the course of our lives. Every person is capable of both good and evil. There is always a choice, and that choice is ours.”

Moral: Good and evil begin with choices. What shapes our lives is not simply how clever we are, but what we choose to do.

Letting Go of Troubling Thoughts

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a man came to visit one of the scholars of his time and asked:

“How can I escape the painful thoughts that keep me prisoner?”

Instead of answering, the scholar stood, wrapped his arms around a nearby post, and began shouting:

“Help! Free me from this post!”

The man, looking at him in surprise, asked:

“Why are you doing that? I came because I thought you were wise. The post isn’t holding you—you’re holding it. Let go, and you’ll be free!”

The scholar let go of the pole and said to the man:

“You’ve answered your own question,” the scholar said, releasing the post. “Your thoughts are not holding on to you; you are holding on to them. Let go, and you can begin to feel free.”

Moral: You yourself hold the thoughts that oppress you; it is in your hands to let them go. Change your perspective, and your burden will lighten.

A Little Inner Housekeeping

Audio
Image
Text

Today I did some cleaning.

I started inside myself:

in my muscles, my nerves, my veins—right down to my blood.

I cleared out everything that had been gathering in the depths.

First, the disappointments. I let them go without examining each one again.

You should have seen

how much space they had taken up.

In their place, I planted forgiveness, one seed for every disappointment.

Then I swept out jealousy.

There was more than I had realized.

Letting it go was easy, and I felt happy,

as though I had recovered something I had lost.

Thank Allah, I found no hatred or grudges in my heart.

Somehow, those had already gone.

Next came fear.

At first, I was afraid even to touch it.

I had grown so used to living with it.

How had I become accustomed to so much pain and worry?

Each day, fresh anxieties had brought new fears.

Meanwhile, I had kept putting off happiness and hope.

If only I had given them as much attention—nurtured a little hope each day, given without expecting a return, loved without conditions, and recognized the strength already within me. Perhaps then I would not have needed such a thorough clearing out.

The fears were hardest to remove.

They were tangled together like chains. Still, I let them go without reproaching myself for keeping them so long.

I knew that fighting them with anger would only draw them back.

Today I did some cleaning.

In the space I cleared, I planted joy, trust, and love.

I planted generosity, unconditional affection, and a willingness to share.

I planted a little wildness, sincerity, and forgiveness.

I planted love in every cell of my body.

I planted enthusiasm, excitement, and quiet.

My best thoughts come in quiet moments.

And I planted acceptance:

not surrender, but the willingness to see things as they are.

Moral: True cleanliness is in the heart: throwing away disappointment, fear, and resentment and planting love, forgiveness, and hope in their place. The one who cleans his inner world finds peace.

Words to Avoid

Audio
Image
Text

Avoid these phrases, especially in the family, and never use them against your spouse:

“Don’t talk nonsense!”

This dismisses your spouse’s thoughts and feelings. Instead, try saying gently, “I see it differently” or “I don’t entirely agree.” You can disagree while showing that their opinion matters.

“Don’t exaggerate!”

This can make your spouse feel that their concerns are being dismissed. Instead, ask, “Can you tell me more about what’s worrying you and how you feel?” That opens the way to understanding.

“Why can’t you be more like their husband or wife?”

Comparing your spouse with someone else brings an outsider into your relationship and can leave your partner feeling hurt, unloved, and unable to trust you. Say clearly what you need rather than making comparisons.

“You don’t understand!”

This can sound insulting and dismissive. If you do not want to discuss a subject, say so respectfully: “Please don’t be hurt, but I’d rather not talk about this now. Could we talk about something else?”

“Don’t go there, don’t talk to her, don’t do this, don’t wear that, don’t talk like that, bring this, take that!”

Your spouse is your partner, not your subordinate. Discuss your concerns respectfully. Make requests rather than issuing orders, and remember to say “please.”

Moral: Words can hurt the person we love the most. In the family, we should use the language of respect and requests, not orders and insults.

Text

On a bitter winter day, a king went out in disguise with his chief vizier. Beside a stream they met an old tanner dipping hides into the icy water and beating them.

The king greeted the old man:

“Peace be upon you, venerable elder.”

“And upon you, ruler of the world,” the old man replied.

“What did you do during the six?” the king asked.

“Unless you add six to six, you cannot feed thirty-two,” the tanner replied.

The king asked again:

“Did you never rise in the night?”

“We did, Your Majesty, but others benefited.”

The king laughed and asked his last question:

“If I send you a goose, will you pluck it?”

“Certainly—and without making it cry out.”

The king and vizier walked on. The king asked:

“Did you understand our conversation?”

“No, Your Majesty,” the vizier admitted.

The king grew angry.

“Find out what it meant by this evening, or you will lose your head.”

After escorting the king home, the frightened vizier hurried back to the stream. The tanner was still at work.

“What were you and the king talking about?” he asked.

The old man looked him up and down.

“I don’t give answers for nothing. Pay me a hundred gold coins.”

The vizier handed over the coins.

“How did you recognize the king?”

“I’m a tanner. No one else could afford the fur he was wearing.”

The vizier scratched his head.

“What did you mean about adding six to six to feed thirty-two?”

The tanner collected another hundred coins before answering.

“He asked why I hadn’t earned enough during the six warm months to rest in winter. I told him I had to work all twelve months to feed the thirty-two teeth in my mouth.”

The vizier went on:

“And what did he mean about rising in the night?”

The tanner accepted another hundred coins.

“He was asking whether I had children to support me. I told him I had daughters, but they had married and now belonged to other households.”

The vizier lowered his head.

“And the goose he said he would send?”

The tanner laughed.

“That one you can work out for yourself.”

Moral: A wise word is revealed to those who understand it. Not everyone understands what they hear — some answers a person must find for themselves.

Text

A beekeeper filled containers with the honey he had collected and brought them to a grocer to sell. They struck a bargain on the price and began to weigh the honey.

Because the grocer had only a one-kilo weight, he broke off pieces of honeycomb by hand and weighed the honey kilo by kilo. But before placing each piece on the scale, he never failed to lick his honey-smeared fingers.

Once, twice, three times… The beekeeper saw that some of the honey never reached the scale — it stayed on the grocer’s fingers and went to his tongue. He frowned and showed his displeasure. The grocer paid no attention and kept weighing, licking his fingers as he went.

At last the beekeeper’s patience ran out:

“You’ve licked away half my honey!”

Still licking his honeyed fingers, the grocer smiled quietly and replied:

“Don’t take offense, brother. Whoever handles honey is bound to lick a finger.”

Moral: Even a small share in a job usually brings some gain from it.
