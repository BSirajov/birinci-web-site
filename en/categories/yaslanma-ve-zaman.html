Life flows silently

Audio
Image
Text

Time is a strange wonder... We can neither see it, nor hold it, nor bring it back. It passes silently—in the movement of clock hands, in the changing days, in the gray appearing in our hair, in children growing up, in memories receding into the distance...

Often, we understand time only after we have lost it. A day that seemed ordinary yesterday becomes our most treasured memory years later. A conversation, a glance, a smile that we once considered insignificant, or a simple evening spent with someone we love, may later become one of the most beautiful moments of our lives.

Time stops for no one. It yields neither to wealth, nor to fame, nor to human desire. It is the greatest treasure, given to everyone in equal measure, yet valued differently by each of us. A person's life is measured, in truth, not in years but in the meaningful moments they have lived.

Sometimes we think that life still lies ahead and that we have plenty of time. We will do it tomorrow, go next week, talk someday, meet later... But the time we call ‘later’ does not always come.

Time takes much from us: our youth, certain people, certain dreams, certain opportunities... Yet in return it gives us memories, experience, and the chance to understand the true meaning of life.

So let us not measure time simply by the years that pass. Let us value our time not by how long we live, but by how we live. Let us tell our loved ones today that we love them. Let us not put off meeting our friends. Let us look at beautiful landscapes, watch the sunrise, listen to the sea, and savor the moments that bring joy to our hearts.

For time passes...

Let us not put off expressing our love or making time for our loved ones. A moment that has passed does not return; we must appreciate today.

Etibar Siracsoy

Life after sixty

Audio
Image
Text

Turning sixty is not a signal to step aside. It may be a chance to make room for wishes you have postponed for years and to give yourself more care.

Let yourself enjoy some of what you have worked for. Keep future needs in mind, but make room for comfort, pleasure, and things you have long wanted to do. You need not deny yourself everything simply to leave everything to others.

Keep loving your children and grandchildren and helping where you can. But you do not have to carry every worry for them. Give your adult children room to make decisions, take responsibility, and find their own way. Spending time and resources on yourself does not make you less caring.

Pay attention to your body. Move in ways that suit your health and abilities, take walks, and rest. Discuss your health needs and appropriate check-ups with your doctor. Avoid pushing yourself unnecessarily, but do not let age alone persuade you to give up activities you enjoy.

Look after your appearance for your own sake. Wear clean, comfortable clothes that feel like you. Being older does not mean giving up your sense of style or the colors you love.

Keep your curiosity alive. Read, listen to music, and learn something new. Use technology, if you wish, to stay close to people and discover more of the world. You do not have to follow every new trend; choose what is useful or enjoyable to you.

Listen to younger people. Their lives need not follow the pattern of yours. Share your experience, but leave room for questions before offering advice. They may have as much to teach you as you have to teach them.

When you say, “In my day…,” remember: this is your day too. Your memories may belong to the past, but your life is happening now.

Nurture relationships with people who respect you and let you be yourself. Care about others’ troubles, while setting limits with people who repeatedly belittle or exhaust you.

Make time for what you enjoy. Garden, cook, write, dance, or travel—whatever your circumstances and interests allow. Even a day without grand plans can hold small pleasures.

Go out when you can. Meet a friend, sit in a park, or visit an exhibition. If getting out is difficult, a phone call or a cup of tea with someone at home can also bring connection. Stay involved in life in ways that work for you.

Try to ease the weight of old hurts. Apologize when you have hurt someone. Being ready to forgive may take time, so do not rush yourself. Forgiveness does not have to mean forgetting what happened or returning to a hurtful relationship.

Talk when you are sad. Ask for help when you need it. You do not owe anyone constant strength or a permanent smile.

Love, laugh, stay curious, and make plans. Give yourself permission not only to rest, but also to begin again. Live this part of your life according to your values and circumstances, rather than other people’s expectations.

Caring for yourself does not require loving others less. At every age, your time, comfort, and hopes matter. This is still your life.

What Changed After Sixty

Audio
Image
Text

I asked a friend who was nearing seventy what changes he had noticed in himself as he grew older. This was his answer:

I no longer tell older people, “You’ve already told me that story.” I let them tell it. Sometimes sharing a memory allows them to relive a beautiful moment.

I no longer try to correct every mistake. It is not my job to make everyone perfect. Peace of mind is often worth more than having everything just right.

I am generous with kind words. A sincere compliment lifts the other person’s spirits—and mine too.

I no longer fret over a small crease or a spot on my clothes. Being presentable matters, but there is more to a person’s worth than appearance.

I have learned to step back from relationships that leave me emotionally drained. I do not have to be close to everyone. I treasure relationships built on mutual respect and understanding.

I am no longer ashamed of my feelings. Joy, tenderness and longing are all part of being human.

I have learned to set needless pride aside for the sake of a relationship. Being right every time does not make you happy. Sometimes reaching out matters more.

I make time for things that bring me joy. I have come to understand that my own happiness needs care too.

Before helping, I ask whether my help is needed and wanted. Good intentions do not excuse me from respecting another person’s choices.

I no longer complain endlessly about my health, my neighbors, my relatives or retirement. I do not ignore life’s difficulties, but I am also grateful to be alive and to have people I love beside me.

I am slower to tell young people, “When I was your age…” as though my life should be their model. Nor do I say, “I gave you the best years of my life,” to make someone feel indebted to me. Every generation faces different circumstances. The present cannot be judged solely by the standards of the past.

I give my wife more attention. I remember how young, strong and full of joy she once was. If the years have tired her and she needs more care now, I try to be there for her. I cherish the life we have shared.

I have stopped blaming myself for everything. I have made mistakes, but I have also done my best for myself and my children. I learn from the past instead of using it to punish myself.

And finally, I have learned to ask God for strength to change what I can, patience to accept what I cannot, and wisdom to tell the difference. I accept that I am human and cannot do everything.

Growing older can teach us that peace matters more than perfection, and understanding more than always being right. Life becomes richer when we learn to treat ourselves and others with compassion.
