Albrecht Dürer

Audio
Image
Text

In a small village near Nuremberg lived a family with ten children. Their father worked eighteen hours a day to support the household. Despite their hardship, the two eldest sons, Albrecht and Albert, dreamed of becoming renowned artists. They knew their father could not afford to educate them both at once.

After long discussions by candlelight, the brothers decided to draw lots. The winner would study at the academy in Nuremberg, while the other worked in a nearby mine to support him. After four years, they would exchange places.

Albrecht won the draw. He left for the academy, and Albert began working in the mine.

For four years, one brother studied art while the other labored underground. Albrecht’s work soon attracted wide attention; some said that it surpassed the work of many of his teachers.

By the time he completed his studies, Albrecht was earning a good income. He returned home joyfully and told his brother:

“Dear brother, now it is your turn. Go and pursue your dream; this time I will support you.”

Albert held out his hands and answered softly:

“No, brother. It is too late. During these four years, the mine has crushed my hands and every finger. I can no longer draw.”

Albrecht took his brother’s hands in his own and wept.

Centuries later, the works of Albrecht Dürer (1471–1528) continue to be displayed in leading galleries around the world. The famous drawing associated with his brother’s sacrifice is known as Praying Hands.

Moral: Behind success there often stands an unseen sacrifice. To recognize and honor that sacrifice is one of the finest expressions of humanity.

Sinan Brought Water to Istanbul but Died in a House Without It

Audio
Image
Text

During the reign of Suleiman the Magnificent, rapidly growing Istanbul suffered a severe water shortage. The solution became associated with the great architect Koca Sinan (1489–1588).

One day, the sultan summoned him and asked:

“Chief architect, people pay dearly even for a packhorse’s load of water. Can Istanbul be supplied with enough water?”

Sinan requested permission to survey the surrounding country. With his assistants, he examined wells, valleys, and streams from Çekmece to Beşiktaş and calculated where dams and aqueducts could be built.

When he returned, he told the sultan:

“It can be done, but at a heavy price: sacks filled with gold would have to be placed end to end.”

The sultan replied:

“Tell me only that it is possible. If necessary, I will place the sacks not end to end, but side by side.”

Sinan began work and created a magnificent system culminating in forty public fountains across the city. Taps were fitted so that the precious water would not be wasted.

The sultan declared the public fountains the property of the people and prohibited secret pipes into private homes. In recognition of Sinan’s service, however, he verbally allowed a private line to the architect’s house.

Years passed. Suleiman and those who knew Sinan’s service firsthand died. One day, the elderly architect was summoned to the palace, where the council was investigating a complaint about the private water in his home.

Sinan explained that the sultan himself had granted permission. When asked for a written decree, he admitted:

“At the time, I was ashamed to ask the Ruler of the World for a document. I have no written order.”

After a long debate, the council decided not to punish Sinan for his past use of the water, but ordered the private line cut off.

Near the end of his life, Sinan fell gravely ill. When those beside him tried to moisten his lips, the tap was dry. The great architect said:

“We did not serve for profit, but for God’s pleasure. We await our reward in the hereafter and do not grieve that this world has denied water to our home.”

Moral: Do not place excessive trust in fame, worldly privilege, or human remembrance. True service is rendered not for personal gain, but for what one knows to be right.
