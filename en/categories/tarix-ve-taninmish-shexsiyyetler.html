A Remarkable Lesson

Audio
Image
Text

A tale is told about the Ottoman sultan Murad II and his son Mehmed.

As a child, the future Mehmed the Conqueror would fidget and misbehave during lessons. Whenever his teacher, Akshamsaddin, reprimanded him, he would reply:

“You can’t tell me what to do. I’m the sultan’s son!”

Akshamsaddin was troubled by the prince’s behavior. At first, he hesitated to complain to the sultan, but eventually he gathered his courage, requested an audience, and explained the situation.

Murad thought for a moment, then whispered a plan into the teacher’s ear. Akshamsaddin protested:

“Your Majesty, I couldn’t possibly do that!”

But the sultan stood firm.

“We must do this so that my son learns.”

The next day, the prince disrupted the lesson again. When his teacher warned him, he gave his usual reply:

“I’m the sultan’s son!”

At that moment, the door opened and Murad II entered the classroom.

As they had agreed, Akshamsaddin pretended to be angry. He scolded the sultan for entering without permission, even slapped him, and ordered him to leave.

The sultan lowered his head, apologized, and stepped outside.

Mehmed was astonished. His father, before whom everyone bowed, had accepted the teacher’s reprimand and obeyed the classroom rules.

A little later, there was a knock at the door. This time, Murad asked permission to enter and apologized again.

According to the tale, the prince’s behavior changed from that day on. Without a lengthy lecture, his father had shown him that being the sultan’s son did not give him the right to treat others disrespectfully.

Moral: In raising a child, example speaks louder than a lengthy lecture. A parent’s authority comes not from placing themselves above the rules, but from showing the respect and responsibility they expect of their child.

A legend about Albrecht Dürer

The story goes that two brothers, Albrecht and Albert, grew up in a poor family with many children near Nuremberg. Both dreamed of becoming painters. Their father worked from morning until night, but could not afford to pay for their studies.

Night after night, the brothers talked by candlelight, searching for a way to pursue their dreams. Finally, they decided to draw lots. One would study painting; the other would work in a mine to pay for his brother’s education. After four years, they would exchange roles.

Albrecht won the draw.

He left to study art, while Albert began working in the mine. One learned to handle a brush and explored the world of color. The other descended underground each day, working hard so his brother could continue his studies.

Four years passed. Albrecht’s talent attracted attention, and his work began to bring him recognition and an income. When he returned home, he embraced his brother and said joyfully:
“Now it’s your turn, Albert. Go and study. Follow your dream. You supported me; now I’ll support you.”

But Albert slowly shook his head.
“No, brother… It’s too late for me.”

He held out his hands. His palms were calloused, his fingers bent from years of heavy labor.
“These hands can no longer hold a brush the way they once could.”

Albrecht gently took his brother’s hands in his own. He wanted to speak, but no words came. His tears fell onto Albert’s work-worn hands.

Only then did he fully understand what the fulfillment of his dream had cost his brother.

The legend connects Dürer’s famous drawing Praying Hands with this sacrifice. In the story, those hands symbolize brotherly love, gratitude, and the quiet labor behind another person’s success.

Moral: Our achievements also belong, in part, to those who have generously given us their time, strength, and support. True gratitude means recognizing their sacrifices, remembering them, and returning their care with care of our own.

Mihrimah and Sinan

Audio
Image
Text

A beautiful legend links two mosques in Istanbul: the story of Mihrimah Sultan and the architect Sinan.

Mihrimah, the daughter of Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent, bore a name of Persian origin: mihr means “sun,” and mah means “moon.”

According to the legend, when Mihrimah was seventeen, two men wished to marry her: Rüstem Pasha and Sinan. She married Rüstem Pasha in 1539.

The tale says that Sinan, already married and approaching fifty, kept his love to himself. Unable to share his life with Mihrimah, he gave his feelings expression through his art.

Sinan first built a mosque bearing her name in Üsküdar, then another in Edirnekapı. Both buildings are indeed his work; the idea that they express a secret love belongs to the legend.

Those who tell the story see a woman in flowing skirts in the silhouette of the Üsküdar mosque. In the slender minaret, luminous interior, and delicate ornamentation of the Edirnekapı mosque, they look for echoes of Mihrimah’s beauty. Some even liken its decorative patterns to her long hair.

The most memorable part of the story is the meeting of the Sun and Moon.

It is said that from a certain spot in Istanbul, the Sun can be seen setting behind the Edirnekapı mosque while the Moon rises behind the mosque in Üsküdar. The legend associates this scene with the spring equinox and presents it as Sinan’s silent declaration of love.

Mihrimah—the Sun and Moon…

Did Sinan deliberately plan this view, or did people later give his work this romantic meaning? We cannot present that interpretation as established history.

Yet the legend lives on. Looking at the two mosques, people see more than stone, domes, and minarets. Sometimes they also search for the trace of a feeling never put into words.

Moral: Not every love leads to a life together. When people channel their feelings into creativity, those feelings can live on in works that bring beauty to others.

A legend about Mimar Sinan

During the reign of Suleiman the Magnificent, Istanbul was growing rapidly, and its water shortage was becoming increasingly severe. According to legend, the sultan summoned the architect Sinan and asked:
“People pay dearly for water and suffer great hardship. Is there a way to free Istanbul from this shortage?”

Sinan asked for time to survey the surrounding countryside. With his assistants, he examined springs, riverbeds, and valleys, searching for ways to bring water into the city.

Then he returned to the sultan.
“It can be done,” he said. “But it will require a great deal of work and money.”

“As long as it is possible,” the sultan replied, “the money will be provided.”

Sinan set to work. In time, water flowed through the channels and aqueducts he built to the city’s public fountains. Water that had once been so difficult to obtain now supplied people’s everyday needs.

The sultan ordered that the public water supply be protected and forbade private connections without permission. But he made an exception for Sinan: in recognition of his service, he gave him verbal permission to bring water directly to his home.

Years passed. Suleiman died, and fewer and fewer people remained who remembered his permission.

One day, the elderly architect was summoned to the palace. A complaint had been brought before the imperial council: why did Sinan have a private water connection when others were forbidden one?

“Sultan Suleiman gave me permission,” Sinan explained.

“Do you have a written decree?”

“No,” he answered quietly. “I trusted the sultan’s word. I hesitated to ask him for written confirmation as well.”

After much discussion, the council decided that Sinan would not be punished for his past use of the water, but the supply to his home would be cut off.

According to the legend, Sinan fell gravely ill near the end of his life. He asked for water to moisten his dry lips, but nothing flowed from the tap in his home.

Then the architect who had brought water to an entire city said softly:
“We did this work not for our own benefit, but to please God. It is from Him that we hope for our reward. Though there is no water in our home, our work still serves the people.”

Moral: People’s gratitude may fade, but that does not diminish the value of a good deed. Serving others without seeking personal gain is a mark of character; remembering those who have served us is a responsibility we share.

Three Essentials for a City

Audio
Image
Text

Every city needs three things: a sewer system, a bathhouse, and a library. The sewer system cleanses the city, the bathhouse cleanses the body, and the library cleanses the soul.

— Attributed to Sultan Mehmed the Conqueror

Moral: Our inner lives deserve as much care as the cleanliness of our cities and bodies. Books bring clarity to our thinking and help us understand ourselves and the world more deeply.
