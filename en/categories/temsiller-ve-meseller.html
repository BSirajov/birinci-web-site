A Fly in a China Shop

Image
Text

A fly could never destroy a china shop by its own strength. But if it flew into a bull’s ear, the frightened animal might panic and wreck the entire shop.

The fly represents superstition, falsehood, and prejudice. The bull represents an ignorant person who acts without thinking.

Moral: A small lie whispered into an ignorant ear can cause enormous destruction. Knowledge, critical thinking, and strong moral values are our best protection against it.

Elephant and Rope

Image
Text

A traveler passes by an elephant enclosure and is surprised to see that these giant animals are tied to a stake by a front leg with just a short rope. There is no chain or cage. It would seem natural that the elephants could easily break the rope at any moment and escape, but they did not seem to be trying to escape. The man approaches a nearby trainer and asks why these magnificent animals stand there obediently and do not try to run away.

The trainer explains:

— When elephants are young, a short rope is enough to tie them and hold them. As they grow, these animals gradually begin to believe that they cannot break the rope and escape. They think that this rope will still hold them and will not let them go free.

The man is amazed to hear this. Imagine: these animals remained bound to the spot where they stood, believing freedom was impossible, even though they could break free at any moment.

Moral: Often it is not the rope that binds us, but the distrust born of past failures. It is impossible to be free without overcoming the fear of “what will people say.”

The donkey's law

Image
Text

One day, a donkey becomes the king of the forest. A few days later, he makes a law: everyone must go to bed at six in the evening and wake up at six in the morning. For twelve hours, there must be complete silence in the forest, and anyone who breaks the law must be punished.

In this way, for a while, the forest is quiet.

One day, a rooster wakes up at five in the morning and begins to crow. The world in the forest clashes with each other. A court is immediately set up and the rooster is executed by the donkey's verdict. After that day, no one touches the law for fear of the king.

Years pass, the donkey grows old and weak. He retreats to a corner of the forest and awaits his death. The lion comes to him, takes stock of his condition, and then asks:

— You are already living your last days. We want to know: what was the wisdom of that law that ordered you to go to bed at six in the evening and get up at six in the morning? How many times have we tried to revolt and abolish it, but we held back because it would disrupt order in the forest. We said to ourselves that perhaps there is some wisdom in this law that we do not understand.

The donkey smiled and replied:

— Indeed, I myself have been wondering about this for years: why did the wild animals of the forest obey this law that I derived from my donkeyness like sheep?

Moral: What keeps a meaningless law standing is not the power of the one who gave it, but the silence of those who obey. Behind an order there is not always wisdom — sometimes only donkeyness — so do not hesitate to ask, for an unanswered question can turn even the oldest nonsense into law.

Text

Until the lion learns to write, every storyteller will write the hunter's tale.

African Proverb

Moral: He who tells the story becomes the hero. If you don't learn to write your own truth, you will always be told by others.

The Lion's Track

Image
Text

A cowardly, yet arrogant hunter, following a lion, reaches a forest and asks the woodcutter who comes across him:

— Brother, have you come across a lion or its track around here?

Woodcutter:

— I saw the lion himself; if you want, come and take you to where he is.

The pale hunter:

— No, my dear, thank you. I was actually looking for the lion's track, not the lion itself.

Moral: A person is known not by his pretensions, but by his behavior in the moment of trial. Many people "look for a lion" from afar, but when they come face to face, most of them retreat.

The tortoise's wrong calculation

Image
Text

They ask the tortoise:

— How long will it take you to reach this nearby village?

— Taking into account the rain, mud, wind, and ups and downs, it's a three-day journey, but I'll make it in six days... — the tortoise replies.

After six days, they look and see that the tortoise has not yet reached the village. They search and finally find him halfway along the way: — Well, you couldn't make it three days' journey in six days? — they ask in surprise.

The tortoise replies in disappointment: — Never mind, friends. I calculated the rain, mud, wind, and ups and downs, but unfortunately, it didn't occur to me to calculate the bad people. As soon as I started to move, they caught me and turned me upside down.

Moral: You can calculate every difficulty in life, but not people with bad intentions. May God not let you face those who prevent you from moving forward, but those who support you.

Two donkeys

Image
Text

Two donkeys are walking along a path. They see a monkey sitting in a tree. They say, let's tease this monkey: let's ask him if he has a friend or not? If he says "Yes", we say "Which fool is your friend?"; if he says "No", we say "Which fool would be your friend?" They approach the tree and ask: "Monkey, do you have a friend?" The monkey looks down at the donkeys who want to tease him and says:

— Wherever I look, they are all donkeys; who should I be friends with?

Moral: A trap set to humiliate someone often explodes on the head of the one who sets the trap. The one who wants to tease himself becomes ridiculous.

Water-carrying ant

Image
Text

According to legend, Nimrod (a Biblical character, the ruler of the land of Shinar) wants to punish the prophet Abraham, whom he considers an obstacle to his cruelty, by throwing him into the fire. He builds a huge bonfire in the middle of the square. All living things that see the bonfire run away.

Meanwhile, an ant with a drop of water in its mouth starts running towards the bonfire in a hurry.

Seeing it, another ant asks:

— Where are you running in such a hurry?

— Haven't you heard that Nimrod is going to throw the prophet Abraham into the fire? — the water-carrying ant does not hide his concern.

— You probably haven't seen that bonfire. It's a huge bonfire, there's no other way but to run. Will you put out that bonfire with this drop of water in your mouth? — the second ant asks mockingly.

The water-carrying ant replies:

— Even if I can't put it out, at least it will be clear whose side I'm on.

Moral: Even if you can't, at least stand by the truth. Being there for your loved one when they're in trouble is a value in itself.
