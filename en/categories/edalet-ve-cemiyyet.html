A bell to hang from the cat's neck

Image
Text

A group of mice gathered together and looked for ways to get rid of a cat that had become a nuisance to them.

Many ideas were put forward, but none were accepted. Finally, a young mouse suggested hanging a bell from the cat's neck.

The essence of the plan was this: as the cat with the bell hanging from its neck approached the mice, they would hear the bell and run away, thus saving their lives. This proposal was met with great enthusiasm and applause by all the mice.

At this moment, the old mouse, who was silently watching what was happening in a corner, spoke up:

— This is a very clever proposal, and if it were implemented, I have no doubt that it would be successful. But one question makes me think: well, tell me, who would hang the bell from the cat's neck?

Moral: It is easy to make a plan, but difficult to carry it out. Even the brightest idea that is not implemented is worthless.

A woman whose house was robbed

Image
Text

In ancient times, the house of an old woman living in a city was robbed. The woman came to the king in a state of dismay to complain. The king listened to her and angrily asked: “Then why did you fall into such a deep sleep that you did not even notice that a thief had entered your house?” The woman replied: “Thank God, king, we thought you were awake, so we slept peacefully in our house.”

Moral: The peace of the people comes from the trust they have in the state. If the people know that their leader is awake, they will sleep peacefully in their own homes.

Nazim Hikmet in Bursa Prison

Image
Text

Turkish poet Nazim Hikmet was serving a sentence in Bursa Prison. One day, an inspector from the Ministry of Justice came to inspect the prison. He asked the prison chief to call Nazim Hikmet — he wanted to meet with him.

Nazim was called. He entered the room... They had a short conversation. But the ministry employee did not offer the poet a seat. After the conversation, he said: "You can go." Nazim Hikmet turned around and asked the inspector as he was leaving the door:

— Do you know Omar Khayyam?

— Of course. Is it okay not to know him?

— And can you tell me who the king was during Omar Khayyam's time?

The inspector was surprised by this unexpected question and could not answer.

Nazim Hikmet said:

— You see, you remembered the artist, but not the king. The time will come when they will neither remember the Minister of Justice nor you. But they will always remember my name.

Saying this, he leaves. The inspector realizes his mistake. Even though he calls him, the poet does not return...

Moral: As time passes, those in positions of authority are forgotten, but those who create true value are remembered. Positions are temporary, deeds are permanent.

Sharing justice

Image
Text

Either no one should cry,

or these always laughing people should not laugh;

or no one should have this disease called money,

or the moneyed nation should learn to share;

or no one should say what they love,

or they should give the definition of this true love;

or these holidays should not be experienced at all,

or there should be no one who does not eat meat during the holidays.

Moral: Humanity is created when joy, bread, and love are shared. If one side laughs and the other side cries, there is no justice there.

The just verdict of the Frankfurt judge

Image
Text

This incident was recorded in the city of Frankfurt in 1506. A merchant lost his bag with 800 guilders on the road. By chance, a carpenter passing by found the bag. The carpenter, who was extremely religious, did not tell anyone that he had found the bag, thinking that it was impossible not to notice that so much money was lost, so the owner would definitely look for it.

One day, the carpenter went to church to worship. After the service, the monk announced to the congregation that a merchant who had come to Frankfurt had lost his 800 guilders and promised a reward of 100 guilders to whoever found it.

Hearing this, the carpenter took the money he had found, went to church, and handed the bag over to the monk.

The merchant was informed that his money had been found; he went to church and took the bag, but refused to pay the carpenter the 100 guilders he had promised—he gave him only 5 guilders. The carpenter asks the merchant to keep his promise. The greedy merchant claims that he has 900 guilders in his wallet, not 800, in order to avoid giving him 100 guilders, and that the carpenter took money from the bag. The monk intervenes in the argument to defend the carpenter: he says that he knows him, that he is an honest man, and that he would never do such a thing. The argument becomes heated. The monk takes the merchant and the carpenter to the city court.

The trial begins. The judge asks the merchant to swear by placing his hand on the Bible that he has lost his 900 guilders. The merchant, without hesitation, places his hand on the Bible and swears. The judge also asks the carpenter to swear by finding the 800 guilders. The carpenter also places his hand on the Bible and swears.

Everyone is impatiently waiting for the judge's decision. The judge says that everything is clear as day and explains his decision as follows:

— The carpenter found 800 guilders, and the merchant lost 900 guilders. Therefore, the money that the carpenter found does not belong to the merchant. Therefore, since the owner has not been found, these 800 guilders are given to the carpenter. The merchant can continue to search for the 900 guilders he lost.

Moral: Greed traps a person. The stingy person who denies the truth eventually becomes a victim of his own lies; justice is on the side of the righteous.

The Skins of the Lambs

Image
Text

“It is better to shear the wool of rams than to skin lambs.”

This saying is associated with Azmzadeh Asad Pasha, who was the governor of Damascus province during the Ottoman period. The story goes like this:

One day, Asad Pasha’s treasury was empty and he was in great distress. His advisors advised him to impose an additional tax on the weavers of Damascus as a way to fill the treasury. Asad Pasha, who listened to the advice, asked: “How much income would we get if we imposed such a tax?”

“We will buy fifty or sixty bags of gold,” they said.

Asad Pasha objected: “These people are already struggling to make ends meet. How will they pay this tax?” The advisors said: “They will sell the gold and jewels in their houses, Pasha,” and tried to persuade him.

Asad Pasha says: “I will get this amount in a better way.”

The advisors are silent.

The next day, the Pasha sends an invitation to the Mufti and offers to meet secretly at night. At night, the Mufti comes to visit the Pasha. The Pasha says:

— Mufti Effendi! According to the information we have received, you are acting contrary to the Sharia in your personal life, drinking alcohol secretly at home. I must report this situation to Istanbul. However, I thought I would inform you in advance.

Hearing this, the Mufti Effendi begins to beg the Pasha. In order not to inform Istanbul, he offers him a thousand mecidiyes. The Pasha does not accept. The Mufti offers twice as much. The Pasha does not accept again. In the end, they agree to six thousand mecidiyes.

The next day, Asad Pasha invites the Ghazi and tells him: “Gazi Effendi, we have learned from reliable sources that you are taking bribes and using your position for your personal gain.”

This time, the Ghazi Effendi begins to beg the Pasha. He, like the mufti, bargains with Asad Pasha: “Oh, my lord, do not remove me from my post, or I will be disgraced among the people.” They also agree to six thousand mejidiye gazi.

Then Asad Pasha invites the treasurer, the chief of security, the merchants and the wealthy to a meeting one by one.

At the end of this operation, Asad Pasha collects exactly two hundred bags of mejidiye gold. Then he calls his advisors and asks: “Have you heard that I imposed a tax on the people of Damascus?” “No, we have not heard,” they say.

The Pasha says: “Despite not imposing a tax, I have collected not fifty, but two hundred bags of mejidiye gold.”

— How did you do this, Pasha?

The Pasha replies: “It is better to shear the wool of rams than to skin lambs.”

Moral: It is more just to take the unjust profit of the wealthy than to rob the poor. The burden should be placed on the strong, not the poor.

The story of the Bedouin whose camel was stolen

Image
Text

A Bedouin walking in the desert with his camel came across a man who was barely standing, his lips parched with thirst. The man begged the Bedouin for water to drink. The Bedouin got off his camel and gave him water. The man drank the water, then suddenly pushed the Bedouin away, mounted the camel, and started to run away.

When the Bedouin realized that he could do nothing to save his camel, he shouted after the thief:

— Take the camel, it is yours. But never tell anyone about this story.

Surprised by the Bedouin's strange request, the thief stopped for a moment, looked back, and asked why.

— If you tell, this story will spread everywhere, and no one will ever help the needy people he meets again, — the Bedouin replied.

Moral: Sometimes hiding your own harm is to protect the faith of others in the goodness of others. One bad news can close the door to thousands of good things.

The world can be conquered with this nation

Image
Text

One day, the king changed his clothes and went to the city to observe how the people lived. He began to inspect the city markets to learn about the quality of the goods, their prices, and the mood of the shopkeepers.

The king entered the first shop at the beginning of the street. He greeted and said:

— Give me half a batman of butter, half a batman of honey, and some cheese.

The shopkeeper, who greeted the customer with a smile, politely accepted the greeting and gladly took half a batman of butter. While giving the butter, he addressed the man in front of him, not knowing that he was the king, as follows:

— My lord, if you wish, I can also give you honey and cheese. However, by selling this butter, I have already made a siptah — the first sale of the day. If you buy your other requests from my neighbor across the street, who has not yet made a siptah, I will be very pleased.

The king, who was very happy with this, paid for the butter and went to the shop opposite. From there, he also asked for half a batman of honey and cheese. After the old owner of the shop had tasted the honey:

— Thank God, thanks to you we have also tasted it today. But I ask you this: do not buy the cheese from me, but from the shop next door. It has not tasted it yet, — he said.

The king, who was very impressed by what he saw, went to the shop next door and made his purchase, and said:

— With such high morality in this nation, the whole world can be conquered, — he said and left the market happy and satisfied.

Moral: Real power does not lie in weapons, but in the honesty and halalness of the people. Not buying land, but conquering hearts is the real victory.

Why injustice when there is justice?

Image
Text

Haji Bektaş Veli:

Why hatred when there is love?

Why war when there is peace?

Why quarrel when there is brotherhood?

Why enmity when there is friendship?

Why intolerance when there is tolerance?

Why captivity when there is freedom?

Why injustice when there is justice?

Moral: It is man's own fault to choose hatred, strife and injustice when love, peace, brotherhood and justice are possible.
