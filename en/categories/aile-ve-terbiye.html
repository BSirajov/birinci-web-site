A Mother’s Love

Audio
Image
Text

Erich Fromm

I am one year old—completely happy, because my mother is always beside me.

I am two—my mother is with me only sometimes. I wonder what is happening.

I am three—my mother is at work, and my nanny is with me.

I am five—my mother is in a meeting, and my grandmother is with me.

I am seven—my mother is at a seminar; I am at school with my teacher.

I am thirteen—a teenager. My mother still has to work, and my friends are beside me.

I am seventeen—my mother comes home exhausted. I am at school, in class, or at a café with friends.

I am twenty—young and confident. My mother is not at home, but my friends are with me.

I am twenty-three—I have started working. My mother is home now, while I am at work.

I am thirty—and I wonder: when did my mother and I last spend real time together? Something is wrong.

I am thirty-five—married. My mother lives in her home, and I in mine.

I am forty—my mother waits for me just as I once waited for her as a child.

I am fifty—I am older now, but one wish remains unchanged: to hold my mother close and rest my head in her lap.

I am fifty-five—another mother is always beside me now, the mother of my children. My own mother has passed away, and I live with the ache of missing her.

In the rush of ordinary life, one of our gravest failures is not giving enough time to our children and those we love. Children are the most precious characters in the extraordinary film of life unfolding before us. If we fail to notice them growing, those passing moments can never be recovered.

Life has no pause or rewind button. We cannot replay the moments that have passed. May we understand that while there is still time.

Love your child today. Hold them, take their hand, and remain beside them. Do not miss today’s episode, because tomorrow a new scene of childhood will already have begun.

“A mother’s love is a selfless, protective, and warm refuge. Precisely because it is unconditional, it cannot be obtained by force.”

Moral: A mother’s love is unconditional and irreplaceable. Make time for those you love today, because time is the most precious thing we can never recover.

The Advice Fakir Baykurt Never Forgot

Audio
Image
Text

As a child, the Turkish writer Fakir Baykurt (1929–1999) once asked his mother for tea. She took him to Topal Hüseyin’s teahouse and ordered a cup for him.

Eager to drink, he took a large mouthful before realizing how hot it was. Startled by the pain, he dropped the cup.

It fell to the ground without breaking. He expected his mother to slap him, but she simply asked for another cup.

This time, he sipped carefully. For years afterward, he asked why she had not punished him. She never answered.

Much later, his mother, Elif Khanum, visited the village school where he taught. He recalled:

“I became angry during the lesson and shouted at the children. Afterward, I asked, ‘Well, Mother, what did you think of my teaching?’

“‘It wasn’t bad, son.’

“‘The inspectors praise me and give me good marks, and all you say is that it wasn’t bad?’

“She answered, ‘For years you’ve asked why I didn’t slap you over the tea. Now listen.

“‘If I had shouted, the lion inside you would have roared back. If I had beaten you, I might have killed that lion. You might never have become a teacher. Be patient, as I was. Don’t use a stick to kill the courage in these children.’”

Moral: Don't kill the "lion" inside a child — his self-confidence — by beating him. True education is not given with fear, but with patience and love.

Father and Son

Audio
Image
Text

After a bitter argument with his father, a young man left home. Hurt and angry, his father forbade anyone to mention his name. Each night, his mother sat on his bed and fell asleep holding his pillow close, breathing in its familiar scent.

“I miss our son,” she pleaded. “Let’s find him and bring him home.” But her husband refused.

A long time passed.

One day, unable to bear seeing his wife in tears, the father took a slip of paper from his pocket and handed it to her.

“You’ll find him at this address,” he said. “But don’t tell him I gave it to you.”

He said a few more things, but she barely heard them. Overjoyed, she hurried out. The address led to a repair shop, where she found her son working in overalls. For a moment she watched him through her tears, then walked toward him. He had heard nothing from his family and believed they had forgotten him. Startled to see her, he pulled her into a tight embrace.

He asked after everyone except his father. Finally, he said:

“How is that man? Is he still angry?”

His mother avoided the question.

“Come home, son,” she said.

“No, Mother. I can’t live under the same roof as him again.” He turned back to his work.

She watched him for a while, then called him over to give him the sweets she had brought.

He accepted them, but said:

“Please don’t insist. I’m not coming home. He hasn’t looked for me once. He doesn’t care about me.”

His mother lowered her head.

“Well, if you’re determined to stay, that’s your choice. But your father asked me to tell you to keep away from the friend you’ve been spending time with this past month. He says that person is no good for you, and that you should make peace with your old friend.”

Her son froze in astonishment.

Back at home, the mother confronted her husband.

“If you knew where he was, why didn’t you tell me? Is that why you seemed so calm?”

The father broke down in tears.

“He’s my own flesh and blood,” he said. “My whole heart.”

“How long have you known?” she asked.

“Since the day he left. Sometimes I went during my lunch break to watch from a distance and make sure he was eating properly. That’s why I came home late some evenings too. I went to see what he was doing and who he was with.”

As the parents held each other and cried, someone knocked. The mother wiped her eyes and opened the door.

Their son stood there. He kissed their hands and embraced them. Now he understood: the father he thought had cast him out of his heart had never stopped watching over him.

Moral: Don't hide your love, show it. Sometimes a timely step can prevent years of resentment.

A Wise Father’s Advice to His Son

Audio
Image
Text

My beloved son,

Cultivate the understanding that helps you build good relationships. It is an inexhaustible source of happiness and joy.

Our desire for life is never fully satisfied. Whether a life is long or short does not determine its worth.

Fruit is ready to fall only when it is ripe. In the same way, people have always valued the wisdom that comes with experience.

In youth, people often do wrong without thinking. Later, the memory of those wrongs can pierce the heart like a dagger.

Those who follow an honest path in youth earn respect in old age.

Strength is a good thing, but it must be guided by reason.

Allah, the Creator of all worlds, did not place us here to live forever. Every life must come to an end.

Through many trials, Allah has taught me two habits that have helped preserve my health: eating modestly and walking after meals.

I also want you to understand that there are three kinds of people.

The first think clearly and can look ahead to the consequences of their actions.

The second lose their way through the influence of others, but are willing to listen when someone offers guidance.

The third know little, yet believe they know everything and refuse all advice. They are the most unfortunate of all.

My son,

If you belong to the first group, I will be glad.

If you belong to the second, listen to good advice.

Do not become one of the third. Such people earn favor neither with Allah nor with others.

Moral: Wisdom is the source of happiness, and strength is the servant of wisdom. He who listens to advice will be exalted; but he who thinks he knows everything will perish.

Text

As a father and his young daughter crossed a bridge, he grew anxious.

“Hold my hand tightly, sweetheart, so you don’t fall into the river.”

The little girl replies:

“No, Dad. You hold mine.”

Puzzled, he asked:

“What’s the difference?”

“There’s a big difference. If something frightens me, I might let go of your hand. But if you hold mine, I know you won’t let go, whatever happens.”

Moral: Love offers a steady hand. Do not simply wait for those you love to reach for you; reach out and hold on to them.

Text

“I’m tired, Mom.”

“Of what, son?”

“Everything. Work, friends, traveling, always trying to please someone. I’m exhausted.”

“Rest your head in my lap, son. Let it all go for a moment. Share your troubles with me.”

“Thank you, Mom. I’m so glad you’re here. With you, I forget my age and all my worries. Please always be here.”

“I’m your mother, son. I’ll always be here for you.”

“We wear you out too, don’t we? Forgive us.”

“You don’t wear me out. I’m tired because I no longer have a mother of my own. There’s no lap where I can rest my head. Treasure what you have, son.”

Moral: A mother’s lap can be our safest refuge. Remember that she, too, may long for someone to comfort her.

Mother and Son

Audio
Image
Text

The man set the suitcases against the wall, straightened up, and took a deep breath. “It’s a lovely place, Mom.”

“Of course it is, son,” she replied. For a while they stood silently, looking around the room.

Then the woman opened her small suitcase and took out her nightgown, slippers, a few towels, and a framed picture. She placed the picture on the table next to her bed.

In the photograph, a small boy in school uniform stood stiffly, his eyes brimming with tears and his lips pressed together.

The man approached the window and opened the curtain. A pale daylight filled the room. It was freezing outside. He leaned his hands on the heater and watched the muddy cars passing by for a while. Then, without even turning his head, he asked, “Is this closet enough for you, Mom?”

She did not hear him. Seated on the bed, she was gazing at the frightened boy in the worn frame.

It was a picture they had taken on the first day of school.

The memory returned. The day before school began, she had spent hours telling him how wonderful it would be. Even so, she had barely persuaded him to enter the classroom. She waited all day, reassuring him at every break: “I’m here, son. I’m waiting for you.” He clung to her hand. “Don’t leave me, Mom. I’m scared.”

The sun was setting. Her son moved his warm hands from the radiator to the cold window. “Shall we open the other suitcase and put your things away?”

“Don’t let me keep you. I can do it. There isn’t much.” She reached for his hand with trembling fingers. For a moment she seemed about to say more. Then she swallowed. “The children will be wondering where you are.”

The door closed. She was alone with the peeling paint and the photograph. After a while, she put on her slippers and went to the window. Below, her son was getting into his car.

The old woman was afraid. Despite the fact that she had listened for hours the day before to how beautiful this place was, she was still afraid.

His handprint still marked the glass. She pressed her cheek against it, her eyes filling and her lips trembling.

“Don't leave me, son, I'm so scared,” she whispered.

Her words seemed to melt into the misted glass and flow toward her son as he waved from the car.

That night, the other residents heard her crying. Sobs in the corridor were a familiar sound at the nursing home.

Moral: The child who once feared being left may one day leave a parent with the same fear. Caring for parents means attending to their need for love as well as their physical needs.

Parenting

Audio
Image
Text

A boy constantly defied his parents, neglected his schoolwork, and refused to do his homework. His mother’s blows and his father’s punishments changed nothing.

One day, on the way to school, his mother saw a crowd around a bear trainer. As the man beat a tambourine, a bear with a ring through its nose performed for the spectators.

“I can’t manage my own child,” she thought, “yet this man can train a bear.”

She approached him. “How do you make that wild animal obey? I can’t get my son to listen.”

“Everything has its method,” he said with a smile. “I have mine.”

“Then please help me.”

The trainer looked at the boy. “Bring him to me for a week. I’ll make him obedient.”

The woman was happy and started taking the boy to the bear trainer every day.

Within days, the boy became quiet and compliant. By the end of the week, his mother was delighted. She thanked the trainer and offered him her blessings.

The neighbors were very surprised by this. That disobedient, rebellious boy was gone, and in his place was a calm, peaceful, well-mannered boy.

His teacher welcomed the change too. Soon, however, a different problem emerged. Though the boy no longer openly rebelled, he stole classmates’ belongings, hid what he was doing, and lied. The teacher summoned his mother. Desperate, she went looking for the trainer, but he had left.

A neighbor suggested that she consult a wise, kindhearted man.

She took her son to him and explained. “Let him visit me for a week,” he said. “I’ll see what I can do.” She agreed.

She began bringing the boy to him.

After two meetings, the man spoke to her with concern. “What has happened to this child? He behaves like a frightened animal.” She explained that she had taken him to a bear trainer because he was so disobedient.

“An animal trainer?” he exclaimed. “You want to help a child develop a conscience, not frighten him into obedience. Beating, punishment, and confinement can produce fear, but fear is not an education.”

“Reach his heart,” he continued. “Be close to him. Hug him, listen to him, and let him know he is loved. When he feels safe with you, trust can begin to grow.”

“Neither the bear trainer nor I can take your place. He needs you, his parents.”

Moral: A child is educated not with fear, but with love. It is not a teacher or an outsider who educates a child—it is a loving parent.

Puppies for Sale

Audio
Image
Text

A little boy entered the shop after seeing a sign in the window saying “We have puppies for sale.”

“How much do you sell puppies for?” he asked.

“Between thirty and fifty dollars. Each one has a different price,” said the shopkeeper.

The boy emptied his pocket onto the counter and carefully counted the money.

“I only have thirteen dollars and twenty-seven cents,” he said, disappointed. Then he looked up.

“Can I at least see the puppies?”

The shopkeeper smiled and called to his wife. She came from the back room with five plump, fluffy puppies following her. One struggled to keep up. The boy pointed to it.

“What’s wrong with him? Why can’t he walk?” he asked.

“The vet says he was born with a malformed leg bone. Walking will always be difficult for him.”

The boy’s face lit up.

“That’s the puppy I want.”

“You don’t need to buy that one, son. You can have him for free.”

The little boy was very angry. He looked the shopkeeper straight in the eye and said:

“I don’t want him for free. He’s worth just as much as the others. I’ll give you what I have now, then bring five dollars every week until I’ve paid thirty.”

The shopkeeper said:

“But he won’t be able to run and play with you like the others.”

“He still deserves a chance.” The boy bent down and raised his trouser leg, revealing a leg weakened by polio and supported by a metal brace. He looked up and spoke softly.

“I can’t run and play either. We both need a friend who understands.”

Moral: Every living thing needs a friend who will understand and support it. The one who is different is just as valuable as the others; we need to give him a chance.

The Seed That Grew

Audio
Image
Text

After his wife died, a man sent his often-ill young son to an orphanage, dismissing him as hopeless.

Years passed.

Years later, he saw his son outside a school. The boy had grown healthy and strong. Filled with regret, the father stared at his bright eyes.

“I never thought you would recover like this. How did it happen?”

With tears in his eyes, the young man pointed to an older woman approaching them.

“When you sent me away, you said a rotten seed could never grow. She adopted me and never stopped caring for me. She told me, ‘Every seed watered with love can grow.’”

Moral: Words either build or destroy. It is possible to elevate or destroy someone with a single word—every seed watered with love will germinate.

Spinach

Audio
Image
Text

We lived in a remote village without even a proper road. My mother seemed obsessed with spinach. As a child, I sometimes wondered whether grief over my father’s death had unsettled her.

“You need to eat a lot, it gives strength to the body,” my mother would say, and for breakfast, lunch, and dinner, she would only feed us spinach, with a little cornbread on the side. I hated spinach already, I couldn’t stand to see this vegetable.

“When I’m grown and have a job and a family, there’ll be no spinach in my house,” I told her. She said she looked forward to that day and promised to dance at my wedding, however old she was.

The day came, I moved from our village to the city to study in high school. I somehow managed to make ends meet with the small scholarship I received. Once a month, when I came home, I didn't want to eat a spoonful of the spinach dish my mother put on the table. I only wanted to go back to the dormitory and eat delicious food.

I went on to university, then completed a master’s degree and a doctorate with government loans. By then, my mother was old and bent. When I returned to the village to marry, she kept her promise and danced at my wedding, tears streaming down her face. I did not understand those tears then.

Years passed. My mother passed away two years ago.

One day, I found an old notebook in a chest. In it, she had written: “All I had left were spinach seeds and a little corn to plant. There was no money. I cooked what I could grow so my children would not go hungry. I told them I served spinach three times a day because it was good for them. Saying it broke my heart. This was all I could do, though I loved them with everything I had.”

At fifty, I learned to love spinach. My wife wonders why I buy it so often. She thinks I like the taste. She does not know that its smell brings back my mother’s sacrifices and the sight of her dancing through tears at my wedding.

Moral: We often understand a mother's sacrifice in the face of impossibility years later. Love is sometimes hidden in a plate of simple food.

The King’s Missing Teeth

Audio
Image
Text

A king once dreamed that all his teeth had fallen out. He summoned an interpreter of dreams and asked what the dream meant.

The interpreter said:

“Your Majesty, all your relatives will die before you.”

The king was angered by the answer and sent him away. Then he called another interpreter and asked the same question.

The second man replied:

“Your Majesty, you will outlive everyone else in your family.”

Pleased by the answer, the king rewarded him generously.

Moral: The same truth can be spoken harshly or with tact. Words can wound or heal; wisdom lies in knowing how to speak them.

The Value of Your Family

Audio
Image
Text

One evening, a little boy asked his father, “How much do you earn in an hour?” The father wanted to know why. “I’m just curious,” the boy said. Reluctantly, his father answered, “A hundred dollars.”

The boy looked down, then asked, “Could you lend me fifty?” His father grew angry, assuming he wanted a pointless toy. He refused and sent the boy to bed. The child went quietly.

After pacing for a while, the father calmed down. He had not even asked what the money was for. Feeling guilty, he knocked on his son’s door. “Are you awake?”

He went in and apologized. “I had a difficult day and took it out on you. Here’s the fifty dollars.” The boy brightened and pulled some money from beneath his pillow. As he counted it, his father’s irritation returned. “Why ask for more when you already have money?”

“Because I didn’t have enough. Now I have a hundred dollars.” He held it out. “Can I buy an hour of your time? Could you come home early tomorrow and have lunch with me?”

These words deeply moved the father, and his eyes filled with tears. He hugged his little son and apologized to him.

Moral: The greatest need of children is the attention, love, and time spent with them by their parents.

Waiting for the Teeth

Audio
Image
Text

On a cold winter evening, an elderly couple entered McDonald’s. The husband bought one hamburger, a large portion of fries, and a large Coke. At their table, he cut the burger in half and divided the fries equally. Then he took a sip of Coke and passed it to his wife.

Other customers watched sympathetically. “Poor things. They’re sharing one meal.”

But while the man ate eagerly, his wife left her food untouched, taking only an occasional sip of Coke.

Eventually, a young employee approached.

“Excuse me, sir. Your wife hasn’t touched her food. If she doesn’t like it, could I bring her something else?”

The man smiled.

“Thank you, but we’re quite happy. We’ve been married for sixty years. We share everything.”

Still puzzled, the employee turned to the woman.

“Then why aren’t you eating? What are you waiting for?”

The old woman points to her husband and replies:

“The teeth, dear. I’m waiting for the teeth.”

Moral: No one should be left in need of the one they love. May Allah not leave anyone helpless, even in their own family.

Pull Out the Roots

Audio
Image
Text

During school holidays, we worked in our family garden. My late father wanted us to learn the value of effort, so he left us chores while he went to work. We watered the melons he had planted. For drinking water, we went to the well in our neighbor Uncle Aghasalim’s garden, since our own well water was unsuitable.

We learned to turn the soil and clear the weeds, but they kept coming back. One day, Father explained, “You must work deeper. Until you remove the roots, the job isn’t finished.”

Moral: Deal with the roots of a problem or a bad habit. Clearing away only what shows above the surface does not stop it from returning.
