A Different Path

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a cow returning to its shed crossed a forest where there was no path. Unable to choose the right path, it went uphill and downhill, leaving a crooked, difficult trail behind it.

The next day, a dog came to the same place. To reach the other side of the forest, it followed the cow’s trail. Then came a flock of sheep. Seeing a ready-made path, the shepherd led the flock along it.

Before long, people began using the trail as well. They complained about its difficulty as they turned left and right, ducked beneath branches, and twisted their way through the bushes. Yet no one thought to look for a better route.

Through constant use, the trail gradually became a road. Unaware that the other side of the forest could be reached in only half an hour, people and heavily burdened animals spent three hours following the cow’s winding path.

Years passed. The small road first became the town’s main route and eventually its most important street. Although it was the least convenient of the existing roads, everyone merely complained about the traffic.

Watching it all, the old and wise forest pitied the people who blindly followed the familiar road without ever asking whether it was the right one.

Moral: The path everyone follows is not always the best one. Sometimes those who step beyond habit and search for a new route reach their destination more quickly and easily.

Text

A hunter walking through a forest came upon an unusual scene. A woodcutter had been struggling for a long time to cut down a tree, swinging the axe with all his strength. His face was drenched in sweat and his whole body was tense.

The hunter approached and asked:

“Why is the work going so hard and slowly? Your axe is blunt. Why don’t you sharpen it?”

The woodcutter stared at him in surprise:

“What are you talking about? Where would I find time to sharpen my axe? I still have twenty more trees to cut!”

Moral: Hard work is valuable, but labor that has not been thought through can be ineffective. Sometimes a short pause to sharpen the tool makes all the work that follows faster and easier.

At the Bookstore

Audio
Image
Text

After a long search, a medical student found the book he needed at the Bilge bookstore in Bursa. When he saw the price on the back, his smile vanished: he could not afford it.

Hiding his disappointment, he leafed through the pages, found the section assigned by his professor, and—seeing that the owners were busy with customers—read it quickly before leaving.

For the next month, the student returned every other day and studied among the shelves. One week before his graduation examinations, he came again, but the book was gone. He searched every shelf; it seemed to have vanished.

As the pale-faced young man was about to leave, the owners stopped him:

“Please sit down. We have something to tell you.”

One bookseller reached from under his table and produced the book:

“A customer asked the price recently and wanted to buy it. I took it off the shelf and set it aside for you. You can keep reading it here.”

After completing his studies and becoming a doctor, the young man never forgot the two booksellers. He never charged patients who could not afford treatment.

Moral: A small act of kindness can leave a lifelong mark on the heart. Those who receive kindness often carry it forward to others.

At the Doctor’s Office

Audio
Image
Text

I was waiting for my turn at a doctor’s office. Having missed breakfast at home, I bought a sandwich and apple juice from the café and began to eat.

A boy sitting near the window was watching me closely. His expression seemed to say, “I am hungry too.” Feeling sorry for him, I returned to the café and bought him a sandwich and apple juice.

The boy ate eagerly, while I felt pleased with what I believed was a good deed.

At that moment, his mother emerged from the registration room. Seeing the scene, she hurried over in alarm:

“What have you done? The doctor had told us to bring the child to the examination hungry. I took time off work, and we’ve come such a long way. Now we’ll have to do it all again tomorrow.”

The woman could not hold back her tears. I stood frozen: while believing I was helping, I had undone an entire family’s effort.

Moral: Judgment based only on emotion and assumption can lead us to the wrong decision. Asking about the situation before helping is itself part of compassion.

I have lived another day...

Audio
Image
Text

The day began... The day ended...

Every morning when we open our eyes, we see that a new day has begun. Yet often we do not notice when evening arrives or when another day slips away from our lives. Time flows like a quiet river, while we stand on its bank, watching the passing moments drift away.

Sometimes we ask ourselves: What wisdom lay behind God's gift of this day to me? Did I cherish this divine trust? What did I add to this day, and what did I take from it? What light did I bring to my soul, and what opportunity did I let slip away?

The greatest treasure in a human life is time. For other things we lose may sometimes return, but not even a single moment that has passed comes back. That is why every day, every hour, and even every breath has its own value and meaning.

And so, I have lived another day... In the endless book of time, another page of my life has turned. Only God truly knows what was written on that page.

Moral: Every new day is a precious trust given to us. Reflecting at the end of the day on how we have spent it helps us live more meaningfully tomorrow.

Small changes, great value...

Audio
Image
Text

A monotonous life quietly wears down the human spirit. When every day is the same as the one before, when the same tasks are repeated, or when time passes without our doing anything at all, an invisible emptiness and a sense of inner unease take hold. For the spirit, like life itself, longs for movement, change, and something new.

Sometimes a small change enters our lives, and it is as though a new window opens within us. Ordinary days suddenly take on color, and our hearts fill with a pleasant excitement, a zest for life, and a sense of well-being. In truth, the beauty of life lies not only in the chance events that come our way, but also in how much beauty and freshness we ourselves can bring into it.

We should not be mere spectators in our own lives. Sometimes choosing a new path, meeting someone new, seeing a new place, learning, creating, or making time for something we love is enough to change the color of life. For something new need not be a major event; sometimes a small step is the beginning of a great uplift in spirits.

Life is not a page handed to us already written. We write each day ourselves. It is therefore in our own hands to fill the pages of our lives not with repetition, but with new feelings, new dreams, and beautiful memories.

Moral: We do not need to wait for major changes to bring something new into our lives. Even a little time devoted to learning and the things we love can give meaning to our day.

Text

One day, a villager’s donkey fell into a dry well. For hours it cried out in distress. Its owner and the neighbors came to help, but after much discussion they decided there was no way to pull it out. They saw no option but to fill the well with earth. Each took a shovel and began throwing soil into the well.

The Diderot Effect

Audio
Image
Text

The eighteenth-century French writer and Enlightenment thinker Denis Diderot fell deeply into debt. News of his difficulties reached the Russian empress Catherine.

The empress offered to help by buying his library and allowing him to keep it. She also paid him twenty-five years’ salary in advance to serve as its librarian. This unexpected fortune cleared his debts and lifted a great weight from his shoulders.

Then one day, a friend gave him a luxurious red velvet robe. That was where it all began.

Diderot put on the robe and sat down to work. Suddenly, he thought that the beautiful velvet robe and the desk did not suit each other.

Emboldened by his newfound wealth, he bought a magnificent desk to match his robe.

But now the old carpet seemed out of place beside both the robe and the desk, so he hurried and bought a carpet suited to his money and to himself.

Still, something felt wrong. The armchairs, cupboards, chairs, paintings, and ornaments all looked old and mismatched.

Now everything offended his eye.

Before long, Diderot had replaced everything in the house.

Eventually he realized that his endless desire for more had brought him back to where he had started.

He later wrote his famous work titled “I Regret Parting with My Old Robe.” He expressed the situation he had fallen into in a single sentence:

“I was the master of my old robe, but I became the slave of the new one.”

Moral: Buying something new can make us feel compelled to replace everything around it. This is known as the Diderot effect. Desire has no natural stopping point; it is better to be master of the old than slave of the new.

An Istanbul Woman Remembers

Audio
Image
Text

It was 1919. Istanbul was completely under British occupation. I had just finished middle school.

I was a pretty young woman, and matchmakers often visited our home. One day, a handsome, tall young man came with his representatives. He introduced himself as a lawyer. I liked him, and we became engaged. I loved him and dreamed of making a happy home. Until morning I stayed up by lamplight, weaving little towels for my dowry.

One day, a rumor spread in the neighborhood.

“They say Ayesha’s fiancé isn’t a lawyer at all. He earns his living carrying coffins in front of the mosque.”

I was devastated. My father and I went to the mosque, watched from a distance, and saw that he was really carrying a coffin.

I was heartbroken. We returned the engagement and parted.

Five years passed. I married someone else and had a child. Our country was free again.

One day I met him in the city. My son was with me.

He stopped when he saw me. He said hesitantly, “If you have time, I would like to offer you a cup of tea.” I agreed. We went into an office.

It was a law firm; his name was written on the sign on the door. His assistants were working inside.

“Are you really a lawyer?” I asked.

“Yes,” he said.

“But if you are a lawyer, why were you carrying a coffin in front of the mosque?”

He bowed his head and said, “Forgive me.”

“Istanbul was under occupation. British soldiers were everywhere, searching people and inspecting everything. We disguised arms shipments as funerals, sending coffins full of weapons to the National Forces in Anatolia. It was a vital duty for the country. I couldn’t tell even you.”

Moral: Do not judge hastily by what you see; behind every behavior there may be a truth that you don’t know. Our freedom is owed to the labor and the sacrifices of selfless people.

Hayat’s Life Story

Audio
Image
Text

When my mother left our home, my father, and us, I was six and my brother was eight. Even at that age I understood why she left my father, but I could not understand why she left us. Would a mother abandon her children?

My father used to beat my mother. He beat me and my brother too. I came to believe that all men beat their wives and children…

After my mother left, home became four walls. In the mornings, no one said, "Wash your hands and faces and come; breakfast is ready." No one even asked whether we were hungry, though we might go days without food. The hardest lesson was this: without a mother’s scent, children grow up in an instant, however old they are. I grew up at six.

About ten days after my mother left, the police came to our house. They said she had taken her own life. In her hand she had been clutching an envelope. On it was written: “To be given to my daughter and my son.”

I could not read then — how was I supposed to read it? My brother read it. I cried as I listened. My brother cried too.

My mother had gone to her father’s house, but he flew into a rage. In his eyes, a woman had to stay with her husband. Even with a mouth full of blood, she was expected to say, “I’ve been drinking red sherbet.” My mother begged him: “Let me stay for a month. Then I’ll find a way to take my children and start again.”

But a woman living alone? To my grandfather, that would make his honor worth five kopecks. He would never be able to hold his head up again; he said he would rather see his daughter dead. That evening, my mother hanged herself. My grandmother told me this years later, on her deathbed, when I was nineteen.

When my father heard of my mother’s death, he was not upset at all. He said he would put us in an orphanage. From then on we would live there; that would be our home. Brother and sister might be parted, forget each other, and years later fail to know each other — so we were both to keep Mother’s letter.

But before I could ask how the two of us would keep one letter, my brother cut it in half with scissors. He gave me the beginnings of the sentences. Since I had the openings, once I learned to read I would have to guess the rest. My brother had already memorized the letter anyway.

When my aunt heard we were to be sent to an orphanage, she came. She told my father: “A girl is not given to an orphanage. I will take Hayat and raise her myself.” Living with my aunt, I understood why: a girl meant an unpaid servant ordered about the house. Not once did my aunt call me by my name. My name became “Come-here”: “Come-here, fetch water… Come-here, wash these dishes…”

Once a month my brother visited me at my aunt’s. He said he was fine in the orphanage. I said I was fine too — I did not want to upset him. Did he also say “I’m fine” so I would not worry? I never dared ask.

I had started school. I was the first in the class to learn to read. How could I not — I was going to read my mother’s letter. The part I still had began: “Hayat, my beautiful daughter, I you…” Every night in bed I tried to finish that sentence:

“Hayat, my beautiful daughter, I love you so much.”

“Hayat, my beautiful daughter, I miss you terribly.”

“Hayat, my beautiful daughter, I am waiting for you.”

I built a different dream around each ending. In those dreams, I was happy. Who chooses to dream of sorrow?

I finished middle school. My aunt said I would not go to school anymore. But I wanted to study — to stand on my own feet and live under one roof with my brother.

That summer the wife of Uncle Hasan, the neighbourhood grocer with three children, died of cancer. I was heartbroken. What would the three children do — how would they find a mother’s scent? Why did mothers die? Would those three forget their names as I had, and become “Come-here” too?

While I was grieving for Uncle Hasan’s children, it came out that I was betrothed to him. It turned out I had been sold for eight bracelets and three thousand liras. Because I was too young for a civil marriage, forty days later our religious marriage was performed.

I was fifteen. I was Uncle Hasan’s wife. I had three children, aged two, five, and six. And my aunt insisted we must have a child of our own… I was supposed to have a child so my place would be secure and Uncle Hasan would not look at other women.

The first slap from Uncle Hasan came because I called him “Uncle Hasan.” Does a woman call her husband “uncle”? When I lost my mother at six, I learned to keep silent. So I did not ask, “Does a man call a fifteen-year-old girl his wife?” — I stayed quiet.

When Uncle Hasan hit me, all three children clung to their father’s legs: “Don’t hit Sister Hayat — she plays with us, she tells us stories,” they begged. I was those children’s older sister. What I called stories were my dreams.

When Uncle Hasan went out, I practiced saying “Hasan” in front of the mirror. But every time I said it, I pictured his thinning hair, his glasses slipping down his nose, and his large belly. It was as if I had sinned; I was ashamed, and I could not bring his name to my tongue. While I was practicing in front of the mirror, his six-year-old son came up to me. “Sister Hayat, Mother used to call him ‘Bey,’” he said. “You could call him that too.”

“Bey” — of course, “Bey” would be better. I bent and kissed dark-eyed Sami, who listened to my dreams as if they were fairy tales. I called five-year-old Elif and two-year-old Zahra and began to tell them a story. That day I began: “Once upon a time there was a six-year-old girl named Nagil. Nagil had lost her mother. She searched everywhere; when she could not find her, she went to the land of dreams. There Nagil was so happy that she never returned to the real world.”

In my tale Nagil was always happy, always smiling. Every day I told the children that story. We were very happy.

Uncle Hasan was good too. I now called him “Bey.” From time to time he grew angry, but I understood why: he had thought he was buying a wife for eight bracelets and three thousand liras, but he had brought home a sister who told the children stories.

My brother came to visit. Each time he wanted to give me his half of Mother’s letter. I would not take it. I was afraid that if I read the other half, I would fall from the land of dreams into a cruel real world and be unhappy. All my dreams were built on that other half.

By the time I was forty, the children who had listened to my stories were grown. Sami had become a doctor and, on assignment, moved to another city. Whenever he felt low, he called and said, “Sister Hayat, tell me a story.”

Elif had become a teacher and married. I had a granddaughter. They badly wanted to name her Hayat; I would not allow it. Elif said, “Then let her name be Nagil.” So my granddaughter’s name was Nagil.

My youngest, Zahra, became a veterinarian. “Sister Hayat, whenever an animal is restless I tell it a story, and it calms down,” she would say. Zahra married too, and I had a grandson.

When I was forty-five, Uncle Hasan—my Bey—died. I grieved for him. His last words were a plea for forgiveness, and I forgave him with all my heart. He was a good person.
