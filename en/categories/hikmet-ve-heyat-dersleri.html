A Different Path

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a cow returning to its barn had to cross a forest where there was no path. It climbed uphill, wandered downhill, and wound its way around trees and bushes, leaving a crooked trail behind it.

The next day, a dog followed the trail to reach the other side of the forest. Then came a flock of sheep. Seeing a path already there, the shepherd led his flock along it without looking for another route.

Before long, people began using it too. They turned this way and that, ducked beneath branches, and complained about the difficult walk. Yet no one stopped to ask:

“Could there be an easier way?”

As more feet passed over it, the trail widened into a road. People and laden animals spent three hours reaching a place they could have reached in half an hour by a more direct route. Still, everyone followed in the footsteps of those who had gone before.

Years passed. A town grew where the forest had stood. The old road became a main thoroughfare and eventually the town’s busiest street. The bends the cow had made while wandering among the trees remained unchanged.

Now cars became stuck along those same bends. Everyone complained about the traffic, but few wondered why the road followed such a route.

At the edge of town, the old forest seemed to whisper:

“One cow’s wandering footsteps have determined the path of generations…”

Moral: A path is not necessarily the best one simply because it has been used for years. Before following a habit, try to understand its purpose. Sometimes progress means changing your route, not walking faster.

The Light of the World

Audio
Image
Text

British artist William Holman Hunt’s painting The Light of the World was exhibited at the Royal Academy in London. It depicts Jesus Christ standing at a closed door at night, holding a lantern and knocking. He waits for the door to open.

According to a story told about the painting, an art critic examined it closely and asked Hunt:

“It’s a beautiful painting, but there’s something I don’t understand. How is the door supposed to open? Have you perhaps forgotten to paint the handle?”

Hunt smiled and replied:

“This is no ordinary door. It is the door to the human heart. That is why there is no handle on the outside. It opens only from within. Unless the person inside opens it, no one can enter.”

Moral: The door to the heart opens only from within. Love and kindness may knock, but each of us must choose whether to let them in.

A hunter walking through a forest came upon a curious sight. A woodcutter was swinging his axe with all his strength, yet making little progress. Sweat ran down his forehead, and every blow took a tremendous effort.

The hunter approached him.

“Your axe is blunt. Why not stop for a moment and sharpen it? The work would be much easier.”

Moral: Working efficiently means thinking about how we work, not just how hard. Time spent preparing and improving our tools is not wasted; it saves effort later.

At the Bookstore

Audio
Image
Text

After a long search, a medical student found the book he needed in a bookshop. He picked it up eagerly, but his smile faded when he saw the price on the back. He could not afford it.

Hiding his disappointment, he leafed through the pages until he found the section his lecturer had assigned. Seeing that the staff were busy with customers, he stood to one side and began reading. He remembered as much as he could, then put the book back and quietly left.

For the next month, he returned every other day. He studied among the shelves, believing no one had noticed.

A week before his final exams, he came back as usual. This time, the book was missing. He searched shelf after shelf, growing increasingly anxious.

As he was about to leave empty-handed, a member of staff called him over.

“Come and sit down. There’s something we’d like to tell you.”

He took the book from beneath the desk and placed it in front of the student.

“A customer wanted to buy this the other day. But we could see you hadn’t finished reading it, so we put it aside for you. Whenever you come in, you can sit here and study in comfort.”

For a moment, the student could not find the words to reply. He had thought he was going unnoticed. Yet the bookshop staff had understood his difficulty all along and helped him without embarrassing him.

Years later, he became a doctor. He never forgot the kindness he had received at the bookshop: he treated patients who could not afford his care without charging them.

Moral: Kindness that preserves a person’s dignity can leave a lifelong impression. The care you show one person may, through them, reach many others.

At the Doctor's Office

Audio
Image
Text

I was waiting for my doctor’s appointment. Having missed breakfast at home, I had bought a sandwich and some apple juice from the café.

As I began to eat, I noticed a boy sitting by the window watching me. He kept looking at the food in my hands.

“He must be hungry,” I thought.

I felt sorry for him. I went back to the café and bought him a sandwich and juice too. He thanked me and began eating eagerly. I felt pleased, thinking I had helped someone.

Just then, his mother returned from the reception desk. Seeing the food in his hands, she hurried over, alarmed.

“Did you give him that?”

I nodded.

“The doctor said he mustn’t eat before his examination… I’ve taken time off work, and we’ve come a long way. Now we may have to reschedule.”

Her eyes filled with tears. I did not know what to say. A moment earlier, I had been certain I was doing something kind. Now I realized that I had never asked why the boy had not eaten.

“I’m sorry… I wanted to help, but I should have asked you first.”

I sat there holding my half-eaten sandwich. I had noticed the boy’s gaze, but acted without understanding his situation.

Moral: Good intentions do not always guarantee a good outcome. Asking, listening, and understanding the situation before offering help are also acts of compassion.

Another day

Audio
Image
Text

The day began... The day ended...

Every morning when we open our eyes, we see that a new day has begun. Yet often we do not notice when evening arrives or when another day slips away from our lives. Time flows like a quiet river, while we stand on its bank, watching the passing moments drift away.

Sometimes we ask ourselves: What wisdom lay behind God's gift of this day to me? Did I cherish this divine trust? What did I add to this day, and what did I take from it? What light did I bring to my soul, and what opportunity did I let slip away?

The greatest treasure in a human life is time. For other things we lose may sometimes return, but not even a single moment that has passed comes back. That is why every day, every hour, and even every breath has its own value and meaning.

And so, I have lived another day... In the endless book of time, another page of my life has turned. Only God truly knows what was written on that page.

Moral: Every new day is a precious trust given to us. Reflecting at the end of the day on how we have spent it helps us live more meaningfully tomorrow.

Etibar Siracsoy

Small changes, great value…

Audio
Image
Text

A monotonous life quietly wears down the human spirit. When every day is the same as the one before, when the same tasks are repeated, or when time passes without our doing anything at all, an invisible emptiness and a sense of inner unease take hold. For the spirit, like life itself, longs for movement, change, and something new.

Sometimes a small change enters our lives, and it is as though a new window opens within us. Ordinary days suddenly take on color, and our hearts fill with a pleasant excitement, a zest for life, and a sense of well-being. In truth, the beauty of life lies not only in the chance events that come our way, but also in how much beauty and freshness we ourselves can bring into it.

We should not be mere spectators in our own lives. Sometimes choosing a new path, meeting someone new, seeing a new place, learning, creating, or making time for something we love is enough to change the color of life. For something new need not be a major event; sometimes a small step can greatly lift our spirits.

Life is not a page handed to us already written. We write each day ourselves. It is therefore in our own hands to fill the pages of our lives not with repetition, but with new feelings, new dreams, and beautiful memories.

Moral: We do not need to wait for major changes to bring something new into our lives. Even a little time devoted to learning and the things we love can give meaning to our day.

The Dry Well

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a farmer’s donkey fell into an old, dry well. Hearing the animal’s distressed braying, the farmer called his neighbors for help.

For hours, they tried to get the donkey out. Everyone had a suggestion, but nothing worked. Eventually, they gave up hope and decided to fill the well with earth.

They each took a shovel and began throwing earth into the well. As the first shovelfuls landed on its back, the donkey brayed even louder. Then, after a while, it fell silent.

The farmer peered into the well and could hardly believe what he saw. The donkey was shaking each shovelful off its back and stepping onto the earth beneath its feet.

With every shovelful, it rose a little higher. The earth meant to bury it was becoming a stairway to safety.

At last, the donkey reached the top of the well and stepped out unharmed, to everyone’s astonishment.

Moral: Life sometimes places a heavy burden on your back. When you can, shake it off, find your footing, and take another step upward. Sometimes the very thing that makes life difficult can become part of the way out.

The Diderot Effect

Audio
Image
Text

In his essay Regrets on Parting with My Old Dressing Gown, the eighteenth-century French philosopher and writer Denis Diderot describes how a single possession can lead to a succession of new expenses.

It all begins with a luxurious scarlet dressing gown.

When Diderot puts it on, the room where he once felt comfortable suddenly looks different. None of his old belongings has changed, yet beside the beautiful gown they now seem shabby and out of place.

The simple furnishings that once served him well no longer satisfy him.

One change leads to another. A leather armchair replaces his plain chair. An ornate cabinet takes the place of his wooden bookshelf. The pictures and decorations change. Eventually, even his old worktable gives way to an expensive writing desk.

The room becomes more elegant, but Diderot feels less at ease. The new gown has changed not only what he wears, but how he sees everything around him. Things he never needed before now seem essential.

He sums up his predicament:

“I was the master of my old dressing gown; I have become the slave of the new one.”

He decides to keep his old rug. Let that modest possession remind him of his former life and keep him from losing himself in luxury.

Today, the urge to follow one new acquisition with other purchases to match it is known as the Diderot effect. Sometimes a purchase does more than meet a need: it creates needs we did not have before.

Moral: One new possession does not require you to replace everything around it. Before buying something, ask yourself: “Do I really need this, or do I only want it to match something else?” Let your belongings make life easier, not dictate your choices.

An Istanbul Woman Remembers

Audio
Image
Text

It was during the occupation of Istanbul. I had just finished school, and families were beginning to visit ours with marriage proposals.

One day, a young man came with his family. He introduced himself as a lawyer. He was tall, handsome, and quietly composed. We liked each other and became engaged.

I loved him. At night, I worked by lamplight, embroidering linens for the home I imagined we would share. Every stitch held a hope for our future.

Then a rumor spread through the neighborhood.

“Ayşe’s fiancé isn’t a lawyer at all. He earns his living carrying coffins outside the mosque.”

What hurt most was not the work itself, but the thought that he had lied to me.

My father and I went to the mosque. From a distance, we saw him carrying a coffin with several other men.

We took that sight as proof of everything we had heard. Without having an honest conversation with him, we broke off the engagement.

Five years passed. I married and had a son. The occupation was over.

One day, walking through the city with my little boy, I met him again. He stopped when he saw me, then spoke hesitantly:

“If you have time, may I invite you for a cup of tea?”

I agreed. We went to a nearby law office. His name was on the door.

I could not hide my surprise.

“So you really are a lawyer?”

“Yes.”

“Then why were you carrying coffins outside the mosque?”

He was silent for a while. Then he lowered his head.

“I wish I could have explained it to you then. The city was occupied. Roads and goods were being checked. We were helping to send weapons to those fighting for independence in Anatolia. The coffins you saw contained weapons. We carried them as though we were taking part in a funeral. Revealing that secret could have put other people’s lives at risk. I couldn’t even tell you.”

This time, I was the one who fell silent.

The scene I had witnessed years before had not changed. But its meaning had changed completely. I had seen the coffin on his shoulder. I had known nothing of the responsibility he carried.

Moral: What you see may be only part of the truth. Before passing judgment, ask, listen, and try to understand. Behind an ordinary-looking act, someone may be carrying a responsibility you cannot see.

Hayat's Life Story

Audio
Image
Text

I was six and my brother was eight when our mother left home. Even then, I understood why she had left our father. What I could not understand was why she had not taken us with her.

In my child’s mind, one question kept returning: how could a mother leave her children?

My father beat her. He beat my brother and me too. I thought all men were like that. I thought this happened in every home.

After my mother left, our house felt like four walls and nothing more. No one called in the morning, “Wash your hands and faces. Breakfast is ready.” No one asked whether we had eaten.

The hardest lesson was this: sometimes growing up does not take years. One day of feeling utterly alone is enough.

I grew up at six.

About ten days after she left, the police came. They told us our mother had taken her own life. She had left a letter. On the envelope were the words: “For my daughter and my son.”

I could not read yet. My brother read it aloud. We both cried.

It was much later that I learned what had happened during those ten days. I was nineteen when my grandmother told me, as she lay dying.

My mother had sought shelter at her parents’ home. But her father did not want her there. He believed a woman belonged in her husband’s house, whatever happened. Even with a mouth full of blood, she was supposed to say she had been drinking sweet red sherbet.

My mother had begged him:

“Let me stay for a month. I’ll find a way. Then I’ll bring my children and make a new life.”

But my grandfather cared more about what people would say than about what his daughter had suffered. To him, a separated woman living on her own brought shame on the family. He had even told her she would be better off dead.

That evening, my mother died by suicide.

When my father heard the news, we saw no sign of grief. He told us he was sending us to a children’s home. That would be our home now.

My brother and I were terrified of being separated. We thought we might forget each other. We both needed to keep our mother’s letter.

But how could one letter go to two places?

My brother took a pair of scissors and cut the page down the middle. He gave me the half with the beginnings of the sentences. When I learned to read, he said, I could imagine how they ended. He already knew the whole letter by heart.

When my aunt heard we were being sent away, she came to see my father.

“You can’t send a girl there. I’ll take Hayat.”

I thought I was finally going to have a home. Instead, I learned that my aunt saw me less as a child who needed care than as another pair of hands.

She hardly ever used my name. She called me Slowcoach.

“Slowcoach, fetch some water.”

“Slowcoach, wash these dishes.”

My brother visited once a month. He said he was all right at the children’s home. I said I was all right too. I did not want to upset him.

Was he saying the same thing for the same reason?

I never dared to ask.

When I started school, I was the first in my class to learn to read. My mother’s letter was waiting for me.

My half began:

“Hayat, my beautiful girl, I…”

Every night, I finished that sentence differently.

“…love you so much.”

“…long to hold you again.”

“…am waiting for you.”

From those unwritten words, I built a world. My mother was there. I was not alone. Nobody shouted at me.

Real life hurt enough. At least in my imagination, I could be happy.

When I was fifteen, my aunt decided I had had enough schooling. But I wanted to study, earn a living, stand on my own feet, and share a home with my brother.

That summer, the wife of Hasan, our neighborhood grocer, died of cancer. She left three children: Sami, six; Elif, five; and Zehra, two.

I cried for them. Would they search for their mother’s familiar scent too? Would someone forget their names and call them Slowcoach?

While I worried about the children, the adults were deciding my future.

I learned that I was to be married to Hasan. The price was eight bracelets and three thousand lira.

I was too young for a civil marriage. Forty days later, they arranged a religious wedding.

I was fifteen. Still a child, yet expected to be a wife to a grown man and a mother to three children.

My aunt insisted I should have a baby of my own as well.

“That will secure your place in the family.”

Hasan first slapped me because I called him Uncle Hasan, as I always had.

“What woman calls her husband Uncle?”

I said nothing. I had learned silence long before.

But inside me was another question: what grown man calls a fifteen-year-old child his wife?

The three children clung to their father’s legs.

“Don’t hit Hayat! She plays with us. She tells us stories!”

To them, I was a big sister. And the stories I told them were my own dreams.

After Hasan left, I stood before the mirror and tried to say his name without “Uncle.” I could not. I still saw the older man I had always known, not a husband.

Six-year-old Sami came over.

“Mom used to call him Bey. You could call him that.”

Bey was a respectful form of address. That, at least, I could say.

I bent down and kissed him. Then I called Elif and Zehra over and began a new story.

“Once upon a time, there was a six-year-old girl called Fairy Tale. She had lost her mother and searched everywhere for her. When she could not find her, she went to the land of dreams. There, nobody hurt her or left her alone…”

In my stories, Fairy Tale always smiled. Each day I added a little more happiness to her life. The children listened, and I told my stories. In those moments, we could all breathe.

I called him Hasan Bey now. There were peaceful days and days when he grew angry. For years, I tried to explain his anger, sometimes blaming myself. He thought he had brought home a wife; I was a girl who played with his children and told them stories.

But being a child was not my fault.

My brother kept visiting. Each time, he offered me his half of the letter. Each time, I refused.

I was afraid to read it. For years, I had filled those missing lines with hope. I feared the real words would destroy the world I had built.

By the time I was forty, the children had grown up.

Sami became a doctor and moved to another city. When he was unhappy, he called.

“Hayat, tell me a story.”

Elif became a teacher and married. When her daughter was born, she wanted to name her Hayat. I asked her not to.

“Then we’ll call her Masal—Fairy Tale,” she said.

And that became my granddaughter’s name.

Zehra became a vet.

“When an animal is frightened, I tell it a story,” she would say with a smile. “My voice helps it settle.”

She married too, and I gained a grandson.

I was forty-five when Hasan—the man I had called Bey for so many years—died. I grieved for him. His last words were:

“Please forgive me for the wrong I’ve done you.”

I forgave him sincerely. Over the years, I had known kindness from him too. But forgiving him did not make what had happened to me as a child right.

I cannot extend that forgiveness to those who see nothing wrong with marrying off fifteen-year-old girls;

to those who turn abused daughters away in the name of family honor;

or to those who ignore a helpless child’s plea and defend the people responsible.

My name, Hayat, means “life.”

Yet for so many years, I was not allowed to choose my own.

Moral: No tradition or notion of family honor can justify violence or taking away a child’s future. Children belong in safe homes and classrooms, not forced marriages. Someone asking for help needs protection, not judgment.

The cost of indifference

Audio
Image
Text

When I asked one of our employees how he was getting on with an assigned task, his reply disappointed me deeply.

“The world won’t fall apart if I don’t do it in the next few days.”

True, I thought, the world would not fall apart. But what about the trust between us? Would that not collapse too? Would that attitude not harm our working relationship, our mutual respect, and our shared work?

A delay of a few days was not the real issue. It was his attitude toward a commitment. Responsibility means not only doing the work but also keeping your word, honoring the trust others place in you, and making sure people can rely on you.

It is wrong to treat a commitment so irresponsibly. Even more troubling was the way he expressed that attitude. To me, such an answer sounds rude, to say the least, and shows too little respect for the other person’s time, effort, and trust.

I admit that these words broke my heart. I knew then that we could not continue working together in quite the same way. A professional relationship depends on trust, and when trust is shaken, the partnership also faces a serious test.

Most disappointing of all, my colleague did not recognize that his words were inappropriate, apologize, or complete the task. Because my trust in him had been shaken, I had to solve the problem another way.

The experience reminded me that an organization’s most valuable asset is not time, money, or technology, but the trust people place in one another. It can take years to build and can sometimes be lost with a single sentence.

Moral: Trust is an organization’s most valuable asset. It can take years to earn and a single sentence to lose. Those who keep their word protect that trust.

The Liver

Audio
Image
Text

Why do Azerbaijani speakers call a close friend jan-jiyar, a beloved child jiyargusham, or someone precious to them jiyarparam? And why can aghjiyar, the word for “lung,” also describe a coward?

In Azerbaijani, jiyar is more than an anatomical term. It also appears in expressions of affection, closeness, sorrow, and character.

Similar associations existed in ancient cultures. In Mesopotamia, the liver was regarded as a seat of life, the soul, and emotions. When people sought to explain their inner world, they gave symbolic meaning not only to the heart but also to the liver.

Yet not every expression involving the liver can be traced to a single belief. Each expression has its own history and development.

Today, someone saying jiyarparam—literally “a piece of my liver”—is not thinking about anatomy. They mean that another person is as precious as a part of themselves. This is the power of language: a word for part of the body becomes an expression of our deepest feelings.

Moral: Language carries not only our thoughts but also the ideas of earlier generations. An everyday word can hold generations of love, sorrow, and ways of understanding the world.

“And the sheep and the people riding it?” the young man asked.

“In that symbolic scene,” the old man replied, “the sheep carries people towards Paradise. Moral: Do not overshadow your kindness with boasting or silence your conscience by growing used to wrongdoing. When seeking guidance, turn to people whose knowledge, integrity, and actions are in harmony.
