The Church Bell

Audio
Image
Text

Long ago, a country had a custom: when a commoner died, the church bell rang once; for a nobleman, twice; for a relative of the king, three times; and for the king himself, four times.

One day, a court that people trusted to uphold justice wrongfully convicted a citizen. The church bell rang five times.

The bewildered townspeople gathered at the church. “Father, who could be more important than the king? Why did the bell ring five times?”

“Justice,” the priest replied. “Today, justice has died.”

Moral: When justice dies, something greater than the king dies. Stand by the truth so that injustice does not go unpunished.

Confidence Is Half the Battle

Audio
Image
Text

A school principal invited several of his fellow teachers into his office and said to them:

“You are our most capable teachers,” he told them, “so we’re entrusting you with a group of our brightest students. Over the coming year, give them the best education you can and help them reach their potential. I wish you every success.”

Teachers, students, and parents considered this initiative a very good idea and were eagerly awaiting the end of the school year.

At the end of the year, their test results surpassed those of other students across the city.

The principal congratulated the teachers and said: “I have a confession to make. Those students we entrusted to you were not the most capable children at all - they were average. We chose them by chance.”

Hearing this, the teachers became proud and began to consider the students’ success a result of their professionalism.

“But I have another confession,” the principal continued. “You weren’t selected as our best teachers either. I drew your names at random from a bag. You succeeded because you believed you could.”

Moral: Confidence can bring hidden abilities to life. High expectations may be the first step toward strong results.

The Dervish’s Long Spoons

Audio
Image
Text

One day a dervish was asked:

“What is the difference between people who truly love and those who merely talk about love?”

“Wait, and I’ll show you,” he replied.

He invited several people who spoke often of love and set a meal before them. First came bowls of hot soup, then unusually long spoons. The dervish laid down one rule:

“You must hold your spoons at the very end of the handle.”

They tried again and again, but the spoons were too long to bring to their own mouths. Soup spilled over them, they burned themselves, and at last they left the table hungry and embarrassed.

Then the dervish invited people who understood what true love meant. He served the same soup and gave them the same rule. This time, each person filled a spoon and held it out to someone across the table. They fed one another, laughed together, and rose from the table well fed and grateful.

When the guests had gone, the dervish turned to those who had asked the question:

“Now you see. At the table of life, those who think only of themselves may go hungry. Those who reach out to others are nourished in return. We gain from giving as well as receiving.”

Moral: Those who think only of themselves may remain hungry at life’s table. Those who care for others are nourished in return. Giving is its own reward.

The Dervish’s Robe

Audio
Image
Text

One day a wounded bird came before the king and complained that a dervish had broken its wing. The king immediately summoned the dervish.

“This bird says you broke its wing. Why did you do it?”

The dervish tried to defend himself:

“Your Majesty, I wanted to catch it. I approached, but it didn’t fly away. I came closer, and still it stayed. I thought it had surrendered. But when I reached for it, it tried to escape and hurt its wing.”

The king turned to the bird:

“He didn’t approach you without warning. You saw him coming and could have flown away. Why did you stay?”

The bird replied:

“Because he was dressed as a dervish. If he had looked like a hunter, I would have flown away at once. But I thought, ‘A dervish fears Allah. Surely he won’t hurt me.’”

The king was silent for a moment, then ordered:

“The bird is right. Break the dervish’s arm!”

The bird protested immediately:

“Please, Your Majesty, don’t do that.”

“Why not?”

“When his arm heals, he may do the same thing again. Take away his dervish’s robe instead, so that other birds won’t be deceived as I was.”

Moral: The appearance of holiness does not make a person righteous. Judge people by their actions, not their clothing; a robe alone does not make a dervish.

Work with Your Hands and Trust in Allah

Audio
Image
Text

A dervish spent much of his time in prayer and solitude. He did not want to depend on others, so he also worked each day, buying bread with his earnings and sharing what remained with the poor.

One day in the desert, he saw a fox whose injured legs could barely carry it. “How does it survive?” he wondered. “It cannot hunt. How does Allah provide for it?”

Just then, a lion appeared with a gazelle in its jaws. It ate its fill and walked away. The fox crawled over and fed on what remained.

The dervish marveled at how Allah had provided for the fox. Then he thought, “If the fox receives food without working, why shouldn’t I? I could spend all my time in worship. Surely Allah will provide.”

He stopped working and prayed day and night. But no one brought food, and he grew weak and thin.

Finally, one day when he was exhausted, he heard a voice from the unseen:

“I gave you health and strength. Why choose the fox as your example instead of the lion? Rise and work. Earn your living, and let those who cannot help themselves find support in you. Feed yourself and others.”

Ashamed, the dervish repented and returned to work. Once more, he earned his bread and shared it, abandoning the wish to live at others’ expense.

Moral: A person who looks at the share of the needy despite having the strength is not trusting in Allah - he is simply covering up his laziness with faith.

The Fortieth Day

Audio
Image
Text

In some Turkic communities, tradition keeps a mother and newborn at home without visitors for forty days after the birth. The custom is said to date back to the Göktürks.

On the fortieth day, a close relative invites the mother and baby to visit and presents symbolic gifts: coins for prosperity; cotton for a long life and a gentle heart, even when the child’s hair turns white; sugar for sweet words and a cheerful face; wheat for abundance at home; salt for a life that never loses its flavor; and eggs for nourishment and strength.

Moral: Customs can hold generations of love and blessings. A baby’s gifts express hopes for an entire lifetime.

Ill-Gotten Money

Audio
Image
Text

One day, I saw a man drop some money in the street. Usually I would have called out, “You’ve dropped your money!” This time, temptation got the better of me. I picked it up and slipped it into my pocket.

When I got home, I told my wife the story.

“Add a little of our own and let’s go to the movies,” she said. I agreed, and we made plans for the weekend.

“You always promise, then change your mind,” she said. “Let’s buy the tickets online now.” So I did.

I work as a repairman. Soon afterward, a good customer called to offer me a job at his garden on Saturday. I asked whether I could come on Sunday, but he said it had to be Saturday. Of course, that was the day I had booked the movie. I lost a well-paid job.

When Saturday came, my wife and I were about to leave when her parents arrived unexpectedly.

I was ready to say, “Sorry, we’re off to the movies,” but my wife got in first. “We’re going to the movies—come with us!” There was nothing I could do.

My mother-in-law disliked public transportation, so we took a taxi. Naturally, I paid.

At the cinema, I bought two more tickets and popcorn for everyone. After all, the son-in-law had found money in the street; he could afford to treat them! I had spent less on meat for the feast than I spent on that popcorn. They soon finished it, so I bought more. My windfall was already costing me dearly, but the worst was still to come.

After the movie, I ran into an old acquaintance with whom I had a longstanding quarrel. Despite the presence of my in-laws, he insulted me. I lost my temper, and we began fighting. My father-in-law joined in. By the time the police arrived, my nose and his arm were broken. We settled the dispute at the station, but I still had to pay both our medical bills.

Recently, I saw a man buying a doner kebab. When the seller handed him his change, some coins fell to the ground. The man left them there. I couldn’t help myself. “Brother, if you won’t think of yourself, at least think of whoever finds that money! Please pick it up. Don’t bring trouble on someone else!”

Moral: Money taken dishonestly brings no true benefit and may cost far more than it seems to offer. Leave what is not rightfully yours.

The Door of the Heart

Audio
Image
Text

A story about the British painter William Holman Hunt describes a picture of a figure standing at a closed door at night, holding a lantern in one hand and knocking with the other.

An art critic asked him, “It’s a beautiful painting, but how can that door open? Have you forgotten to paint a handle?”

Moral: The door of the heart opens only from the inside. Love and goodness do not force their way in - you must let them in yourself.

Once there was a man who had grown tired of wasting his days in idleness and living off his inheritance. Wanting to lead an honest life and stay on the right path, he went to the king for advice.

Moral: A person who gives full attention to what is right and remembers that Allah sees his actions is far less likely to lose his way.

Meat for the Feast

Audio
Image
Text

I was in fifth grade. We were managing well enough then. My father, a builder, had joined a housing cooperative, and we hoped to move into our own home within six or seven months.

One morning we woke up to a terrible shaking. There had been an earthquake. Thankfully, nothing bad had happened to us or to those around us. No one in our building was hurt. But although our house and our lives were safe, this event brought another trouble to our family: as a result of the earthquake, construction work had stopped, and my father was unemployed.

No matter how hard we tried to save, the money my father earned from the repair jobs he found from time to time was not enough to support our household.

My father couldn't find work all winter. He would go with hope and return sad.

A few days before Ramadan, he came home beaming: someone had offered him work. “They told me to bring my papers,” he said, hurrying out again. “I need to get them there today.”

A few hours had passed when our neighbor came in with a panicked expression on his face.

“Don’t be afraid, but your father has had a minor accident,” he said.

At the hospital, we found him pale, his arms and legs bandaged. A driver had run a red light and hit him. As we gave thanks that he was alive, he tried to hide his tears. “Why did I survive?” he whispered. “Now I’ve made things even harder for you.”

After a while, my father came home. Our stove wasn’t burning, our house was cold. Several of our neighbors had called the municipality and asked for coal for us. “No way,” they said — we had to register in advance.

Eid al-Fitr came and went without sweets. At least the weather was warmer, and my father’s arm had healed, though his leg was still bandaged.

Then came Eid al-Adha. I thought to myself: “There are twelve apartments in our building, many of them will sacrifice. At least they will give us a share, and my mother will cook my favorite meat dishes.” As I watched from the window, the sacrificial animals were slaughtered, skinned, and bowls of meat were carried to the houses in the empty area opposite. Every time the door was opened, the delicious smell of meat being roasted in the houses spread to our house. But unfortunately, no one brought us a piece of meat.

Meanwhile, my grandmother called from the village to congratulate us on our holiday. She sensed that I was worried by the sound of my voice as she spoke. She asked insistently and anxiously: “Has your father gotten worse?”

“No,” I said slowly. “The neighbors didn’t give us sacrificial meat.”

In the summer months, we would go to the village to my grandmother. She had a cow and five or six chickens. Her cow gave good milk. When she milked it, my grandmother would sing songs to her.

On the third day of the holiday, there was a knock on our door early in the morning. It was my grandmother who came. We welcomed her with joy. She hugged us all, crying. “My lambs, my lambs, I brought you a lot of meat,” she said.

We filled the refrigerator with meat. My sister cut some up to cook, and the smell made my mouth water. Too hungry to wait for the table, I lifted a piece from the pan with a fork. Just as I was about to eat, I heard my father say:

“Mother, why did you slaughter your cow?”

A visiting scholar came to Tamerlane’s court and asked, through an interpreter, to meet the country’s learned men. None dared debate him. At last, the Mullah volunteered.

“Finally, I asked about humans and animals. With the egg, he reminded me to include birds.”

After the visitor had gone, Tamerlane summoned the Mullah for his explanation.

“The scholar?” said the Mullah. “I thought he would be a learned man, but he was only hungry. First he drew a circle, meaning a pan of omelet. Then he raised his hand, asking for a pot of pilaf.

“I turned my hand down to say I’d have the topping. Then he rubbed his stomach to show he was hungry from traveling. I took out my egg and told him, ‘I’m hungrier than you, and this is all I have. Don’t ask me to share it!’”

Moral: The same sign is read differently in each person’s world. We often see the other in the mirror of our own thoughts.

The Price of a Miracle

Audio
Image
Text

(A True Story)

Lisa was eight years old when she heard her parents talking about her younger brother, Oliver. All the little girl knew was that her brother was very sick and that her parents had no money for his treatment.

Only a very expensive operation could save Oliver. There was no one to lend them money. Lisa heard her father whisper helplessly to her tearful mother, “Only a miracle can save him now.”

The girl went into her room and took out a glass jar that she had kept in a secret place in the closet. She poured out all the money in the jar on the floor and counted it carefully—she did this several times. When she was finished counting, she carefully put the coins in the jar, closed the lid, and went out the back door to the pharmacy.

At the pharmacy, she waited for the pharmacist to notice her. He was deep in conversation and ignored her attempts to attract his attention. Finally, she placed a few coins firmly on the glass counter. He turned, startled.

“What do you need, little girl? I’m talking to my brother. I haven’t seen him in a long time.”

“I need to talk about my brother too,” Lisa said urgently. “He’s ill, and I need a miracle to make him better.”

“A miracle?”

“His name is Oliver. There’s something bad growing in his brain. My parents say only a miracle can save him now. How much does one cost?”

“I’m sorry, but we don’t sell miracles here,” the pharmacist said more gently.

“I have money. If it isn’t enough, I’ll find more. Please just tell me the price.”

A smartly dressed man who had been watching the conversation from the sidelines until now—the pharmacist’s brother—leaned down and asked Lisa:

“What sort of miracle does your brother need?”

“I don’t know. He’s very sick and needs an operation. My parents can’t pay, so I brought my own money.”

“How much do you have?”

“One dollar and eleven cents. That’s all, but I can try to get more.”

“One dollar and eleven cents,” he said with a smile. “What a coincidence. That’s exactly the price of the miracle your brother needs.”

The man took the money from the girl, took her hand, and said:

“Take me home with you. I’d like to meet your brother and your parents. Let’s see what I can do.”

This handsome man was Dr. Carlton Armstrong, a renowned neurosurgeon.

He performed the operation without charge, and Oliver soon came home. His parents were overjoyed. “It’s a miracle,” his mother whispered. “I wonder what that operation would have cost.”

Lisa smiled. She knew the price: one dollar and eleven cents, and a little girl’s faith that her brother could be helped.

Moral: Faith is a powerful force. Sometimes, when a child’s pure faith meets the kindness of a human being, a miracle is born.
