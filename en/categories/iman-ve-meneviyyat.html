The Church Bell

Audio
Image
Text

Long ago, so the story goes, a country had a custom: when an ordinary citizen died, the church bell tolled once. For a noble, it tolled twice; for someone close to the king, three times; and for the king himself, four times.

One day, the court—the very place people turned to in the hope of finding justice—convicted an innocent man.

That day, the church bell tolled five times.

Bewildered, the townspeople gathered outside the church and asked the priest:

“Who could be more important than the king? Why did you ring the bell five times?”

The priest answered sorrowfully:

“Justice. Today, justice died.”

Moral: An unjust verdict harms more than one person: it shakes everyone’s faith in justice. Speaking out against injustice protects both those who suffer and the conscience of society.

Confidence Is Half the Battle

Audio
Image
Text

A teaching story tells of a principal who called several teachers into his office and gave them an assignment.

“You are among our most capable teachers. We are therefore putting a class of specially selected, talented pupils in your care. This year, we want you to help them develop their abilities even further.”

Encouraged by this trust, the teachers prepared their lessons enthusiastically, tried new approaches, and patiently supported pupils who struggled. The children sensed that their teachers believed in them. They became more willing to ask questions and attempt difficult tasks.

By the end of the school year, the class had achieved excellent results. Teachers and pupils alike were delighted.

The principal congratulated the teachers.

“There is something I need to tell you. These children were not selected for exceptional ability. They were chosen at random.”

The teachers exchanged glances. Their hard work must have played a considerable part in the children’s progress.

The principal continued:

“And you were not selected through a competition either. I drew your names by lot.”

The room fell silent.

“You believed in your own abilities and in the children’s,” he said. “Then you turned that belief into effort, patience, and care. That is what made the difference.”

Moral: Belief alone does not create success, but it can encourage people to try, learn, and persevere. If you believe someone can succeed, show them that belief and support their growth.

The Dervish's Long Spoons

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a dervish was asked:

“What is the difference between those who truly love and those who only talk about love?”

“Let me show you,” he replied.

First, he invited a group of people who spoke a great deal about love to share a meal. Bowls of hot soup were set before them, along with spoons that had very long handles.

“There is one condition,” he said. “You must hold your spoons at the very end of the handle.”

The guests began to eat. But the spoons were so long that no one could bring the soup to their own mouth. However hard they tried, the soup spilled and the spoons knocked against one another. Each person was intent on feeding only themselves. Eventually, they left the table tired and hungry.

Then the dervish invited people who showed their love through their actions. They were given the same soup, the same spoons, and the same condition.

One guest filled a spoon and offered it to the person opposite. That person returned the gesture. Soon, everyone was feeding one another. Conversation and laughter rose around the table, and no one went hungry.

After the guests had left, the dervish turned to those who had asked the question.

“The spoons were the same. So was the soup. The difference was in how the people treated one another. At the first table, everyone thought only of themselves. At the second, everyone cared for the person opposite them too.”

Moral: Love shows itself in acts of care, not words alone. When we notice one another’s needs and offer our help, we can overcome difficulties together.

The Dervish’s Robe

Audio
Image
Text

One day, an injured bird came before the king with a complaint.

“A dervish has broken my wing. I ask you for justice.”

The king summoned the man.

“Why did you break this bird’s wing?”

The man in the dervish’s robe tried to defend himself.

“Your Majesty, I was trying to catch it. I approached, but it did not fly away. I moved closer, and still it stayed where it was. I thought I could catch it easily. But when I reached for it, it struggled to escape. That was when its wing broke.”

The king turned to the bird.

“Why didn’t you fly away when you saw him approaching?”

The bird replied:

“He was wearing a dervish’s robe. Had I known he was a hunter, I would have flown away. I thought he was a compassionate man who feared God and would do me no harm. I stayed because I trusted what his robe stood for.”

The king was silent for a moment. Then he ordered angrily:

“Break his arm as well!”

The bird spoke up at once.

“No, Your Majesty! Please don’t!”

“Why not?” asked the king in surprise.

“His arm will heal, and he may hurt someone again. Have him take off the dervish’s robe, so that others like me won’t be deceived into trusting him because of what he wears.”

Moral: Wearing something that inspires trust does not make a person worthy of it. Judge people by their actions, not their clothing or titles.

Hands at Work, Hope in God

Audio
Image
Text

There was once a dervish who spent much of his day praying in solitude. He supported himself through his own labor: each day, he worked for a while, bought bread with his earnings and shared whatever he did not need with the poor.

One day, while praying in the desert, he noticed a fox that could barely use its legs. The animal struggled to drag itself along. The dervish wondered:

“How does this fox survive? It cannot hunt or even walk properly. How does God provide for it?”

Just then, a lion appeared in the distance, dragging a gazelle it had caught. It set down its prey, ate its fill and walked away, leaving the rest.

The fox, which had been waiting nearby, dragged itself over and ate until it was no longer hungry.

The dervish marveled at what he had seen. He praised God for providing for the fox through the lion. Then he thought:

“God, who feeds this fox, will surely feed me too. I can spend the time I usually work in prayer instead. There is no need to work any longer.”

From that day on, he stopped working and devoted all his time to worship. But the food he expected never arrived. No one knocked on his door or brought him even a piece of bread. Day by day, he grew weaker.

At last, one day, he heard an unseen voice:

“I gave you the strength and health to work. Why did you choose to imitate the fox that could not walk? Why did you not learn from the lion? Get up, work and earn your living. Let those who need help benefit from your labor too. Feed yourself, and feed someone in need.”

The dervish realized that he had drawn the wrong lesson from what he had witnessed. He repented and returned to his former way of life, earning his bread and sharing it with those in need.

Moral: Trusting in God does not mean giving up your efforts. It means doing what you can and placing your trust in Him. If you have the strength and opportunity, work both to support yourself and to help others.

Drinking the Sherbet of Martyrdom

Audio
Image
Text

According to a traditional account, mortally wounded soldiers in Ottoman times were given sherbet—a sweet drink—to leave a pleasant taste in their mouths during their final moments. Doctors are said to have prepared it with water from the sacred Zamzam well and carried it in small barrels.

The expression “to drink the sherbet of martyrdom,” meaning to die a martyr, is sometimes linked to this account. However, the story should be treated as a traditional explanation rather than an established account of the expression’s origin.

Its most moving element is the idea that a person should not be deprived of tenderness and care, even at the end of life.

Moral: Language preserves memories, beliefs, and stories from the past. Honoring that inheritance means appreciating its meaning while distinguishing tradition from documented history.

Fasting

Audio
Image
Text

During Ramadan, a few young men noticed an elderly man sitting quietly to one side, eating. They approached him and asked mockingly:

“What’s this? You’re eating? Aren’t you fasting?”

The old man answered calmly:

“I am, my boys. I just happen to be eating and drinking.”

They laughed.

“What kind of fasting is that?”

He replied:

“I keep my tongue from lying, my eyes from looking at others with ill intent, and my hands from taking what belongs to someone else. I don’t mock people, belittle them, or gossip about them. I avoid wastefulness. My illness prevents me from going without food, but I try to make my words and actions reflect the spirit of this month.”

Then he asked:

“And what about you? Are you fasting?”

The young men lowered their heads in shame.

“Perhaps we’re only going without food…”

Moral: The spiritual value of fasting lies not only in enduring hunger, but also in disciplining our speech, thoughts, and behavior. Before judging someone else, try to understand their circumstances and examine your own actions.

The Fortieth-Day Rite

Audio
Image
Text

In some Turkic communities, the first forty days after childbirth are regarded as a time of special care for mother and baby. Customs concerning outings and visitors vary between regions and families.

One account of the tradition marking the end of these forty days describes a visit to relatives. The hosts prepare small gifts for the baby, each carrying a wish:

Coins, for a life free from want;

Cotton, for a long life reaching into gray-haired old age, and a gentle heart;

Sugar, for sweetness and happiness;

Rice, for abundance in the home;

Salt, for a life that never loses its savor;

An egg, for nourishment and good health.

These objects do not determine the child’s future. They give a tangible form to the family’s hopes for a happy life.

Moral: A small gift can hold a lifelong wish. The beauty of this custom lies in the care shown to mother and baby, and in the blessings offered for the years ahead.

Friend of Allah

Audio
Image
Text

An elderly man saw a barefoot child in the street. He took the child to a nearby shop and bought a pair of shoes.

The child held the new shoes and gazed at them in silence. Then, looking up, the child asked:

“Are you God?”

The man’s eyes filled with tears. He knelt beside the child.

“No, little one. I’m just a person, like you.”

The child thought for a moment, then said softly:

“Then you must be a friend of God. Last night, I was crying, and I told Him I didn’t have any shoes.”

The man could find no words. He helped the child put the shoes on.

Moral: Sometimes the help someone prays for arrives through another person’s compassion. A kindness that seems small to you may kindle a great hope in someone else’s heart.

The Gifts of Allah

Audio
Image
Text

Every blessing from God brings a responsibility. We will be called to account not simply for what we have received, but for how we have honored it:

If given strength—whether we have used it justly;

If given authority—whether we have treated others with compassion;

If given wealth—whether we have used it with integrity and generosity;

If given peace and comfort—whether we have appreciated them and given thanks;

If given a life partner—whether we have shown faithfulness and care;

If given a child—whether we have raised them with love, patience, and responsibility.

Moral: Every blessing is also a test. What reveals our character is how we value what we have been given and what we do with it.

The Hajj Pilgrimage

Audio
Image
Text

A parable tells of a man on the Hajj pilgrimage who, during the symbolic stoning of the devil, began directing the stones at himself. A young man watching him asked in astonishment:

“Sir, what are you doing? Why are you throwing stones at yourself?”

The man replied:

“My son, casting stones here is not enough. We must first confront the evil within us, discipline our selfish desires, and cleanse our hearts.”

The heat is in the fire, not the iron above it;
Wisdom is in the mind, not the crown upon it.
Whatever you seek, begin by looking within—
Neither holy cities nor pilgrimage can do that work for you.

Moral: Our hardest struggle is with our own ego and desires. In this parable, the stones turned inward symbolize examining ourselves, not harming our bodies. The task is to free the heart from resentment, arrogance, and greed. A pilgrimage finds its deepest meaning in the change it brings within us.

Ill-Gotten Money

Audio
Image
Text

One day, I saw some money fall from the pocket of a man walking ahead of me. Normally, I would have called out straight away:

“You’ve dropped your money!”

This time, temptation got the better of me. He walked on, and I picked up the money and pocketed it.

At home, I told my wife what had happened.

“Add a little to it, and let’s go to the cinema this weekend,” she said.

I agreed. We bought the tickets that very day so I couldn’t change my mind.

I work as a handyman. The next day, a good customer called to ask whether I could do some work at his country house on Saturday. I offered Sunday, but that didn’t suit him. Our cinema tickets were for Saturday, so I turned down a worthwhile job.

On Saturday, just as we were leaving, my wife’s parents arrived. Before I could think what to say, my wife greeted them cheerfully:

“We’re going to the cinema. Come with us!”

Instead of taking public transport, we called a taxi. At the cinema, I bought two more tickets. Then came popcorn and drinks. With every payment, that little windfall seemed to shrink.

When the film ended, I thought the spending was finally over. I was wrong.

Outside, I ran into someone I held an old grudge against. He made a cutting remark. I answered back. One word led to another, and soon we were fighting. My father-in-law tried to separate us and got caught in the middle.

By the time the police arrived, my nose was injured, as was my father-in-law’s arm. Then came the hospital and treatment expenses. I ended up spending many times the amount I had picked up.

At first, I blamed everything on that money. Then I realized: no one had forced me to keep it from its owner, and no one had forced me to answer an insult with a punch. Each decision had been mine.

Some time later, I saw a man buying a kebab. As the seller handed him his change, a few coins fell to the ground. The man started to leave without picking them up.

I couldn’t help calling out:

“Pick that money up, my friend! If you don’t need it, at least spare a thought for the poor soul who finds it!”

He stared at me. I laughed at my own words. By then, I knew that money doesn’t bring trouble on its own. Sometimes we do that through our choices.

Moral: Keeping money when you know who owns it is not good fortune; it is taking what belongs to someone else. Resist the lure of easy money, and take responsibility for your decisions. Peace of conscience is worth more than any windfall.

If fate allows

Audio
Image
Text

Someone who used to say, “We’ll meet,” now says, “We’ll meet if fate allows.” I find that strange. I have not changed, and my feelings toward him are as warm as before; I would still be delighted to see him. Yet those words seem to place a barrier between us. I know nothing of his fate, and he knows nothing of mine. Do our two fates know each other? Can they arrange a meeting on our behalf?

Perhaps we should not expect anything from fate. Perhaps this is simply another way of saying, “Out of sight, out of mind.”

I did not like the phrase “if fate allows” at all.

Moral: Do not let distance grow between you and those you care about. Do not put it off by saying “if fate allows” — meet today and brighten their day.

Bakhtiyar Sirajov

The door to the heart

Audio
Image
Text

British artist William Holman Hunt’s painting The Light of the World was exhibited at the Royal Academy in London. It depicts Jesus Christ standing at a closed door at night, holding a lantern and knocking. He waits for the door to open.

According to a story told about the painting, an art critic examined it closely and asked Hunt:

“It’s a beautiful painting, but there’s something I don’t understand. How is the door supposed to open? Have you perhaps forgotten to paint the handle?”

Hunt smiled and replied:

“This is no ordinary door. It is the door to the human heart. That is why there is no handle on the outside. It opens only from within. Unless the person inside opens it, no one can enter.”

Moral: The door to the heart opens only from within. Love and kindness may knock, but each of us must choose whether to let them in.

My Alif Has Been Dotted

Audio
Image
Text

Turkish has an expression, “elifim noktalandı,” which literally means “my alif has been dotted.” It conveys the feeling of sighing in sorrow, of a heart in pain—a poetic way of saying that joy has turned to sadness.

One explanation draws on the shapes of letters in Ottoman script. When the letter alif (ا) is followed by he (ه), they form the word “ah,” a sigh. Beside the tall alif, the small, rounded he is likened to a dot. This resemblance provides the imagery behind the expression.

In this poetic reading, the upright, slender alif suggests beauty and freedom. The curved he beside it seems to carry the weight of sorrow. Together, the two letters become a sigh.

The shapes of the letters thus reflect a change within the heart: where there was joy a moment ago, there is now sadness.

Moral: Sometimes a small change can express a powerful feeling. Language preserves not only our thoughts, but also the traces of our joy and sorrow.

Staying on the Right Path

Audio
Image
Text

A man lived idly, spending his inheritance without ever working. Eventually, he grew tired of this life and went to the king, asking for guidance on how to live honestly and with purpose.

The king thought for a moment, then said:

“Take this jar, filled to the brim with olive oil, to the other end of the city without spilling a single drop. If you spill even one, you will be beheaded.”

He sent two soldiers to watch over him.

The man focused all his attention on the jar. Taking each step carefully, he reached his destination without spilling a drop. Then he returned to the palace and appeared before the king.

“Your Majesty, I have carried out your command exactly.”

The king asked:

“What did you see in the city? What happened along the way?”

It was market day, and the streets were packed with people. Yet the man replied:

“Your Majesty, my life was at stake. I was so intent on keeping the oil from spilling that I never took my eyes off the jar to look around. I noticed neither the people nor anything that was happening.”

The king said:

“There is the lesson you were seeking. Bring that same care and sense of responsibility to your work and to every duty you undertake. Give your attention to what you do, and remember that God always sees you. Then you will find it easier to stay on the right path.”

Moral: Staying on the right path begins with everyday choices. Care in our work, a sense of responsibility for fulfilling our commitments, and awareness that we are accountable to God help keep us from going astray.

I was in fifth grade. We managed well enough and did not depend on anyone’s help. My father, a bricklayer, had joined a housing cooperative. We counted the days, telling ourselves, “In six or seven months, we’ll move into our own home.”

One morning, we woke to violent shaking. An earthquake had struck. Thankfully, neither our family nor anyone nearby was hurt. Everyone in our building was safe. But the earthquake brought a different hardship: construction stopped, and my father lost his job.

However carefully we spent, the money he earned from occasional repair jobs was not enough. All winter, he searched for work. Each morning he left with hope; each evening he returned discouraged.

A few days before Eid al-Fitr, he came home beaming. He had found a job!

“They told me to bring my documents. I have to get there today!”

He gathered his papers and hurried out.

A few hours later, our next-door neighbor rushed in. The look on his face frightened us.

“Don’t panic. Your father has been in an accident…”

At the hospital, my father was terribly pale. His arm and legs were bandaged. A driver had run a red light and hit him.

We were thankful he was alive. He tried to hide his tears.

“If only this hadn’t happened… Now you’ll have to look after me as well.”

After some time, he came home. Our stove was unlit, and the rooms were cold. A few neighbors had called the municipality to ask for coal for us, but they were told we should have registered beforehand.

And so Eid al-Fitr passed, without sweets or gifts.

The weather gradually grew warmer. The bandages came off my father’s arm, but his leg had still not healed.

Then Eid al-Adha arrived.

I felt hopeful. “There are twelve flats in our building. Many of our neighbors will offer an animal for sacrifice. Surely someone will bring us a share. Mom will make one of the meat dishes I love.”

I watched from the window. In the empty lot opposite, the animals were slaughtered and the meat divided. Full basins were carried into the flats. Whenever a door opened, the smell of cooking meat reached our home.

But no one knocked on our door. Not a single portion came our way.

That day, my grandmother called from the village to wish us a happy Eid. She heard something in my voice.

“What is it, sweetheart? Has your father taken a turn for the worse?”

“No,” I said quietly. “The neighbors didn’t give us any meat.”

We used to spend summers with Grandma. She had one cow and five or six hens. The cow gave plenty of milk. Grandma always sang while she milked her.

Early on the third morning of Eid, someone knocked.

It was Grandma.

We ran to welcome her. With tears in her eyes, she hugged us all.

“My little lambs… I’ve brought you lots of meat!”

We filled the fridge. My sister immediately began cutting some up. Soon, the smell rising from the stove was the very smell I had been longing for over the past two days.

I could not wait for the table to be laid. Standing beside the stove, I picked up a piece with a fork. I was blowing on it, lifting it towards my mouth, when I heard my father say:

“Oh, Mom… Why did you have the cow slaughtered?”

My hand froze.

I felt sick and dizzy. I put down the fork and ran outside.

The cow that helped support Grandma. The cow she milked every day while singing… Had she given her up because I had wanted meat?

When the meal was ready, they called me in. I did not want to come, but they insisted that I sit with them.

Ashamed, I picked up a piece. I could not swallow it. Tears rolled down my cheeks.

“What’s wrong, sweetheart?”

“My tooth…” I managed to say. “My tooth really hurts…”

Moral: A celebration becomes complete when its joy is shared. We should notice those in need around us without waiting for them to ask. A small portion offered in time may spare someone else a heartbreaking sacrifice.

The Vizier and the Scholar

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a scholar from a distant country came to visit a king. After a lengthy conversation through an interpreter, he asked to meet the local scholars for a scholarly debate.

The king gathered the scholars of his realm, but none dared to take on the visitor. Seeing their hesitation, the vizier finally stepped forward.

“I’ll talk to him!”

The gathering began. The visiting scholar drew a circle on the ground and looked at the vizier. After a moment’s thought, the vizier divided it in half. When the scholar said nothing, he drew another line, making four sections. Then he gestured as though taking three for himself and giving the fourth to the scholar. The visitor nodded approvingly.

Next, the scholar held out his hand, palm upwards, and waited for a response. The vizier turned his own hand palm down, with his fingers pointing towards the ground. The scholar seemed pleased with this answer too.

For his third question, the scholar pointed to himself and mimed eating. The vizier immediately took an egg from his pocket and flapped his arms like a bird. Smiling, the scholar kissed the vizier’s hand, and the vizier left the gathering.

Through the interpreter, the king asked the visitor:

“What were you saying to each other with all those gestures? We understood none of it.”

The scholar eagerly explained:

“By drawing the circle, I indicated that the Earth is round and asked what your vizier thought of this. He drew the equator at once! Then he divided the circle into quarters to show that roughly three-quarters of the Earth’s surface is water and the rest is land.”

He paused, then continued:

“Next, I asked how plants sustain themselves. He explained through gestures that they depend on rain and sunlight. Finally, I asked about human beings and animals. By showing me an egg and flapping his arms, he reminded me to include birds as well. A most learned man!”

After the visitor had gone, the king summoned the vizier.

“Now tell me your side. What were you discussing with the scholar?”

The vizier waved a dismissive hand.

“Some scholar! I thought he would be a man of profound ideas. It turns out all he thinks about is food! First, he drew a circle to say, ‘Wouldn’t a pan of eggs be nice right now?’ So I divided it in half to say, ‘You can’t have it all. There’s me to think of too!’”

“And then?” asked the king.

“He said nothing, so I thought he wasn’t happy with half. I divided the eggs into four portions and took three for myself. That would teach him to be greedy! When he saw his share disappearing, he quickly agreed.”

The vizier continued:

“Then he held his palm upwards to ask, ‘How about a pot of pilaf?’ I turned my hand down to say, ‘I’ll have some if there’s meat and sauce on top.’ Finally, he pointed to his stomach: ‘I’ve come a long way, and I’m hungry.’ So I took out my egg to tell him, ‘Believe me, I’m even hungrier than you, and this is the only egg I have. Don’t ask for it—you’re not getting it!’”

Moral: People can interpret the same words and gestures in very different ways. Agreement does not always mean understanding. Before drawing conclusions, make sure you know what the other person means.

The Price of a Miracle

Audio
Image
Text

Lisa was eight when she overheard her parents talking about her little brother, Oliver. All she understood was that he was seriously ill and that her parents could not afford his treatment.

Only a very expensive operation could save him, and there was no one the family could borrow from. Lisa heard her father whisper helplessly to her tearful mother:

“Only a miracle can save him now.”

She went to her room and took out the glass jar she kept hidden in the cupboard. She tipped her savings onto the floor and counted the coins several times. Then she put them back, closed the lid, and slipped out through the back door to the pharmacy.

Inside, she waited for the pharmacist to notice her. But he was so absorbed in conversation that he did not see the little girl. Lisa shuffled her feet and cleared her throat. Finally, she took out a few coins and placed them on the glass counter. Their clatter made him turn.

“What do you need, little girl?” he asked impatiently. “I’m talking to my brother. I haven’t seen him for a long time.”

“I want to talk to you about my brother too,” Lisa said firmly. “He’s ill. I need a miracle to make him better.”

“A miracle?”

“His name is Oliver. There’s something bad growing in his brain. Dad says only a miracle can save him now. How much does a miracle cost?”

The pharmacist’s voice softened.

“We don’t sell miracles here, sweetheart. I’m sorry. I can’t help you.”

“But I have money. If it isn’t enough, I’ll find some more. Just tell me the price.”

The neatly dressed man who had been listening—the pharmacist’s brother—bent down and asked:

“What kind of miracle does your brother need?”

“I don’t know,” Lisa replied. “I just know he’s very ill. Mom and Dad say he needs an operation, but they haven’t got the money. That’s why I brought mine.”

“How much do you have?”

“One dollar and eleven cents,” she said quietly. “That’s all of it. But I can find a little more if I have to.”

The man smiled.

“One dollar and eleven cents… What a lovely coincidence! That’s exactly the price of the miracle your brother needs.”

He took her money, held her hand, and said:

“Take me to your home. I’d like to see your brother and speak to your parents. Let’s see what we can do about that miracle.”

The man was Dr. Carlton Armstrong, a neurosurgeon.

The family was not charged for the operation. Before long, Oliver had recovered and returned home. His parents were overjoyed.

One day, his mother whispered to his father:

“That really was a miracle. I wonder how much the operation would have cost us.”

Lisa smiled. She knew exactly what a miracle cost: one dollar and eleven cents, and an unshakable belief that her brother could be saved.

Moral: Hope moves us to seek help; compassion moves us to offer it. Sometimes, when a child’s innocent faith meets another person’s skill and kindness, a way forward emerges where none seemed possible.

Why wait?

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a lecturer at an American university begins the class with a question:

“Imagine that the world will end tomorrow. By this time tomorrow, everything will be over, and there is no way to escape it. How would you spend the time you have left?”

The students answer one by one:

“I would pray and ask God to forgive my sins.”

“I would say goodbye to everyone I love.”

“I would spend the remaining time with my family.”

“I would visit my parents.”

“I would enjoy my favorite foods one last time.”

“I would finally get all the sleep I wanted.”

“I would walk in the woods and enjoy the beauty of nature.”

“I would watch the sunrise and the sunset.”

“I would look at the stars at night.”

“I would cook my favorite meal and gather everyone I love around the table.”

“I would go somewhere I had always dreamed of visiting and spend my final moments there.”

“I would find the people I had hurt, apologize and ask for their forgiveness.”

The lecturer writes every answer on the board, then asks with a smile:

“So why wait until the world is about to end to do these things?”

Moral: Do not leave what matters most until your final day. Express your love, apologize, make time for those you cherish and enjoy life’s simple pleasures today.
