A Compliment

Text

Maria lived with her mother in a small apartment in New York and worked as a secretary for a large company. Her life was quiet, ordinary, and rather dull. At work, hardly anyone noticed her.

One morning, on her way to the office, Maria saw a newly opened hat shop and went inside out of curiosity. A woman and her young daughter were there, along with another customer looking for a hat.

Maria tried on several hats. One appealed to her more than all the others. The moment she put it on, the little girl exclaimed with delight:

“Mom, look how beautiful that woman looks in the hat!”

The girl’s mother turned to Maria and said:

“That hat really suits you.”

The other customer joined them:

“It really does. You look wonderful in it!”

Maria stepped in front of the mirror. She had never been so pleased with her reflection. Smiling, she went to the counter and paid for the hat.

When Maria left the shop, an entirely new world seemed to open before her. The flowers looked brighter than ever, and the fresh air smelled especially sweet. Even the sounds of traffic and passing voices seemed to form a harmonious melody. Maria felt beautiful and happy.

At the office entrance, the security guard opened the door and wished her good morning. In the elevator, someone asked which floor she needed and pressed the button for her. Throughout the day, her colleagues admired her and told her how lovely she looked, as though they were seeing her for the first time. Her manager even invited her to lunch to ask for her opinion on a new project.

When the magical workday ended, Maria was still smiling. She thought about how suddenly her life had changed and was certain that she owed it all to her new hat.

Maria took a taxi home. Her mother opened the door, stared at her in amazement, and said:

“Maria, you look wonderful! Your eyes are shining just as they did when you were a child!”

“Yes, Mom, it is all because of my new hat. I have had an amazing day!”

“What hat?” her mother asked, puzzled.

Alarmed, Maria touched her head. The hat was gone. She wondered whether she had left it in the taxi, at lunch, or in the office. Then she replayed the scene in the shop: putting on the hat, hearing the compliments, walking to the counter, taking out her purse, and setting the hat down beside the register…

Suddenly she understood. She had forgotten the hat in the shop and walked out without it, her eyes still shining with joy.

The hat had not made Maria happy. It was the confidence and delight awakened by other people’s kind words. The world had not changed; Maria’s way of seeing it had.

Moral: A single kind word can change how a person sees themselves—and sometimes how they see the entire world. Happiness is not found in outward possessions, but within us and in the sincere attention we offer one another.

A Glass of Milk

Audio
Image
Text

A poor boy who sold small goods from door to door to pay his tuition discovered one day that he had no money left and was desperately hungry. He decided to knock on the next door and ask for something to eat.

A young woman answered. Embarrassed, the boy could not bring himself to ask for food and requested only a glass of water. Seeing that he was hungry, the woman brought him a large glass of milk instead.

After drinking it slowly, the boy asked:

“How much do I owe you?”

“Nothing,” she replied. “My mother taught us to be kind without expecting anything in return.”

The boy could say only:

“Thank you from the bottom of my heart.”

As he walked away, he felt stronger not only physically, but also in spirit. His faith in God and in other people had been renewed.

Years passed. One day, the same woman became seriously ill. Unable to determine the cause of her illness, the local doctors sent her to specialists in a large city.

Dr. Howard Kelly was called in for a consultation. When he heard which town the patient came from, he immediately went to her room. The moment he saw her, he recognized the woman who had given him a glass of milk years before and rekindled hope in his heart.

Dr. Kelly personally took charge of her treatment and resolved to do everything he could to save her. After a long struggle, the illness was overcome and the woman recovered.

When the time came for her discharge, Dr. Kelly requested the final bill. He wrote a few words along its edge and had it delivered to the patient’s room.

The woman opened it anxiously. She feared that she would never be able to pay the amount and would spend the rest of her life repaying the debt. Then she noticed a sentence written in the margin:

“Paid in full with one glass of milk.”

Signed: Dr. Howard Kelly.

Moral: A selfless act of kindness is never lost. It is entered into an unseen account and may return to us one day—often when we least expect it.

Text

A boy entered a nut shop with his mother and kept insisting, “I want some roasted chickpeas.” The shopkeeper saw that the mother did not intend to buy any and felt sorry for the child:

“Come here, son. Take a handful for yourself.”

“I’d rather you gave them to me,” the boy replied.

The shopkeeper handed him a generous handful and asked:

“Because your hand is bigger than mine.”

“What a clever child you are!” the shopkeeper said with a laugh.

A Pair of Boots

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a schoolteacher asked her pupils to write a story on any subject and promised a pair of boots to the author of the best one. Every child completed a story and handed it in.

The teacher read each piece and liked them all. Unable to choose the best, she decided to draw the winner by lot.

Each pupil wrote a name on a slip of paper, folded it, and placed it in a box on the teacher’s desk. After mixing the slips, she drew one and announced:

“Amina!”

The classroom erupted in applause. Amina pressed the boots to her chest and could not hold back tears of joy.

That evening, the teacher returned home and tearfully told her husband what had happened. He was puzzled:

“You found a wonderful solution. Why are you crying?”

The teacher replied:

“Because every slip said Amina. Not one child wrote their own name. They all wanted the girl from the poorest family to have those boots.”

Moral: The finest victory is to value another person’s joy above one’s own. True compassion is revealed in the willingness to surrender an opportunity for someone else.

Image
Text

As they did every day, Javid Bey and his team met to discuss their project. Each person briefly described the previous day’s progress and any difficulties they had encountered.

It was Kamal’s turn. Like the others, he reported on the work he had done, but — as if hoping for help from his colleagues — he spoke in more detail about the problem he faced. As soon as he finished, Nadir accused him in front of everyone: “You are to blame; because of you our work has stopped and we can do nothing.” The room froze. Kamal was deeply embarrassed and did not know what to do.

Before the exchange could escalate, Javid Bey, the project manager, stepped in. “Let’s stop here for today and return to this later,” he said. The team dispersed.

Javid Bey decided to speak to Nadir privately. He invited him to the company café for tea. After a little small talk, he said calmly, “My son, the way you treated Kamal today was wrong. You accused him without understanding the situation, and you did it in front of everyone. Put yourself in his place. He came to us for help and received blame instead. I’m sure he hoped we would think the problem through together and find a solution.”

Nadir lowered his head in shame. Javid Bey’s counsel had worked; it was a good lesson for Nadir — he understood he had done wrong.

Later, Nadir would recall the conversation with gratitude whenever the subject arose. No one else at the company ever learned what Javid Bey had said to him.

Moral: Conflict asks who is right; discussion asks what is right. If you want the truth, discuss — do not quarrel.

Everyone Is Equal Here

Audio
Image
Text

A young, newly appointed municipal leader decided to explore his district on foot. After walking around alone, he stopped for tea at a teahouse on a side street.

“Uncle, may I polish your shoes?” asked a boy of twelve or thirteen.

His shoes were already polished, but he did not want to disappoint the boy.

“All right. If you do a good job, I’ll pay you double.”

“I do the same good job for everyone,” the boy replied.

The official said:

“What do you mean?”

The child answered:

“Our teacher told us to treat everyone equally, whatever we do. I’m earning money to buy medicine for my mother. If I treat you differently, that medicine won’t make her better!”

Deeply moved, the official gave the boy the largest banknote in his pocket and his business card. He promised to help the fatherless child, who was attending school while caring for his sick mother.

He also visited the teacher to thank him for teaching the boy such honesty.

To keep the lesson before him, he had the words “Everyone is equal here” written on the back of his desk nameplate.

Though it was hard, he always tried to put it into practice.

Moral: Treat a person the same because they are human, not because of their rank. Society is changed by those who choose what is right in small moments.

Gossip

Audio
Image
Text

A rude, quarrelsome man was brought before the village elder for spreading gossip.

“It was only a joke!” he protested. “People misunderstood me. I did nothing wrong.”

But the victim of the gossip stood firm:

“You’ve ruined my good name,” said the person he had maligned. “I want justice.”

Under pressure, the man backed down.

“I take it all back. Please forgive me.”

The elder listened, thinking that the man still did not understand the harm he had done and might repeat it. Then he said:

“Take a feather pillow to the market square, cut it open, and scatter the feathers. Then gather every one and bring them to me. Only then can you be forgiven.”

Reluctantly, the man obeyed. “The old man has lost his mind,” he thought. But the moment he cut the pillow, the wind carried the feathers in every direction. He chased them back and forth, yet managed to recover only a handful.

He lowered his head and returned to the elder:

“I couldn’t gather the rest,” he admitted.

Then he sighed deeply:

“Just as I can’t take back the words I spread.”

Moral: A word that is spread is like a feather thrown to the wind - it cannot be collected again. Therefore, stay away from gossip.

Refusing an Insult

Audio
Image
Text

(An instructive story from the life of the samurai)

A wise samurai taught his students both wisdom and martial arts. One day, a warrior known for cruelty and rudeness interrupted a lesson. He usually defeated opponents by insulting them until they grew angry and made mistakes. He tried the same tactic on the samurai, who ignored him and continued teaching.

After several unsuccessful attempts to provoke him, the warrior left in a rage.

The students, who were watching this scene carefully, asked their teacher:

“Master, why did you let him insult you? Why didn’t you fight?”

The samurai replied:

“If someone offers you a gift and you refuse it, who owns it?”

“The person who offered it,” the students answered.

“The same is true of envy, hatred, and insults. If you refuse to accept them, they remain with the person who brought them.”

Moral: You need not take another person’s malice into your heart. Meet an insult with composure rather than more hostility.

Humanity

Audio
Image
Text

Kamal had studied hard for years. At last, the day of the exam that would shape his future arrived. His father, Kazim, was demanding and followed his progress closely. He was certain his son would succeed.

Kamal found his exam room. Nervous at first, he relaxed when he read the questions. He answered them easily and felt sure he had done well. With half an hour left, he glanced toward the window.

Something outside caught his attention. Without hesitation, he jumped up and ran for the door.

“Come back!” the examiner called. “If you leave, your exam will be invalid. You’ll lose a whole year!” But Kamal kept running and left through the back door.

When Kazim came home a few hours later, he scolded his son and slapped him. He demanded to know why Kamal had abandoned the exam. Kamal stayed silent, certain that an explanation would only make his father angrier.

For the rest of the year, Kazim repeatedly reproached him. Hurt but quiet, Kamal kept his head down and continued studying.

The next year, he passed and entered medical school. He became an excellent doctor. Although he had lived in the city for years, he volunteered to work at a remote village hospital. A year later, his family came to visit. By then, the quarrel over the exam seemed long forgotten.

One snowy evening, the telephone rang. Someone in a nearby village needed a doctor. Kamal took his bag, and Kazim insisted on accompanying him along the mountain roads. After an hour through the blizzard, they reached the house. An older man opened the door and led them to his sick wife.

Kamal examined her and arranged treatment. She thanked him in a trembling voice. “You came all this way in the cold, my son. What would the world be without people like you? Being truly human isn’t easy. Let me tell you something.

“Years ago, I was in the city when I was suddenly overcome by severe pain. The streets were empty—there was an exam that day. A young man rushed over and helped me to the hospital. It took half an hour in the heat. I was pregnant and terrified for my child. The doctor said that ten minutes later might have been too late for me. My son was born that day.

“The young man told me his name was Kamal. ‘I saw you through the exam-room window,’ he said. ‘You were alone, so I came to help. I’ve lost a year, but I don’t regret it.’

“I named my son Kamal and raised him on that story. Now he is a teacher, and he tries above all to teach his students humanity. I wish I could find that young man and tell him what I owe him. Anyone can become a doctor, a teacher, or an engineer. The hardest thing is to be a good human being. He did not leave me alone in the road.” Dr. Kamal answered softly, “You owe me nothing, Mother. Be at peace.”

Kazim tried to hide his tears. But once they left, they streamed down his face. “Kamal, my son,” he began, and could say no more.

Moral: A profession tells us what someone does; kindness shows who they are. One act of compassion can inspire generations.

Compassion

Audio
Image
Text

It was a cold winter night, and I was walking home from work. Ahead of me, a man was swaying and clinging weakly to a lamppost. He looked drunk. As I approached, I saw that he was in his thirties, clean and neatly dressed. Some passersby made loud remarks about the dangers of alcohol; others laughed at him.

“Are you all right? Do you need help?” I asked.

His answer came out as a groan.

“I’m ill.”

I put an arm around his waist to steady him and helped him to a taxi stand. The snow, which had stopped earlier that evening, was falling again. The only movement on the empty streets came from stray dogs chasing one another across the freezing roads.

I was beginning to give up hope when a taxi pulled up. I explained what had happened and told the driver we needed to hurry.

We settled the man in the back seat and drove to the hospital. The doctors treated him immediately and gave him medication. The driver and I waited at his bedside.

The doctor on duty told us that, at the very least, we had saved him from freezing. Still unable to speak, the man looked at us and smiled. When he recovered enough to leave, we drove him home.

His wife knew that he was prone to diabetic comas. Terrified, she had gone out to look for him with their son. When they saw us, they rushed over. The family embraced, their eyes shining with joy.

After hours of worry, our tiredness vanished. The family could not find words to thank us, and their happiness became our own.

When we said goodbye, I asked the driver what I owed him.

He shook his head:

“You owe me nothing, my friend. I’m the one who owes you,” he said. “You let me share in something good. I had almost forgotten how it felt to cry—it must have been twenty years. Thank you for reminding me.”

I hugged the driver and set off home on foot, no longer feeling the night’s cold.

Who knew? Perhaps I would meet someone else who needed help along the way.

Moral: Compassion gives humanity its meaning. It means more than sharing someone’s tears; it means looking for a way to ease their suffering.

Permission or Apology

Audio
Image
Text

A saying attributed to Rear Admiral Grace Hopper suggests that it is easier to ask forgiveness than to get permission.

The idea is to take the initiative, then apologize if necessary, rather than allow endless requests for approval to prevent useful action.

Sometimes waiting for permission is a missed opportunity. Do what you know is right, and apologize later if necessary.

Imagine that a company has a clear problem that is slowing down the work process. For example, every week the same information is manually transferred to several different spreadsheets, which takes hours. An employee realizes that this can be solved with simple automation.

An approval request might pass from one manager to another for weeks. Instead, the employee recognizes that a limited test poses little risk, sets up a separate system that leaves the original data intact, and demonstrates the result. If it works, everyone benefits. If the lack of prior approval causes concern, the employee can explain the aim and acknowledge any rule they have broken.

Moral: When an action is low risk, reversible, and clearly useful, excessive approval processes can stifle initiative. Responsible action sometimes achieves more than endless waiting.

Text

I was laid off last winter. To make ends meet, I had to work as a newspaper carrier temporarily. Since the mailbox of one of the houses I delivered newspapers to was sealed, I had to knock on the door. An old man opened the door.

“Why have you sealed your mailbox?” I asked.

“I did it deliberately.”

Then he smiled strangely and continued:

“Please knock or ring each day and give me the paper in person.”

“Of course, but wouldn’t the mailbox be easier for both of us?”

“It’s no trouble. I’m always here.

“My wife has died, my son lives abroad, and I’m alone. Who knows how much time I have left?”

At that moment, I saw the old man’s misty, moist eyes.

“Do you read the paper?” I asked.

“Never. What I subscribed to was the sound of the doorbell.”

Moral: For some people, one ring of the doorbell, one greeting, is worth the whole world. Let's not forget to knock on the door of the lonely.

Welcome Home

Audio
Image
Text

When my father, Hafiz Ali, went to Çanakkale, my mother was seven months pregnant with me. I never met him. We did not even have a photograph.

Those were very difficult days. Mobilization, occupation, liberation, poverty, hardship...

Our childhood was spent constantly running around in search of bread, filled with pain.

Whenever my mother wanted to go somewhere, she would always come to me and say:

“Son, I’m going to the market. Tell me at once if your father comes.”

“I’m going to see relatives. Tell me at once if your father comes.”

“I’m going next door. Tell me at once if your father comes.”

She was always watching the road, waiting for him.

I grew up and opened a shop. Whenever she went out, she stopped there to tell me where she was going. “Let me know if your father comes,” she always added.

Years passed, and she grew old. Leaning on her cane, she still came to remind me, “Call me if your father returns.”

One day, she became ill and fell into bed.

On her deathbed, she thanked us for caring for her and asked our forgiveness. Then she turned to me. “If your father comes, tell him your mother always waited for him.” Suddenly, she looked toward the door and smiled. “Welcome home, my bey. Welcome.” Then she died.

From the memoirs of Cevdet Algalp, an old shoemaker living in Balıkesir.

Why Do We Shout?

Audio
Image
Text

One day during class, a teacher asked his students:

“Why do people shout when they argue?”

“Because they lose their temper,” a student replied.

“But the other person is right beside them. Why raise their voices instead of speaking calmly?”

Each of the students gave an answer, but none of them satisfied the teacher. Finally, he said:

“When people quarrel, their hearts seem to move apart. They shout as though trying to make themselves heard across that distance.”

After a moment of silence, the teacher continued:

“When people love each other, they speak softly. As they grow closer, they may need only a whisper. Sometimes a look is enough. So when you argue, do not use words that drive your hearts farther apart.

“One day, the distance may become too great to find your way back.”

Moral: Screaming is the sound of hearts drifting apart. A whisper is enough in love — don't increase the distance between you.

Text

A young man preparing for his wedding asked his father for help. “I can’t manage everything. Would you call the friends on this list and invite them?” His father agreed.

On the wedding day, the young man approached him anxiously. “Did you call everyone?”

“Yes, son.”

“There were fifty names. Only fifteen are here.”

Moral: A true friend is one who is there for you when you are in trouble. It is not the many acquaintances that are valuable, but the few friends who stand the test.
