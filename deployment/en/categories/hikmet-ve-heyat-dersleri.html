A Different Path

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a cow returning to its shed had to cross an untouched forest. Unable to choose a sensible route, the animal wandered uphill and downhill, creating a long, twisting, and difficult trail.

The next day, a dog came to the same place. To reach the other side of the forest, it followed the cow’s trail. Then came a flock of sheep. Seeing a ready-made path, the shepherd led the flock along it.

Before long, people began using the trail as well. They complained about its difficulty as they turned left and right, ducked beneath branches, and twisted their way through the bushes. Yet no one thought to look for a better route.

Through constant use, the trail gradually became a road. Unaware that the other side of the forest could be reached in only half an hour, people and heavily burdened animals spent three hours following the cow’s winding path.

Years passed. The small road first became the town’s main route and eventually its most important street. Although it was the least efficient route imaginable, everyone merely complained about the traffic.

Watching it all, the old and wise forest pitied the people who blindly followed the familiar road without ever asking whether it was the right one.

Moral: The path everyone follows is not always the best one. Sometimes those who step beyond habit and search for a new route reach their destination more quickly and easily.

A Life Lesson

Audio
Image
Text

While teaching his cub about life, a father wolf pointed to some sheep:

“Their meat is delicious.”

Then he pointed to the shepherd:

“But his staff can be very bitter. Be careful.”

Seeing the dog beside the shepherd, the cub exclaimed happily:

“Father, look! That one resembles us.”

The father replied:

“Run the moment you see it, my son. We have suffered most from those who look like us but are not truly one of us.”

Moral: Sometimes the greatest harm comes from those who resemble us while concealing different intentions. Recognizing hypocrisy in time is the first step toward protecting oneself.

A Magnificent Lesson

Audio
Image
Text

As a child, Mehmed the Conqueror, son of the Ottoman Sultan Murad II, would not sit quietly in class and was constantly disruptive. Whenever his teacher, Akşemseddin, corrected him, the boy replied:

“You cannot tell me what to do. I am the sultan’s son!”

The teacher was deeply troubled. He hesitated to complain to the ruler, but one day he gathered his courage, appeared before Murad II, and explained what had been happening.

The sultan thought for a moment and whispered a plan into the teacher’s ear. Akşemseddin protested that it would be impossible, but Murad II insisted:

“It will be done.”

The next day, young Mehmed again misbehaved and answered his teacher with the same threat. At that moment, the sultan opened the door and entered the classroom.

Following the plan, Akşemseddin pretended to be furious. He reprimanded the sultan for entering without permission, even slapped him, and ordered him to leave immediately.

Murad II lowered his head, apologized, and left. Seeing that even his powerful father had to obey the teacher’s rules, the prince was stunned.

A short while later, someone knocked. This time the sultan asked permission to enter and apologized again.

The plan worked. From that day forward, Mehmed no longer disrupted his lessons. For the sake of his son’s upbringing, Murad II had set aside even a ruler’s pride.

Moral: In education, personal example is stronger than any lecture. A responsible parent may even set aside personal pride to help a child find the right path.

Text

A hunter walking through a forest came upon an unusual scene. A woodcutter had been hacking at a fallen tree for a long time with enormous effort. His face was drenched in sweat and his whole body was tense.

The hunter approached and asked:

“Why is the work going so slowly and painfully? Your axe is completely blunt. Why not sharpen it?”

The woodcutter stared at him in surprise:

“What are you talking about? Where would I find time to sharpen my axe? I still have twenty more trees to cut!”

Moral: Hard work is valuable, but unexamined effort can be wasteful. Sometimes a short pause to sharpen the tool makes all the work that follows faster and easier.

At the Bookstore

Audio
Image
Text

After a long search, a medical student found the book he needed at the Bilge bookstore in Bursa. When he saw the price on the back, his smile vanished: he could not afford it.

Hiding his disappointment, he leafed through the pages, found the section assigned by his professor, and—seeing that the owners were busy with customers—read it quickly before leaving.

For the next month, the student returned every other day and studied among the shelves. One week before his final examinations, he came again, but the book was gone. He searched every shelf; it seemed to have vanished.

As the pale-faced young man was about to leave, the owners stopped him:

“Please sit down. We have something to tell you.”

One bookseller reached beneath the counter and produced the book:

“A customer recently asked its price and appeared ready to buy it. I removed it from the shelf and kept it for you. You may continue reading it here.”

After completing his studies and becoming a doctor, the young man never forgot the two booksellers. He never charged patients who could not afford treatment.

Moral: A small act of kindness can leave a lifelong mark on the heart. Those who receive kindness often carry it forward to others.

At the Doctor’s Appointment

Audio
Image
Text

I was waiting for my turn at a doctor’s office. Having missed breakfast at home, I bought a sandwich and apple juice from the café and began to eat.

A boy sitting near the window was watching me closely. His expression seemed to say, “I am hungry too.” Feeling sorry for him, I returned to the café and bought him a sandwich and juice.

The boy ate eagerly, while I felt pleased with what I believed was a good deed.

At that moment, his mother emerged from the registration room. Seeing the scene, she hurried over in alarm:

“What have you done? The doctor told me to bring him fasting. I took time off work, and we traveled a long way. Now we will have to begin everything again tomorrow.”

The woman could not hold back her tears. I stood frozen: while believing I was helping, I had undone an entire family’s effort.

Moral: Judgment based only on emotion and assumption can lead us to the wrong decision. Asking about the situation before helping is itself part of compassion.

Blind Well

Audio
Image
Text

One day, a villager's donkey falls into a blind well (a well whose water has dried up). Hours pass; finally, the owner and his neighbors, hearing the donkey's painful cries, rush to the rescue. He says, he says, and finally they come to the conclusion that it will be impossible to get it out of the well, and they see the only solution in filling the well with earth. Each takes a shovel in his hand and begins to carry earth from the surrounding area and pour it into the well.

The poor animal shakes off the earth that has fallen on it and throws it under its feet, and thanks to this earth it gradually rises, finally managing to climb to the mouth of the well. The people are very surprised when they see the donkey come out of the well safely.

Moral: Shake off the earth that has fallen on it and throw it under its feet, and rise. The way out of helplessness is not in whining, but in shaking and struggling.

Diderot effect

Audio
Image
Text

One of the 18th century French writers, the enlightener Denis Diderot, falls into a huge debt swamp. His miserable situation reaches the ears of the Russian Empress Catherine.

The Empress makes Diderot a kind offer to get out of this swamp: she buys his library for money and gives it back to him. Moreover, she pays Diderot 25 years of salary in advance for working in that library. Of course, paying this advance at such an unexpected moment meant making a fortune for Diderot. Now Diderot was free from all debts and relieved.

One day, a friend gives him a red velvet robe (luxurious outerwear). It all starts from there...

Denis puts on the robe. While working enthusiastically at his desk, he suddenly thinks that this beautiful velvet robe and the table do not match.

Intoxicated by the cash in his safe, he immediately goes to change the table and buys a magnificent one. The dress and the table now match each other...

But then he sees that the old carpet on the floor does not suit either the dress or the table. He runs out and buys a carpet that suits his money and himself.

Still, there was a feeling of unease inside him. The armchairs, cupboards, chairs, pictures on the wall, and the decorations in the room no longer match each other, they seem old...

Everything is starting to sink in...

Time comes, time goes... Diderot changed everything in the house, from the needle to the thread.

It did not take Diderot long to realize the situation. He realized that the reason for his return to the starting point was his own inexhaustible greed.

As a result, the author wrote his famous work on this subject, "I Regret Parting with My Old Dress." Then he wrote a short sentence that will go down in history:

"While I was the master of my old dress, I became the slave of the new one."

Moral: After a person acquires a new thing, he feels the need to change the rest - this is called the "Diderot effect." There is no end to greed; it is better to be the master of the old than the slave of the new.

From the memories of a woman from Istanbul

Audio
Image
Text

It was 1919. Istanbul was completely under British occupation. I had just finished high school.

I was a beautiful girl, and ambassadors came to our house. One day, a young man who introduced himself as a lawyer came to our house with his ambassadors. I saw this handsome, tall boy and liked him. We got engaged. I loved my fiancé... I wanted to build a happy home, so I wove small towels by lamplight until morning and prepared my dowry.

One day, a rumor spread in the neighborhood.

They said, “Ayesha's fiancé was not a lawyer at all, he was crying all day long by carrying a coffin in front of the mosque.”

I was devastated. My father and I went to the mosque, watched from a distance, and saw that he was really carrying a coffin.

I was shocked. We returned the engagement, and we broke up.

Five years passed. I was married, I had a child. Our country was already free.

One day I met him in the city. My son was with me.

He stopped when he saw me. He said hesitantly, “If you have time, I would like to offer you a cup of tea.” I agreed. We went into an office.

It was a law firm; his name was written on the sign on the door. His assistants were working inside.

“Are you really a lawyer?” I asked.

“Yes,” he said.

“But if you are a lawyer, why were you carrying a coffin in front of the mosque?”

He bowed his head and said, “Forgive me.”

“Istanbul was under occupation. We were surrounded by British soldiers everywhere, they were checking everything and everyone very carefully. And we were sending coffins full of weapons to Anatolia, to help the National Forces, under the guise of funerals. It was a vital task for the country. I couldn’t tell you that…”

Moral: Don’t judge by appearance; behind every behavior there may be a truth that you don’t know. Our freedom is owed to the sacrifice of the selfless.

Hayat’s Life Story

Audio
Image
Text

When my mother left our home, my father, and us, I was six and my brother was eight. Even at that age I understood why she left my father, but I could not understand why she left us. Would a mother abandon her children?

My father used to beat my mother. He beat me and my brother too. I came to believe that all men beat their wives and children…

Only after my mother left did I learn that a home without a mother is nothing but four walls. Just as no one said in the mornings, “Wash your hands and face and come — breakfast is ready,” I learned that no one asked “Are you hungry?” even if you went days without food. Of all I learned, this burned me most: when there is no mother’s scent, children grow up in an instant, whatever their age. I grew up at six.

About ten days after my mother left, the police came to our house. They said she had taken her own life. In her hand she had been clutching an envelope. On it was written: “To be given to my daughter and my son.”

I could not read then — how was I supposed to read it? My brother read it. I cried as I listened. My brother cried too.

When my mother left us, she had gone to her father’s house. Her father flew into a rage. Apparently a woman was supposed to stay in her own home; even if her mouth were full of blood, she was supposed to say, “I drank red sherbet.” My mother begged my grandfather: “Let me stay with you for a month; then I will find a way, take my children, and begin a new life.”

Oh, no, no, no! A woman living alone? She would cheapen my grandfather’s honour for a penny; he would not be able to show his face; better if his daughter were dead. That evening my mother, without thinking, put the clothesline around her neck. My grandmother told me this years later on her deathbed, when I was nineteen.

When my father heard of my mother’s death, he was not upset at all. He said he would put us in an orphanage. From then on we would live there; that would be our home. Brother and sister might be parted, forget each other, and years later fail to know each other — so we were both to keep Mother’s letter.

But before I could ask how the two of us would keep one letter, my brother cut it in half with scissors. He gave me the beginnings of the sentences. Since I had the openings, once I learned to read I would have to guess the rest. My brother had already memorized the letter anyway.

When my aunt heard we were to be sent to an orphanage, she came. She told my father: “A girl is not given to an orphanage. I will take Hayat and raise her myself.” Living with my aunt, I understood why: a girl meant an unpaid servant ordered about the house. Not once did my aunt call me by my name. My name became “Come-here”: “Come-here, fetch water… Come-here, wash these dishes…”

Once a month my brother visited me at my aunt’s. He said he was fine in the orphanage. I said I was fine too — I did not want to upset him. Did he also say “I’m fine” so I would not worry? I never dared ask.

I had started school. I was the first in the class to learn to read. How could I not — I was going to read my mother’s letter. The part I still had began: “Hayat, my beautiful daughter, I…” Every night in bed I tried to finish that sentence:

“Hayat, my beautiful daughter, I love you so much.”

“Hayat, my beautiful daughter, I miss you terribly.”

“Hayat, my beautiful daughter, I am waiting for you.”

According to the words I added, I built dreams. In my dreams I was very happy. Does anyone ever dream of bitterness?

I finished middle school. My aunt said I would not go to school anymore. But I wanted to study — to stand on my own feet and live under one roof with my brother.

That summer the wife of Uncle Hasan, the neighbourhood grocer with three children, died of cancer. I was heartbroken. What would the three children do — how would they find a mother’s scent? Why did mothers die? Would those three forget their names as I had, and become “Come-here” too?

While I was grieving for Uncle Hasan’s children, it came out that I was betrothed to him. It turned out I had been sold for eight bracelets and three thousand liras. Because I was too young for a civil marriage, forty days later our religious marriage was performed.

I was fifteen. I was Uncle Hasan’s wife. I had three children — two, five, and six. And my aunt insisted we must have a child of our own… I was supposed to have a child so my place would be secure and Uncle Hasan would not look at other women.

The first slap from Uncle Hasan came because I called him “Uncle Hasan.” Does a woman call her husband “uncle”? When I lost my mother at six, I learned to keep silent. So I did not ask, “Does a man call a fifteen-year-old girl his wife?” — I stayed quiet.

When Uncle Hasan hit me, all three children clung to their father’s legs: “Don’t hit Sister Hayat — she plays with us, she tells us stories,” they begged. I was those children’s older sister. What I called stories were my dreams.

When Uncle Hasan left the house, I stood before the mirror — I would learn to say “Hasan.” Every time I said “Hasan,” I saw in the glass his thinning hair, the glasses slipped down his nose, his swollen belly. Every “Hasan” made me ashamed, as if I had sinned, and I could not call him that. While I practised, Uncle Hasan’s six-year-old son came: “Sister Hayat, Mother used to call Father ‘Bey’ — you say ‘Bey’ too,” he encouraged me.

“Bey” — of course, “Bey” would be better. I bent and kissed dark-eyed Sami, who listened to my dreams as if they were fairy tales. I called five-year-old Elif and two-year-old Zahra and began to tell them a story. That day I began: “Once upon a time there was a six-year-old girl named Nagil. Nagil had lost her mother. She searched everywhere; when she could not find her, she went to the land of dreams. There Nagil was so happy that she never returned to the real world.”

In my tale Nagil was always happy, always smiling. Every day I told the children that story. We were very happy.

Uncle Hasan was good too. I now called him “Bey.” From time to time he grew angry, but I understood why: instead of a woman he had bought, for eight bracelets and three thousand liras, a storytelling sister.

My brother came to visit. Each time he wanted to give me his half of Mother’s letter. I would not take it. I was afraid that if I read the other half, I would fall from the land of dreams into a cruel real world and be unhappy. All my dreams were built on that other half.

By forty, the children who had listened to my stories had grown. Sami had become a doctor, taken a post in another city, and left our house. When he felt low he would call: “Sister Hayat, tell me a story.”

Elif had become a teacher and married. I had a granddaughter. They badly wanted to name her Hayat; I would not allow it. Elif said, “Then let her name be Nagil.” So my granddaughter’s name was Nagil.

My youngest, Zahra, became a veterinarian. “Sister Hayat, whenever an animal is restless I tell it a story, and it calms down,” she would say. Zahra married too, and I had a grandson.

When I turned forty-five, Uncle Hasan — my Bey — passed away. I grieved deeply. His last words were “Forgive me — make it lawful.” With all my heart I forgave him. He was a good man.

But my claim —

against those who see no wrong in marrying off fifteen-year-old girls, and those who support them;

against the blind minds who call it dishonour when a daughter they know is beaten every day seeks divorce, and those who support them;

against those who show no mercy to children left helpless and hopeless like wounded birds, and those who support them —

I do not forgive!

Moral: Blind custom and a false idea of “honour” can destroy a woman’s life, a child’s life. Those who look away from early marriage, violence, and neglect are partners in that oppression.

If I don’t do it these days, the world won’t fall apart

Audio
Image
Text

When I asked one of our employees what stage of the implementation of a task entrusted to him was, his answer deeply disappointed me:

“If I don’t do it these days, the world won’t fall apart…”

I thought for a moment: Yes, the world won’t fall apart. But what about the trust between us? Won’t that fall apart too? Wouldn’t this relationship harm our cooperation, mutual respect, and the common work we do?

The issue wasn’t that the task was delayed by a few days. The issue was a person’s attitude towards the commitment they took on. Because responsibility is not only about doing the job, but also about keeping your word, justifying the trust of the other party, and ensuring that people can rely on you.

I think it’s not right to approach any commitment you take on in such an irresponsible way. What was even more regrettable was the way this approach was expressed. To me, such an answer sounds at least rude and shows that the other party's time, effort, and trust are not sufficiently valued.

I must admit that these words broke my heart. At that moment, I realized that our cooperation could not continue as before. Because the foundation of any professional relationship is trust. When trust is shaken, cooperation is also seriously tested.

What disappointed me most was that my colleague realized the wrongness of what he said, neither apologized for it nor fulfilled the task. As a result, my trust in him was shaken, so I was forced to provide a solution to the problem in another way.

This incident once again showed me that the most valuable resource in organizations is time, not money, nor technology. The most valuable resource is the trust that people have in each other. It can take years to earn trust, but sometimes it can be lost in just one sentence.

Moral: Trust is the most valuable asset of organizations. It takes years to earn it, and losing it is like a sentence - those who keep their word protect this wealth.

Text

The only “listener” says:

Liver

Audio
Image
Text

Where do the expressions using the word “liver” come from—“jan-jiyar” meaning a very close, sincere, and kind friend; “jiyarguşam” meaning a very beloved child; “agqıyar” meaning a coward, a coward, a helpless person; “jiyarparam” meaning a very beloved person, etc.—originate?

Ancient people were impressed by the warm and bloody appearance of the liver, so they considered warm blood to be life, and its absence to be death. For this reason, the liver has long been considered the center of life.

For example, the Babylonians believed that the human soul resided in the liver. Since the liver was thought to primarily represent emotions in some cultures, the liver came before the heart in idioms and writings.
